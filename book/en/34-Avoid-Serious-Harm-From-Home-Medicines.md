# 34. Do not let medicines kept at home cause serious harm

Fever reducers, painkillers, cold medicines, stomach medicines, and anti-diarrhea medicines: many of them can be bought at a pharmacy with no prescription. Being able to buy them with no prescription does not mean any way of taking them is fine. This section does not say which medicine to take for each illness. It only picks a few actions that each avoid a serious outcome.

Every item is scored on the mortality scale, including health endpoints such as liver failure, stomach bleeding, and kidney failure in a newborn. Most of the benefit is one fewer serious accident. Studies do not give a figure for how much doing it would cut the risk. The size of the benefit is judged by how heavy the outcome is, and each note says why it was set that way.

What is already written elsewhere is only pointed to here. If a medicine or a cleaning product is swallowed by mistake, see section 13, item 20 (accidental ingestion). For taking a chronic-disease medicine in full as the doctor ordered, see section 16, item 1 (finish the medicine as prescribed). For buying a prescription drug online, the prescription has to be checked first, see section 28, item 6 (get weight-loss drugs with a hospital prescription).

The people these items benefit are you and your family, the top two tiers.
### 1. Check ingredient labels before taking two cold or pain medicines together; acetaminophen should be in only one, and go to the hospital the same day if you take too much, even with no symptoms
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=大 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: Free. A quick look at the ingredient label on each package takes one to two minutes.
- In plain terms: Many cold remedies contain acetaminophen (paracetamol). Taking two products at the same time means getting a double dose, and too much can severely damage your liver. In the US, nearly three in ten acute liver failure patients caused by it died before ever getting a transplant. Package inserts recommend taking at most 2 grams a day, and US warnings also caution against having 3 or more alcoholic drinks daily while using it. If you take too much, go straight to the hospital even if you feel completely fine—the antidote works much better the earlier it is given.
- Benefit: Over 6 years, 22 US hospitals continuously enrolled 662 cases of acute liver failure. Among them, 275 cases (42%) were caused by acetaminophen, with a median ingested dose of 24 grams. Of these 275 patients, 131 (48%) took an overdose unintentionally rather than deliberately. Among those unintentional overdoses, 38% took two or more medications containing acetaminophen at the same time. Of the 275 patients, 27% died before receiving a liver transplant, and 8% received a liver transplant. In 2020, China's National Medical Products Administration (NMPA) mandated that over-the-counter (OTC) package inserts state: overdose causes severe liver damage, and the recommended maximum daily dose should not exceed 2 grams. Inserts must also state that combining with other medicines containing acetaminophen or other antipyretic and analgesic drugs should be avoided as much as possible. US regulations require the first boxed warning on OTC labels to list three scenarios that lead to liver damage: exceeding the maximum daily dose, taking with other acetaminophen-containing drugs, or consuming 3 or more alcoholic drinks every day during use. The same NMPA announcement requires extended-release formulations to state: adults or children who overdose must seek immediate medical care, whether they feel unwell or not. Regardless of formulation, the US National Library of Medicine's MedlinePlus drug information advises seeking immediate medical care if someone takes more than the recommended dose, "even if the person does not have any symptoms." The antidote is N-acetylcysteine. Its package insert specifies administering it as early as possible: efficacy is satisfactory within 12 hours, but drops substantially beyond 24 hours. A US multicenter study from 1976 to 1985 tracked 2,540 overdose patients treated with this antidote. Among patients deemed at risk of hepatotoxicity based on blood drug levels, 6.1% developed liver injury if treatment began within 10 hours, compared to 26.4% if treatment began between 10 and 24 hours. Starting within 8 hours of overdose provided a protective effect regardless of how high blood drug levels were.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources:Larson AM, Polson J, Fontana RJ, et al.; Acute Liver Failure Study Group (2005). Acetaminophen-induced acute liver failure: results of a United States multicenter, prospective study. Hepatology, 42(6):1364-1372. <https://doi.org/10.1002/hep.20948>；国家药监局关于修订对乙酰氨基酚常释及缓释制剂说明书的公告（2020年第15号）及附件1《对乙酰氨基酚常释制剂处方药说明书修订要求》、附件2《对乙酰氨基酚常释制剂非处方药说明书修订要求》、附件3《对乙酰氨基酚缓释制剂说明书修订要求》（湖南省药品监督管理局转载）. <http://mpa.hunan.gov.cn/mpa/xxgk/tzgg/wjtz/202003/t20200311_11809335.html>；21 CFR 201.326(a)(1)(iii)(A) Liver warning. <https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/chapter-I/subchapter-C/part-201/subpart-G/section-201.326>；Smilkstein MJ, Knapp GL, Kulig KW, Rumack BH (1988). Efficacy of oral N-acetylcysteine in the treatment of acetaminophen overdose: analysis of the national multicenter study (1976 to 1985). New England Journal of Medicine, 319(24):1557-1562. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJM198812153192401>；MedlinePlus. Acetaminophen (In case of emergency/overdose). <https://medlineplus.gov/druginfo/meds/a681004.html>
- Notes: Acetaminophen is also known as paracetamol. Compound cold medications with "anfen" (氨酚) in their generic Chinese names—such as paracetamol/pseudoephedrine or paracetamol/caffeine/chlorpheniramine—contain this ingredient. If you are unsure, bring all the boxes to a pharmacist at the drugstore. Graded B because the US data comes from a case registry without an unexposed control group, making it impossible to calculate exactly how many incidents are prevented by checking ingredient labels. The benefit is rated "Large" based on the severity of the outcome: nearly 30% of acute liver failure cases ended in death before a transplant could take place. The figures showing that earlier antidote use improves outcomes come from an observational multicenter study without randomization, compared only against historical cohorts.

### 2. Do not give aspirin, nimesulide, or metamizole to children with a fever, avoid metamizole cold medicines in minors, and measure liquid fever meds with the tool from the box
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=大 口径=死亡率 人群=婴幼儿,孩子 -->
- Cost: Free. Check the generic name and ingredient list when buying fever medicine.
- In plain terms: Aspirin is linked to Reye syndrome, a severe illness that damages the brain and liver; roughly three in ten affected children in US surveillance died. Nimesulide is banned under age 12, and metamizole is banned under age 18. Measure liquid fever medicine with the measuring tool included in the box—household spoons vary wildly in size. Drops and syrups can also have very different concentrations (in one US example, a 3-fold difference), so never give the old milliliter dose if you switch formulations.
- Benefit: Reye syndrome causes encephalopathy and fatty liver degeneration, typically developing after influenza or varicella (chickenpox). In US national surveillance from 1981 to 1997, 1,207 cases were reported in individuals under 18 years of age. Salicylates—the active component in drugs like aspirin—were detected in the blood of 82% of tested children. The overall case fatality rate was 31%. Warnings regarding salicylates began in 1980. That year marked the peak with 555 reported cases; from 1987 onward, there were no more than 36 cases per year. In 2011, China's SFDA banned oral nimesulide formulations in children under 12 years of age. In 2020, the NMPA banned metamizole tablets in children and adolescents under 18 years of age. Metamizole-containing combinations, such as compound artemisinin-metamizole tablets, Zhongganling tablets, and Zhongganling capsules, are likewise banned under age 18. For medication measuring, consider US data. In 2011, the US FDA noted that infant acetaminophen drops contained 80 mg per 0.8 mL, while oral liquid for older children was 160 mg per 5 mL. Based on these two numbers, 1 mL of infant drops contained approximately 3 times as much medication as 1 mL of the oral liquid. The FDA pointed out that having both concentrations on the market easily caused confusion and accidental overdoses, and measuring tools from different products must never be swapped. A Greek study measured 71 teaspoons and 49 tablespoons across 25 households, finding water capacities ranging from 2.5 mL to 7.3 mL. In a US emergency department study, 42.5% of parents who used teaspoons or tablespoons made dosing errors, compared to 27.6% of parents who used milliliters alone (adjusted odds ratio [OR] 2.3, 95% CI 1.2–4.4).
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources:Belay ED, Bresee JS, Holman RC, Khan AS, Shahriari A, Schonberger LB (1999). Reye's syndrome in the United States from 1981 through 1997. New England Journal of Medicine, 340(18):1377-1382. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJM199905063401801>；关于加强尼美舒利口服制剂使用管理的通知（国食药监安〔2011〕209号）. <https://www.nmpa.gov.cn/xxgk/fgwj/gzwj/gzwjyp/20110515145101552.html>；国家药监局关于修订安乃近相关品种说明书的公告（2020年第34号）及附件1至3. <https://www.nmpa.gov.cn/directory/web/nmpa/xxgk/ggtg/ypggtg/ypshmshxdgg/20200317171201660.html>；U.S. Food and Drug Administration. - Notes: Metamizole is not a first-choice drug for adults either. Its package insert specifies that it can cause agranulocytosis, aplastic anemia, and anaphylactic shock; it is intended only for short-term use in acute, severe conditions when no other effective medication is available. Zhongganling sounds like an ordinary cold medicine, but it contains metamizole. Ask a pediatrician or pharmacist which fever reducer to give a child and what dose to calculate based on body weight. If an infant under 3 months has a fever, go straight to the hospital—see Section 20, Item 8 (go straight to the hospital at 38 °C). Grade B was assigned because the Reye syndrome figures come from surveillance reports; the decline in cases occurred after warnings were issued (a temporal association, not a randomized controlled trial). The benefit is rated "substantial" because cases dropped from 555 per year to under 36 after the warnings.

### 3. Ask a doctor before taking painkillers like ibuprofen if you are over 60, have had stomach bleeding, or take blood thinners or steroids
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=大 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: Free. Just ask a doctor or pharmacist once.
- In plain terms: Painkillers like ibuprofen, naproxen, and aspirin can damage the stomach. In high-dose trials, people taking ibuprofen had about 4 times the risk of stomach bleeding or perforation compared to those not taking it. The risk is even higher for older adults, anyone who has had stomach bleeding, people taking blood thinners or steroids, and those who drink 3 or more alcoholic drinks a day.
- Benefit: These drugs are called nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), with common examples being ibuprofen, naproxen, and diclofenac. A meta-analysis pooled 280 randomized trials of these medications against placebo, covering 124,513 people in total. Compared to placebo, the relative risk of upper gastrointestinal perforation, obstruction, or bleeding was: ibuprofen RR 3.97 (95% CI 2.22–7.10, roughly 4-fold), naproxen 4.22 (2.71–6.56), and diclofenac 1.89 (1.16–3.09). All of these drugs roughly doubled the risk of heart failure. US regulations require over-the-counter NSAIDs to carry a stomach bleeding warning. The warning names "aspirin, ibuprofen, naproxen" as examples and lists six higher-risk groups: people aged 60 or older, those with a history of stomach ulcers or bleeding, those taking blood thinners or steroids, those taking other NSAIDs at the same time, those having 3 or more alcoholic drinks daily while using the medicine, and those taking more or for longer than directed.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources:Coxib and traditional NSAID Trialists' (CNT) Collaboration; Bhala N, Emberson J, Merhi A, et al. (2013). Vascular and upper gastrointestinal effects of non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs: meta-analyses of individual participant data from randomised trials. Lancet, 382(9894):769-779. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(13)60900-9>；21 CFR 201.326(a)(2)(iii)(A) Stomach bleeding warning. <https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/chapter-I/subchapter-C/part-201/subpart-G/section-201.326>
- Notes: The study conclusions apply to high-dose use. The paper did not provide separate numbers for the risk of occasional use for one or two days at label doses. Many multi-ingredient cold medicines also contain these drugs; taking them together with painkillers leads to accidental duplication, so check the ingredient list first. If your doctor prescribed long-term low-dose aspirin to prevent blood clots, do not stop taking it on your own, and do not add another painkiller on top of it. For using these drugs with poor kidney function, see item 14 of this chapter (impaired kidney function). For how to use these drugs for menstrual cramps and when to see a gynecologist, see item 25 of chapter 3 (taking painkillers like ibuprofen for menstrual cramps).

### 4. For children under 2 with colds, don’t give them over-the-counter multi-ingredient cold and cough medicines on your own
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=死亡率 人群=婴幼儿 -->
- Cost: There is no cost at all; you even save money by not buying these medicines.
- In plain terms: The National Medical Products Administration requires a warning on 14 kinds of combination cold medicines for children. The warning says it does not recommend that a parent give the medicine to a child under 2 on their own. It should be used with a doctor or a pharmacist guiding it. In trials done on children, cough medicines and anti-allergy medicines worked no better than a placebo (a dummy pill).
- Benefit: In its Announcement No. 57 of 2021, the National Medical Products Administration updated the instructions for 14 such medicines, including paracetamol, pseudoephedrine and dextromethorphan oral solution, pediatric paracetamol, artificial cow-bezoar and chlorpheniramine maleate granules, and pediatric paracetamol and amantadine hydrochloride granules. The new warning reads: “It is not recommended for parents or guardians to give these products to infants under 2 years old on their own; they should only be used under the guidance of a doctor or pharmacist.” An additional note was added: “These products must be used strictly according to the dosage instructions on the package to avoid overdosing.” Another note states: “Concurrent use of other cold medicines containing the same or similar active ingredients should be avoided.” A Cochrane systematic review included 29 randomized controlled trials, 10 of which were conducted on children. For all these medicine types, no better effect than placebo was observed in pediatric trials: cough medicines, antihistamines (the anti-allergy component), antihistamines combined with decongestants (the ingredient that relieves nasal congestion), and cough medicines combined with bronchodilators. Adverse reactions were reported in 21 studies, with formulations containing antihistamines and dextromethorphan showing a higher incidence of side effects.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources:国家药监局关于修订氨酚麻美口服溶液等14个品种药品说明书的公告（2021年第57号）及附件. <https://www.nmpa.gov.cn/directory/web/nmpa/xxgk/ggtg/ypggtg/ypshmshxdgg/20210423105404141.html>；Smith SM, Schroeder K, Fahey T (2014). Over-the-counter (OTC) medications for acute cough in children and adults in community settings. Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, (11):CD001831. <https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD001831.pub5>
- Notes: Many of these 14 medicines have “paracetamol” in their name, as they contain acetaminophen. Taking them together with other fever reducers will lead to redundant intake, as noted in Section 1 of this chapter (on acetaminophen). One trial included in the review found honey to be more effective than placebo for treating coughs, but honey should not be given to children under 1 year old, as detailed in Section 20, Item 6 (on honey). The evidence grade is set to B because there are very few trials for each type of medicine in the review, and the authors could not calculate a combined statistical figure. The benefit level is rated “moderate” as there is no proven benefit of these medicines for children, while the main risks are overdosing and redundant intake of similar active ingredients.

### 5. Pregnancy after 20 weeks: avoid taking ibuprofen and similar painkillers on your own
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=死亡率 人群=怀孕 -->
- Cost: No cost involved.
- In plain terms: Taking a drug like ibuprofen after 20 weeks of pregnancy can harm the kidneys of the fetus and leave less fluid around the baby. Among reports of this kind received in the United States, there were cases in which the newborn died. Low-dose aspirin prescribed by a doctor is not included.
- Benefit: In October 2020, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) issued a safety warning. Use of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs after roughly 20 weeks of pregnancy may cause kidney problems in the fetus, leading to low amniotic fluid levels. This warning applies to both prescription and over-the-counter drugs, including aspirin, ibuprofen, naproxen, diclofenac and celecoxib. After reviewing adverse event reports up to July 2017, the FDA identified 35 cases of low amniotic fluid or neonatal kidney dysfunction, all with serious outcomes; 5 of these newborns died, each with accompanying kidney failure. In most documented cases, amniotic fluid levels returned to normal within 72 hours to 6 days after medication use stopped. The FDA advises pregnant women not to take these drugs after week 20 unless explicitly directed by a doctor. Low-dose aspirin (81 mg) prescribed for specific pregnancy-related conditions is exempt from this restriction. When pain relief or fever reduction is needed, the FDA recommends using alternatives such as acetaminophen; consult a pharmacist or doctor first.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources:U.S. Food and Drug Administration (2020-10-15). FDA recommends avoiding use of NSAIDs in pregnancy at 20 weeks or later because they can result in low amniotic fluid. Drug Safety Communication. <https://www.fda.gov/drugs/drug-safety-and-availability/fda-recommends-avoiding-use-nsaids-pregnancy-20-weeks-or-later-because-they-can-result-low-amniotic>
- Notes: The FDA notes that many over-the-counter products for colds, flu or insomnia also contain these ingredients, so always check the ingredient list. Previously, U.S. over-the-counter drug labels only warned against use in the final 3 months of pregnancy; this new warning sets the cutoff at around 20 weeks. Low-dose aspirin for preventing preeclampsia is discussed in Section 27, point 5 (“Low-dose aspirin”). The grade B rating reflects that these figures come from adverse event reports; we have no data on how many women took these drugs without experiencing any issues. The “moderate” benefit rating reflects the seriousness of these outcomes, even though they occur infrequently.

### 6. Take over-the-counter omeprazole for acid reflux for at most 7 days; go straight to the hospital if you have trouble swallowing, vomit blood, or pass black stools
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: Free. Follow the number of days on the package insert.
- In plain terms: Omeprazole enteric-coated tablets became available over the counter in 2022, but the package insert states that self-treatment should not exceed 7 days, and you should not take another course within 2 months. Do not self-medicate if you have difficulty or pain swallowing, vomit blood, have blood in your stool, or have black stools, as these can signal a serious condition. The medication can also mask symptoms of stomach cancer and delay diagnosis.
- Benefit: China's National Medical Products Administration (NMPA) Announcement No. 68 of 2022 switched omeprazole enteric-coated tablets from prescription to over-the-counter status and released a model package insert. The listed indication is "short-term relief of heartburn and acid reflux symptoms caused by gastric hyperacidity." The precautions specify: do not use for more than 7 days; if symptoms do not improve, consult a physician or pharmacist. Do not take it again within 2 months; seek immediate medical attention if symptoms recur. Do not use if you experience difficulty or pain swallowing, vomit blood, have bloody stools, or have black stools, as these may be signs of a serious condition. When alarm symptoms appear—such as marked weight loss, recurrent vomiting, difficulty swallowing, vomiting blood, or black stools—malignancy must first be ruled out, because treatment can mask symptoms and delay diagnosis. Anyone over 55 who develops new or changing symptoms should consult a doctor. People taking clopidogrel should avoid taking omeprazole at the same time. The reason is explained in the US package insert for clopidogrel (Plavix): omeprazole inhibits the enzyme (CYP2C19) that activates clopidogrel in the body. The label states that "omeprazole was shown to reduce significantly the antiplatelet activity of Plavix when given concomitantly or 12 hours apart." Whether taken together or 12 hours apart, clopidogrel's anti-clotting effect is significantly weakened. Indications listed in this label include acute coronary syndrome, recent myocardial infarction, recent stroke, and peripheral arterial disease.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources:国家药监局关于奥美拉唑肠溶片处方药转换为非处方药的公告（2022年第68号）及附件2《非处方药说明书范本》. <https://www.nmpa.gov.cn/yaopin/ypggtg/20220826171517123.html>；Sanofi-Aventis U.S. (2025). PLAVIX (clopidogrel) tablets, US prescribing information, sections 1, 5 and 7（DailyMed）. <https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=de8b0b67-eb25-4684-83b5-7ad785314227>
- Notes: Clopidogrel is an anti-clotting drug commonly used by people who have had a heart attack or stroke. Omeprazole weakens its effect, so patients taking it must consult a doctor first. Grade B is assigned because these are regulatory instructions from official package inserts; no study has quantified how many adverse events are prevented by following them. The benefit is rated "moderate" because it prevents delayed diagnosis of gastrointestinal bleeding and gastric cancer, even though most reflux and heartburn are not life-threatening. For same-day emergency visits regarding black stools or vomiting blood, see Section 13, Item 49 (tarry stools).

### 7. Do not ask a doctor for antibiotics for an ordinary cold
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: No money, and you save the money for the medicine.
- In plain terms: Antibiotics for a cold do not make you better any faster than not taking them. For an adult who takes them, the risk of side effects is more than twice the risk of not taking them. If a runny nose with pus has lasted under 10 days, antibiotics have no definite benefit either.
- Benefit: A Cochrane systematic review pooled 6 randomized trials and 1147 people. Compared with a placebo, antibiotics for a cold made no clear difference in the share who had not recovered or whose symptoms persisted (RR 0.83, 95% CI 0.60–1.14, and the difference is not significant). The risk of adverse reactions was 1.8 times the placebo group (95% CI 1.01–3.21). For adults it was 2.62 times (1.32–5.18). For children it did not rise (0.91, 0.51–1.63). For acute purulent rhinitis with a pus-filled runny nose lasting under 10 days, antibiotics also had no clear benefit (RR 0.73, 0.47–1.13). Adverse reactions were more common, though (RR 1.46, 1.10–1.94, about 46% higher). The authors' conclusion is that routine antibiotics are not recommended in either situation.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources:Kenealy T, Arroll B (2025). Antibiotics for the common cold and acute purulent rhinitis. Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, 11:CD000247. <https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD000247.pub4>
- Notes: This edition was issued again in 2025, and the abstract states that the literature search runs through 2013. In China antibiotics are prescription drugs. A pharmacy has to sell them against a prescription. Do not take leftover antibiotics at home on your own either. The benefit is rated moderate, as a judgment: what it prevents is adverse reactions, not death. Also do not ask for an intravenous drip for a cold. See section 6, item 35 (asking for an intravenous drip for a cold or a fever).

### 8. Rehydrate with oral rehydration salts first for diarrhea, and never give antidiarrheal drugs to children under 5
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=少 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: A box of oral rehydration salts costs a few dollars; keep one at home.
- In plain terms: When you have diarrhea, the most important step is replacing fluids, not stopping bowel movements. Mix oral rehydration salts with water according to the package instructions. Do not substitute fruit juices or sweet drinks, as too much sugar draws water out of the body and worsens dehydration. Never give gut-slowing antidiarrheal drugs (such as loperamide) to children under 5: severe cases can lead to bowel obstruction, which can be fatal.
- Benefit: The World Health Organization (WHO) diarrhea treatment manual states that "antidiarrheal" and antiemetic drugs offer no real benefit for acute or persistent diarrhea in children. They do not prevent dehydration or improve nutrition. The manual specifically warns: "Some drugs have dangerous and sometimes fatal side-effects. These drugs should never be given to children under 5 years." Drugs that slow gut motility, such as loperamide, can cause severe paralytic ileus (bowel obstruction), can be fatal, and may prolong infections. The manual also notes that drinks packed with sugar, like soft drinks and commercial fruit juices, draw water from the body into the bowel, causing hypernatremic dehydration. The WHO manual recommends a reduced-osmolarity formula with a total osmolarity of 245 mOsm/L. Compared to the older formula, it reduced unscheduled intravenous infusions by 33%. A Cochrane review pooling 8 trials found a similar result (odds ratio, OR 0.59, 95% CI 0.45–0.79).
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources:World Health Organization (2005). The treatment of diarrhoea: a manual for physicians and other senior health workers, 4th revision. <https://iris.who.int/handle/10665/43209>（中文版《腹泻治疗：医生和高年资卫生工作者使用手册》第 2.6 节，同一页面下载）；Hahn S, Kim S, Garner P (2002). Reduced osmolarity oral rehydration solution for treating dehydration caused by acute diarrhoea in children. Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, (1):CD002847. <https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD002847>
- Notes: When buying, check the osmolarity on the package insert and choose a reduced-osmolarity formula. The manual notes that bloody stools in children usually indicate bacterial dysentery; see a doctor for antibiotics rather than relying solely on home rehydration. Rating the benefit as "medium" is an editorial judgment: the available trial figures compare the two rehydration salt formulas against each other, not drinking them versus doing nothing.

### 9. For headaches, do not take simple painkillers for 15 days a month or combination pills for 10 days, or the medication may be causing the pain
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: Free. It takes tracking the days you take pills and resisting the urge to reach for them every time.
- In plain terms: If frequent headaches make you take painkillers every day, the medicine itself can make the pain worse. The International Classification of Headache Disorders calls this medication-overuse headache. Having a headache on 15 or more days a month, combined with taking simple painkillers on 15 or more days or combination painkillers on 10 or more days for over 3 months, fits the diagnosis. Most people improve once they stop overusing the medication.
- Benefit: The International Classification of Headache Disorders, 3rd edition (ICHD-3) by the International Headache Society outlines the diagnostic criteria: an existing headache disorder occurring on 15 or more days per month, accompanied by regular overuse of acute or symptomatic headache medication for more than 3 months. For non-opioid analgesics such as acetaminophen, aspirin, and ibuprofen, the threshold for overuse is 15 or more days per month. For combination analgesics, the threshold is 10 or more days per month. Combination analgesics refer to medications combining two or more active analgesic ingredients, or combining an analgesic with an adjuvant such as caffeine, or combinations like acetaminophen with codeine. Alternating between multiple non-opioid painkillers counts toward the cumulative total days. The classification notes that epidemiological evidence across multiple countries shows that over half of individuals experiencing headaches on 15 or more days per month have this condition. Most patients improve after withdrawing the overused medication, and preventive headache treatments also become more effective.
- Evidence grade: B

### 11. While taking an antibacterial such as a cephalosporin or metronidazole, and for 7 days after stopping, do not drink alcohol, and do not take an alcohol-containing medicine such as Huoxiang Zhengqi oral liquid or cough syrup
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=小 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: No money. No alcohol for a little over a week. Before taking another medicine, glance at whether the ingredients include ethanol.
- In plain terms: Some cephalosporins, and drugs such as metronidazole, make the face flush and bring on sweating and a racing heart if they meet alcohol. A severe case brings trouble breathing and a drop in blood pressure. It is not only liquor and beer. An alcohol-containing medicine such as Huoxiang Zhengqi oral liquid (藿香正气水, a Chinese medicine liquid that contains alcohol) or cough syrup can do it too. Do not touch alcohol from the time you are taking the drug through 7 days after you stop.
- Benefit: This is called a disulfiram-like reaction. A 2023 medication-safety article from the Fujian drug regulator says that drinking before or after some cephalosporins can set off this reaction, and that it "can seriously harm health and can even threaten life." The examples include liqueur chocolates and Huoxiang Zhengqi oral liquid. The reason given is that the enzyme the drug inhibits often takes 4 to 5 days to recover. The alcohol came from alcoholic drinks in 40 cases (48.78%) and from ethanol-containing medicines in 31 cases (37.80%). Medicines taken by mouth included Huoxiang Zhengqi oral liquid, cough syrup, and motherwort fluid extract (益母草流浸膏). Of the 82 cases, 68 were mild, 9 were moderate, and 5 were severe. They eased within 1 to 3 hours. Nobody died. The US leaflet for metronidazole requires no alcohol during treatment and for at least 3 days after stopping, and no products that contain propylene glycol.

### 12. Do not drive, work at heights, or operate machinery while taking cold or allergy medicines containing chlorpheniramine or diphenhydramine
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=小 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: Free. Check the ingredient list when buying medicine. Take public transit or a cab while taking it, or ask a doctor or pharmacist to switch you to a non-drowsy alternative.
- In plain terms: Many multi-symptom cold medicines contain allergy relief ingredients like chlorpheniramine or diphenhydramine that make you drowsy. Drug safety authorities warn not to drive, work at heights, or operate machinery while taking them. In driving simulator tests, people who took diphenhydramine drove even worse than those who were legally drunk. Just because you do not feel sleepy does not mean your driving is safe.
- Benefit: A 2010 adverse drug reaction bulletin from China's National Medical Products Administration (NMPA) noted that Wei C Yinqiao tablets contain chlorpheniramine maleate. Its main side effects include drowsiness, weakness, sleepiness, and dry mouth. The bulletin advised doctors and pharmacies to caution patients: do not operate airplanes, vehicles, or boats, and do not perform work at heights, mechanical operations, or work with precision instruments while taking the medication. A 2020 NMPA public medication safety notice pointed out that multi-symptom cold remedies often contain antiallergic ingredients such as chlorpheniramine and diphenhydramine, which cause drowsiness, and that anyone driving, operating machinery, or working at heights should use them with caution. A driving simulator study in the United States enrolled 40 drivers aged 25 to 44 with seasonal allergic rhinitis. Each person was tested across four treatments spaced one week apart, driving for 1 hour each time: 50 mg diphenhydramine, 60 mg fexofenadine, alcohol up to a blood alcohol concentration of roughly 0.1%, and placebo. Fexofenadine is a non-drowsy allergy medication. The ability to match the lead vehicle's speed was worst after taking diphenhydramine, whereas driving after alcohol and after fexofenadine was noticeably better. Lane-keeping ability deteriorated after both alcohol and diphenhydramine. When a lead car suddenly blocked the lane, braking reaction was slowest after alcohol (average 2.21 seconds), compared to 1.95 seconds after fexofenadine. Driving performance on fexofenadine was comparable to placebo. The authors concluded that diphenhydramine impaired driving ability even more than alcohol. Drivers' self-reported drowsiness did not reflect how well they actually drove, meaning you cannot rely on whether you feel sleepy to judge your fitness to drive.

### 13. Check drug labels for grapefruit warnings if you take cholesterol or blood pressure medicine, and skip grapefruit and pomelos if listed
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=小 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: Free. When prescribed long-term medication, check the "Drug Interactions" and "Precautions" sections of the package insert. If grapefruit is listed, avoid grapefruit, pomelos, Seville oranges, and limes, as well as their juices, while taking the drug. If you enjoy grapefruit, ask your doctor whether you can switch to an alternative that is not affected.
- In plain terms: Grapefruit can multiply the amount of certain medicines in your bloodstream several times over. Even though your dose stays the same, your body absorbs it as if you took far too much. A single grapefruit or one glass of juice is enough to cause this spike, and part of the effect lingers even if you wait a full day before taking your pill. In severe cases, this can damage muscles or kidneys, or trigger dangerous irregular heartbeats that can be fatal. Pomelos carry the same risk, but ordinary sweet oranges do not.
- Benefit: A 2013 review by Canadian pharmacologists noted that more than 85 drugs are known or predicted to interact with grapefruit, with 43 capable of causing serious adverse effects. Severe reactions include torsades de pointes (a life-threatening irregular heartbeat), rhabdomyolysis (massive muscle breakdown that damages the kidneys), myelosuppression, respiratory depression, gastrointestinal bleeding, and acute kidney injury. Furanocoumarins in grapefruit bind irreversibly to CYP3A4, an enzyme in the gut wall that breaks down medications, deactivating it permanently until the body produces new enzyme. Under normal conditions, oral drugs are partially broken down in the gut and liver before entering systemic circulation (the first-pass effect). When gut enzymes are deactivated, less of the drug is broken down, sending far more into the bloodstream. A single whole grapefruit or 200 ml of juice is enough to raise blood drug levels to clinically meaningful concentrations. Taking the blood pressure drug felodipine with grapefruit produces, on average, 3 times the blood concentration compared with water; after the same 250 ml of juice, individual responses ranged from no increase to an 8-fold spike. Drinking 200 ml of grapefruit juice daily for 3 days while taking the cholesterol drug simvastatin increased total systemic drug exposure (AUC) to 330% of that with water. For atorvastatin, juice raised exposure to between 180% and 250%. Pravastatin, rosuvastatin, and fluvastatin are unaffected. Consuming 200 ml of juice within 4 hours before taking medication produces the greatest effect; half the inhibitory effect persists at 10 hours, and roughly one quarter remains at 24 hours. The review notes that Seville oranges (frequently used in marmalade), limes, and pomelos produce the same interaction. Sweet oranges such as navel and Valencia oranges do not contain furanocoumarins and carry no such risk. US FDA educational materials specifically highlight simvastatin, atorvastatin, and nifedipine, warning that drinking large amounts of grapefruit juice leaves too much cholesterol-lowering medication in the body, increasing the risk of liver and muscle damage that can lead to kidney failure.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources:Bailey DG, Dresser G, Arnold JMO (2013). Grapefruit–medication interactions: forbidden fruit or avoidable consequences? CMAJ, 185(4):309-316. <https://doi.org/10.1503/cmaj.120951>；U.S. Food and Drug Administration (2021). Grapefruit Juice and Some Drugs Don't Mix. <https://www.fda.gov/consumers/consumer-updates/grapefruit-juice-and-some-drugs-dont-mix>
- Notes: Not all medications are affected, and susceptibility varies even within the same class. Commonly used interacting drugs listed in the review include the cholesterol-lowering statins simvastatin, lovastatin, and atorvastatin; the blood pressure medications felodipine and nifedipine; as well as amiodarone, ticagrelor, tacrolimus, cyclosporine, domperidone, quetiapine, and oxycodone. Clopidogrel behaves in reverse: grapefruit can prevent it from working properly. If the leaflet is unclear or you are uncertain, consult your prescribing physician or pharmacist. The review notes that adults over 45 buy the most grapefruit and also take the most prescription medications; furthermore, older adults taking felodipine with grapefruit experience a drop in blood pressure without the normal compensatory heart rate increase. The evidence is graded B because the quantitative data come from pharmacokinetic trials measuring blood drug concentrations, while severe clinical outcomes are documented primarily in case reports. The benefit is rated "small" because blood drug levels serve as a surrogate endpoint, and existing studies cannot quantify the absolute reduction in adverse clinical events. 〔0〕

### 14. If you have chronic kidney disease or poor kidney function, do not take painkillers like ibuprofen long-term on your own—consult a doctor first, and avoid starfruit and starfruit juice
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: Free. Ask a doctor or pharmacist before buying painkillers. If you are taking diuretics along with blood pressure medications like ACE inhibitors or ARBs, make sure to mention them. Completely avoid starfruit and starfruit juice.
- In plain terms: For people with poor kidney function, taking painkillers like ibuprofen increases the risk of acute kidney injury, and taking them haphazardly long-term is linked to kidney failure. If you already take a diuretic along with blood pressure pills like an ACE inhibitor or ARB, adding these painkillers raises the risk of acute kidney injury by about 30%. For those with kidney problems, eating starfruit can cause persistent hiccups, confusion, and seizures, and about 30% of reported poisoning cases have resulted in death.
- Benefit: These painkillers are called nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), commonly including ibuprofen, naproxen, and diclofenac. Acute kidney injury refers to an abrupt decline in kidney function within a few days. A systematic review pooled observational studies without control groups in community populations. In the general population currently taking these drugs, the odds ratio (OR) for acute kidney injury was 1.73 (95% CI 1.44–2.07). The odds ratio was somewhat higher in individuals aged 50 and older; the abstract reported an OR of 2.51, while the subgroup analysis and Figure 4 in the main text reported an OR of 2.01 (both cited a 95% CI of 1.52–2.68, reflecting an internal inconsistency in the original paper). Among those with pre-existing chronic kidney disease, pooled results from 5 studies showed an OR of 1.63 (1.22–2.19). The authors noted that none of the studies reported baseline incidence rates, precluding absolute risk calculations. A UK study followed 487,372 individuals taking antihypertensive medications for an average of 5.9 years. Combining a diuretic, an ACE inhibitor or ARB, and an NSAID is referred to as "triple therapy." Under triple therapy, the incidence rate ratio for acute kidney injury was 1.31 (95% CI 1.12–1.53), about 31% higher. The risk peaked during the first 30 days of concurrent use, with an incidence rate ratio of 1.82 (1.35–2.46). Dual combinations of just one antihypertensive drug and an NSAID showed no increased risk. The KDIGO 2024 Clinical Practice Guideline for chronic kidney disease states that individuals with chronic kidney disease may be more susceptible to drug-induced nephrotoxicity. Practice Point 4.1.3 recommends reviewing and restricting potentially nephrotoxic over-the-counter medications and herbal remedies. The guideline notes that indiscriminate, long-term self-administration of these drugs is associated with an increased risk of kidney failure and should be discouraged. U.S. regulations mandate that over-the-counter labels for these medications state: ask a doctor before use if you have hypertension, heart disease, liver cirrhosis, or kidney disease, or if you are taking a diuretic. Evidence regarding starfruit comes from case series. A review evaluated 126 patients from 33 reports published between 1993 and 2016. The most common symptom was intractable hiccups (65%). Confusion and seizures were most frequently associated with death. No deaths occurred among patients with previously normal kidney function. Mortality was 36% among non-dialysis chronic kidney disease patients and 27% among dialysis patients. In individuals with normal kidney function who developed toxicity, the quantity of starfruit consumed was substantially higher. Another review of 136 patients found that 69.1% had pre-existing renal impairment. Renal biopsies showed oxalate-induced acute kidney injury, with authors concluding that oxalate and a neurotoxin called caramboxin are the primary toxic agents. Over a 10-year period, one hospital admitted 20 patients with kidney failure who developed illness after consuming starfruit or starfruit juice; 8 died, all within 5 days, including 1 who had not yet initiated dialysis. In a Brazilian cohort of 32 uremic patients, all those who received prompt hemodialysis—including severe cases—recovered without sequelae. Severe cases that were untreated or treated only with peritoneal dialysis did not survive. The KDIGO guideline lists starfruit among herbal remedies and dietary supplements with evidence of potential nephrotoxicity.
