# 3. Don't Waste Energy

This section only counts energy and time: usable attention per day, reaction speed, error rates, and recovery time after interruptions. All numbers come from direct measurements in experiments or cohort studies, not converted into lifespan or mortality. Most items carry an evidence grade of B, which is the norm in this field and does not mean the advice is unsound.
### 1. Turn off non-essential notifications and keep the phone out of sight while working
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: A few minutes of setup; almost no willpower required
- In plain terms: When a phone pings, even if you don't look at it or touch it, your score on an attention task drops — by roughly the same amount as if you had actually answered a call or read a text. Even just having your phone sit on the desk while you resist looking at it leaves your brain less working capacity to spare, and the more attached you are to your phone, the bigger the drop.
- Benefit: Merely receiving a notification (without looking at or touching the phone) significantly lowers performance on attention tasks, with an effect size comparable to actually taking a call or texting (laboratory study, university students); two experiments show that even when people successfully resist checking their phone, the mere presence of their own phone reduces available cognitive capacity, with the largest decline among the most phone-dependent
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Stothart, Mitchum & Yehnert (2015). The attentional cost of receiving a cell phone notification. Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Perception and Performance. <https://doi.org/10.1037/xhp0000100>；Ward, Duke, Gneezy & Bos (2017). Brain Drain: The Mere Presence of One's Own Smartphone Reduces Available Cognitive Capacity. Journal of the Association for Consumer Research. <https://doi.org/10.1086/691462>
- Notes: These are all single laboratory tasks; the cumulative effect in everyday work has not been directly measured. Jobs that genuinely require being on call can keep notifications for a small number of contacts only

### 2. Fix your wake-up time, weekends included
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Free; gives up sleeping in on weekends, needs a bit of willpower
- In plain terms: The most chaotic fifth of people run a body clock about 2.5 hours later than the most regular fifth — the equivalent of living in permanent jet lag. The more regular a student's schedule, the better their grades.
- Benefit: 61 university students tracked for 30 days: the least regular fifth had a circadian rhythm about 2.5 hours later than the most regular fifth (melatonin onset 00:08 vs 21:32), with peak sleepiness about 1.8 hours later; schedule regularity correlated with academic performance (r = 0.37)
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Phillips et al. (2017). Irregular sleep/wake patterns are associated with poorer academic performance and delayed circadian and sleep/wake timing. Scientific Reports. <https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-017-03171-4>
- Notes: Single cohort, observational, small sample. Modeling suggests the rhythm differences mainly come from different light exposure, so a fixed wake-up time only works when paired with morning light

### 3. Sleep 7 to 8 hours a night; don't treat 6 hours as enough
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=多 毅力=些 收益=大 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Set aside an extra 1 to 2 hours per day for sleep
- In plain terms: Sleep only 6 hours a night for two weeks in a row and your brain performs at the level of someone who has gone without sleep for two days and two nights straight. The catch is you can't feel it: subjective sleepiness rises for the first few days and then stops rising, and people sleeping 6 hours rate themselves exactly as sleepy as people sleeping 4. So the statement "6 hours is enough for me" is itself a symptom of sleep debt.
- Benefit: 48 healthy adults aged 21 to 38 slept only 6 hours per night for 14 consecutive days, and cognitive performance declined cumulatively to the level seen after 2 nights of total sleep deprivation; meanwhile subjective sleepiness scores rose only in the first few days and then barely increased, with no difference in subjective sleepiness between the 6-hour and 4-hour groups (RCT); acute sleep deprivation (up to 48 hours) has an effect size of g = -0.776 on simple-attention lapses and g = -0.125 on reasoning accuracy (meta-analysis of 70 studies)
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Van Dongen, Maislin, Mullington & Dinges (2003). The cumulative cost of additional wakefulness. Sleep. <https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/26.2.117>；Lim & Dinges (2010). A meta-analysis of the impact of short-term sleep deprivation on cognitive variables. Psychological Bulletin. <https://doi.org/10.1037/a0018883>
- Notes: The key point is that "I don't feel sleepy" is not evidence of adequate sleep — people who chronically sleep 6 hours adapt subjectively, but their performance does not. The meta-analysis covers acute total deprivation; the effect size for chronic restriction comes from a single RCT

### 4. No caffeine after two in the afternoon
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=大 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Zero; people in the habit of afternoon coffee need a little willpower
- In plain terms: A large coffee (400 mg) drunk 6 hours before bed costs you a measured hour or more of sleep that night — yet your own sleep diary shows no difference at all. A regular cup of coffee (107 mg) has to be finished more than 8.8 hours before bedtime to leave sleep untouched — for someone going to bed at eleven, two in the afternoon is the cutoff.
- Benefit: 400 mg of caffeine taken 6 hours before bedtime reduced objectively monitored total sleep time by 1.1 to 1.2 hours versus placebo, while participants' sleep diaries reported no difference — that is, they could not detect it themselves; meta-analysis: caffeine reduces total sleep time by 45 minutes on average and sleep efficiency by 7%, and a cup of coffee (107 mg) must be consumed more than 8.8 hours before bedtime to avoid affecting sleep
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Drake, Roehrs, Shambroom & Roth (2013). Caffeine effects on sleep taken 0, 3, or 6 hours before going to bed. Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.5664/jcsm.3170>；Gardiner et al. (2023). The effect of caffeine on subsequent sleep: A systematic review and meta-analysis. Sleep Medicine Reviews. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2023.101764>
- Notes: Drake is a single laboratory study and its 400 mg dose is on the high side (about two to three cups of coffee). Slow caffeine metabolizers need an earlier cutoff. The benefit here is measured in sleep duration and stacks with item 3

### 5. Batch email and messages at fixed times a few times per day
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Free; requires telling colleagues your reply rhythm; moderate willpower
- In plain terms: The same people, two weeks, compared against themselves: in the week when email was limited to checking 3 times a day, everyday stress was noticeably lower — while the number of emails sent and received stayed the same. Unrestricted, people check email an average of 15.5 times a day.
- Benefit: 124 adults in a two-week within-subject comparison: in the week limited to checking email 3 times a day, daily stress was significantly lower than in the unrestricted week (Cohen's d = 0.37), with no difference in the number of emails received or replied to across the two weeks; at baseline, people checked email an average of 15.5 times per day
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Kushlev & Dunn (2015). Checking email less frequently reduces stress. Computers in Human Behavior. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2014.11.005>
- Notes: Single study, small-to-medium effect size, and the outcome measured is stress rather than output. Instant messaging has not been studied directly; only analogy applies

### 6. Block out even a few seconds of interruption when doing work that needs sustained thinking
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=大 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Closing a door, wearing headphones, or hanging up a "do not disturb" sign; free
- In plain terms: A single interruption averaging 2.8 seconds doubles the chance of making an error on the next step; a 4.4-second interruption triples it. Field tracking shows the interrupted task takes about 25 minutes on average to get back to — with two other tasks squeezed in along the way. The people being interrupted work faster, but their stress, frustration, and effort are all higher.
- Benefit: An interruption averaging 2.8 seconds doubled the step error rate in a sequential task, and a 4.4-second interruption tripled it (laboratory); field observation of 24 information workers: work topics switched every 11 minutes on average, 57% of work topics were interrupted, resuming the interrupted work took an average of 25 minutes 26 seconds that day, with 2.26 other tasks interleaved in between; the interrupted participants worked faster but reported higher stress, frustration, time pressure, and effort (experiment with 48 participants)
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Altmann, Trafton & Hambrick (2014). Momentary interruptions can derail the train of thought. Journal of Experimental Psychology: General. <https://doi.org/10.1037/a0030986>；Mark, Gonzalez & Harris (2005). No task left behind? Examining the nature of fragmented work. CHI 2005. <https://doi.org/10.1145/1054972.1055017>；Mark, Gudith & Klocke (2008). The cost of interrupted work: More speed and stress. CHI 2008. <https://doi.org/10.1145/1357054.1357072>
- Notes: The field study separates external interruptions from self-initiated internal ones; closing the door only blocks the former. Use together with items 1 and 5

### 7. Do one thing at a time; don't reply to messages during meetings
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Zero; needs a little willpower
- In plain terms: Every time you switch tasks, reactions get slower and errors more likely. People who routinely run several screens at once have not trained up a multitasking ability — they actually perform worse on task-switching tests.
- Benefit: After a task switch, responses are significantly slower and errors more frequent (review); heavy media multitaskers are more easily distracted by irrelevant stimuli and irrelevant memories, and perform worse on task-switching tests (laboratory, university students)
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Monsell (2003). Task switching. Trends in Cognitive Sciences. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S1364-6613(03)00028-7>；Ophir, Nass & Wagner (2009). Cognitive control in media multitaskers. PNAS. <https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0903620106>
- Notes: The exact percentage cost of switching depends heavily on the task type, so no single number is given here. Ophir is a cross-sectional comparison and cannot distinguish "multitasking causes poor attention" from "people with poor attention multitask more"

### 8. No light-emitting screens in the hour before bed; if you must read, use paper or e-ink
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=是 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Free; requires willpower
- In plain terms: Reading on a light-emitting screen before bed takes about 10 minutes longer to fall asleep than reading a paper book, suppresses melatonin by 55%, and pushes your body clock back more than 1.5 hours — leaving you sleepier the next morning and slower to fully wake.
- Benefit: Laboratory crossover trial: reading a light-emitting e-book before bed took about 10 minutes longer to fall asleep than reading a paper book (25.7 vs 15.8 minutes), suppressed evening melatonin by 55%, and delayed the next day's circadian rhythm by more than 1.5 hours (melatonin onset 22:31 vs 21:01), with greater morning sleepiness and longer time to full alertness the next day
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Chang, Aeschbach, Duffy & Czeisler (2015). Evening use of light-emitting eReaders negatively affects sleep, circadian timing, and next-morning alertness. PNAS. <https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1418490112>
- Notes: Small sample, controlled laboratory conditions; the effect at everyday phone brightness and usage duration may be smaller. How much night mode reduces the effect was not measured in this study

### 9. Go to bed on time; don't stay up for games, short videos, or porn
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=是 收益=大 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Free; requires long-term resistance to the "one more episode" pull — keeping the phone out of the bedroom takes the least effort
- In plain terms: In 2025, people in China slept an average of 6.97 hours a night and fell asleep at 0:10 on average. People who use a phone or tablet before bed are more than twice as likely to get insufficient sleep and 2.7 times as likely to be sleepy during the day; the numbers are similar when the device merely sits in the bedroom unused — so the simplest fix is not to bring the phone into the bedroom at all.
- Benefit: In 2025, residents of China slept an average of 6.97 hours at night, with an average sleep onset time of 0:10 (Chinese Sleep Research Society). Bedtime use of portable devices is associated with insufficient sleep (OR 2.17), poor sleep quality (OR 1.46), and daytime sleepiness (OR 2.72), and the same holds when the device is merely present in the bedroom but unused (OR 1.79/1.53/2.27) (meta-analysis of 20 cross-sectional studies, 125198 children and adolescents); among 844 adults aged 18 to 94, those who still used their phone after lights-out fell asleep more slowly, had lower sleep efficiency, and were more fatigued during the day (cross-sectional)
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Carter et al. (2016). Association Between Portable Screen-Based Media Device Access or Use and Sleep Outcomes. JAMA Pediatrics. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapediatrics.2016.2341>；Exelmans & Van den Bulck (2016). Bedtime mobile phone use and sleep in adults. Social Science & Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.socscimed.2015.11.037>；中国睡眠研究会 (2026). 2026 中国睡眠健康研究白皮书（福建省卫健委转载健康报报道）. <https://wjw.fujian.gov.cn/jggk/csxx/xcc/mtbd/202603/t20260323_7114382.htm>
- Notes: Contested: the cost of staying up late itself is covered in item 3 of this section; there is no reliable causal evidence on how porn affects ordinary people's energy and cognition — one systematic review and meta-analysis argues that "porn problems" stem more from a mismatch between personal values and behavior than from the content itself (Grubbs et al. 2019, <https://doi.org/10.1007/s10508-018-1248-x>), and another study pooling data from 280 men found that viewing time was unrelated to erectile function in partnered sex (Prause & Pfaus 2015, <https://doi.org/10.1002/sm2.58>); what this item targets is only the sleep time porn displaces. ### 10. Make no major decisions and send no important messages late at night
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=小 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Free; it only means pushing things to after you wake up
- In plain terms: People kept awake for 49.5 hours take a risky-decision experiment: when well rested, they quickly learn to avoid high-risk options; once sleep-deprived, they pick the high-risk ones over and over — and the older they are, the stronger the effect. Messages sent and decisions made late at night work the same way.
- Benefit: 34 healthy adults performed the Iowa Gambling Task (an experiment measuring risky decision-making) after 49.5 hours without sleep; when rested they quickly learned to avoid high-risk options, but after sleep deprivation they chose high-risk options more often, with older participants more affected (experiment, single study)
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Killgore, Balkin & Wesensten (2006). Impaired decision making following 49 h of sleep deprivation. Journal of Sleep Research. <https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2869.2006.00487.x>
- Notes: The study covers the extreme case of two consecutive sleepless nights; the effect of ordinary staying-up until two in the morning has not been directly measured. The "no messages" part is experience-based (C) — sending them the next day is not too late

### 11. When afternoon drowsiness hits, nap 10 minutes, not half an hour
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: 10 minutes plus somewhere to lie down or rest your head
- In plain terms: A 10-minute nap perks you up on the spot and the effect lasts about two and a half hours; 5 minutes does basically nothing; 20 minutes only kicks in half an hour later; after 30 minutes you wake into a stretch of grogginess during which you perform worse than if you hadn't napped at all.
- Benefit: After a night of restricted sleep, a 10-minute nap immediately improved sleepiness, fatigue, vigor, and cognitive performance, with part of the benefit lasting to 155 minutes later; 5 minutes produced almost no benefit; the benefit of 20 minutes only appeared 35 minutes later; a 30-minute nap was followed first by a stretch of sleep inertia (performance worse right after waking) before improving
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Brooks & Lack (2006). A brief afternoon nap following nocturnal sleep restriction: Which nap duration is most recuperative? Sleep. <https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/29.6.831>
- Notes: Single laboratory study, healthy young adults. If you can't fall asleep, don't force it — there is no research-backed benefit to simply lying with eyes closed for 10 minutes

### 12. Use earplugs or find a quiet room for memory-heavy work when working in an open-plan office
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=少 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=小 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Earplugs cost a few dozen yuan; booking a meeting room takes some time
- In plain terms: When background noise rises from 39 decibels to 51, fewer words get remembered, people feel more tired, and motivation to work drops. Watching a nature video with sound during breaks restores energy better than going on listening to office noise.
- Benefit: Students working in a simulated open office: when background noise rose from 39 dB to 51 dB (LAeq), they remembered fewer words, rated themselves as more fatigued, and showed reduced motivation; during breaks, watching a nature film with sound restored vigor ratings better than listening to river sounds alone or to office noise
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Jahncke, Hygge, Halin, Green & Dimberg (2011). Open-plan office noise: Cognitive performance and restoration. Journal of Environmental Psychology. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jenvp.2011.07.002>
- Notes: Single laboratory study. The main harm from noise comes from intelligible human speech; white-noise headphones help with that, but headphones were not tested in this study

### 13. Keep working hours under 49 hours a week
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=少 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=大 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Possibly less overtime pay, or the cost of negotiating with your manager
- In plain terms: Output records from British munitions factories in World War I: up to 49 hours a week, output grows with hours worked; past 49 hours, the gains start falling away, and a 70-hour week produces almost exactly as much as a 56-hour week — the extra 14 hours are wasted.
- Benefit: Output records of workers in British munitions factories during World War I: up to 49 hours per week, output was proportional to hours; above 49 hours, output gains diminished, peaking around 63 hours, with output at 70 hours almost no different from 56 hours — that is, the extra 14 hours were wasted
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Pencavel (2015). The Productivity of Working Hours. The Economic Journal. <https://doi.org/10.1111/ecoj.12166>
- Notes: The data come from manual labor, century-old factories, and a predominantly female workforce; the threshold need not be the same for knowledge work, and the author himself said other trades could be higher or lower. The directional conclusion (marginal output approaches zero past some point) is more reliable than the specific figure of 49

### 14. When you catch yourself replaying the same upsetting thing, switch to a hands-on task
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Free; requires noticing that you are ruminating, which itself takes practice
- In plain terms: Going over the same upsetting thing again and again doesn't produce an answer — it deepens depression, nails negative thoughts in more firmly, leaves you less able to solve the problem and less likely to actually act, and it wears out the people around you who were willing to listen. Once you notice you're circling in place, go do something that involves your hands.
- Benefit: Review: rumination worsens depression, strengthens negative thinking, impairs problem-solving, hinders concrete action, and drains social support; it is associated with anxiety, binge eating, heavy drinking, and self-harm
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Nolen-Hoeksema, Wisco & Lyubomirsky (2008). Rethinking Rumination. Perspectives on Psychological Science. <https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-6924.2008.00088.x>
- Notes: This is the hardest item to quantify in energy terms; the review gives direction, not numbers. "Switching to another task" is the author's own practice; what the literature supports is that distraction beats continued rumination — which specific task has not been comparatively studied

### 15. Treat thoughts like "things will definitely get worse" as a symptom, not as fact
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: 0 yuan
- In plain terms: In a twin study with 20 years of follow-up, each step up on the pessimism scale came with roughly 13% higher probability of dying in the same period, and roughly 20% higher for cardiovascular death; conversely, high optimism scores brought no corresponding benefit. So what needs handling is pessimism, not "insufficient positivity."
- Benefit: 2978 Australian twins aged over 50, mean follow-up 20 years, 1068 deaths: each 1 standard deviation higher on the pessimism scale, total mortality hazard ratio 1.134 (95% CI 1.065–1.207, P=8.85×10⁻⁵), cardiovascular death 1.196 (1.045–1.368, P=0.0093), no association with cancer death; optimism scores showed no significant association with mortality
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Whitfield JB, Zhu G, Landers JG, Martin NG (2020). Pessimism is associated with greater all-cause and cardiovascular mortality, but optimism is not protective. Scientific Reports, 10, 12609. <https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-020-69388-y>
- Notes: Contested. Observational study; the authors rebut reverse causation by arguing that if illness caused pessimism, cancer deaths should also be associated — but residual confounding remains. Note that optimism has no protective effect, so there is no need to force yourself to "think positive"; what to do is stop treating pessimistic expectations as accurate forecasts of the future

### 16. Cut down on draining relationships and learn to refuse requests you don't want to take on
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=是 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Short-term social friction, possibly losing some relationships; requires considerable willpower
- In plain terms: The damage done by one person who drains you is bigger, and more consistent, than the benefit brought by one who supports you. So subtract first, add later.
- Benefit: A survey of 120 widowed women aged 60 to 89: negative social interactions were more consistently and more strongly associated with psychological well-being than positive ones — that is, the loss caused by one draining person outweighs the gain from one supportive person
- Evidence grade: C
- Sources: Rook (1984). The negative side of social interaction: Impact on psychological well-being. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology. <https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.46.5.1097>
- Notes: Special sample and cross-sectional data; the outcome measured is well-being rather than energy, and placing this item in this section is the author's inference. There is no objective standard for which relationships count as "draining" — you can only judge for yourself

### 17. Fix low-value decisions like what to wear and what to eat
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=小 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Free; a little time up front to settle on defaults
- In plain terms: There is no hard evidence for this one. The original experiment behind "making choices drains willpower" failed twice in large-scale replications (multi-lab replications with two thousand and then three thousand participants, effects essentially zero). Fixing what you wear and eat is still worth doing — the reason is the saved minutes and fewer rounds of deliberation, not "saving willpower."
- Benefit: Not quantifiable. The original experiments claimed that making choices and exerting self-control deplete a shared limited resource (ego depletion), but two large pre-registered multi-lab replications failed to reproduce this: 23 laboratories, 2141 participants, d = 0.04, 95% CI [-0.07, 0.15]; 36 laboratories, 3531 participants, d = 0.06, not significant
- Evidence grade: C
- Sources: Baumeister, Bratslavsky, Muraven & Tice (1998). Ego depletion: Is the active self a limited resource? Journal of Personality and Social Psychology. <https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.74.5.1252>；Hagger et al. (2016). A Multilab Preregistered Replication of the Ego-Depletion Effect. Perspectives on Psychological Science. <https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691616652873>；Vohs et al. (2021). A Multisite Preregistered Paradigmatic Test of the Ego-Depletion Effect. Psychological Science. <https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797621989733>
- Notes: Contested. "Decision fatigue" is treated as settled in popular books, but the laboratory evidence largely fell apart in the replication crisis. This item is kept only because its cost is nearly zero and the time saved is real; don't expect it to improve your judgment in the afternoon

### 18. Walk away first when angry; treat the other person as weather, not as an enemy
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Free; holding your tongue on the spot, turning and leaving, then deciding later whether to deal with it takes a little practice
- In plain terms: Reinterpreting what just happened, or briefly putting yourself in the other person's shoes, both genuinely bring the emotion down (the latter works better); suppressing it and keeping a straight face does nothing. Also, people who punished the offender ended up ruminating on the incident for longer afterward, while those who didn't retaliate moved on faster — the satisfaction revenge will bring is something people overestimate in advance.
- Benefit: Meta-analysis of 306 experimental comparisons: reappraising what happened (cognitive reappraisal) had an effect of d = 0.36 on emotion, taking the other person's perspective d = 0.45, distraction d = 0.27, and suppressing feelings was ineffective (d = -0.04); three experiments showed that people who punished an offender ruminated on them longer afterward, those who did not punish "moved on" faster, and people overestimated in advance the satisfaction revenge would bring
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Webb TL, Miles E, Sheeran P (2012). Dealing with feeling: A meta-analysis of the effectiveness of strategies derived from the process model of emotion regulation. Psychological Bulletin. <https://doi.org/10.1037/a0027600>；Carlsmith KM, Wilson TD, Gilbert DT (2008). The paradoxical consequences of revenge. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology. <https://doi.org/10.1037/a0012165>
- Notes: The meta-analysis measures emotion intensity, not energy; effect sizes are small to medium, and the revenge studies are a single series of experiments — hence the B. "The empty boat" is a parable from Zhuangzi's "Shan Mu": an empty boat bumping into you provokes no anger, while a crewed one does, even though the bump is identical — this is an analogy, not evidence. If there is a real loss, take the legal route in sections 8 and 9; it is far cheaper than blowing up on the spot.

### 19. When feeling low, first do the highest-value-for-effort things: move, get sunlight, sleep on schedule, talk to someone, call 12356
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=中 毅力=些 收益=大 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Free or minimal; brisk walking, jogging, or strength training a few times a week, going outdoors in daylight during the day, a fixed schedule, one phone call
- In plain terms: For depression, brisk walking and jogging worked best across these 218 trials, with yoga and strength training slightly behind — the higher the intensity, the better it works; light exposure is strongly effective for seasonal depression and also works for the non-seasonal kind; online self-help cognitive behavioral courses help, and having a person guide you works better when symptoms are heavier. If things feel unbearable, call 12356 first.
- Benefit: Network meta-analysis of 218 randomized controlled trials, 14170 people: versus active controls, walking or jogging had an effect of g = -0.62 on depression (95% credible interval -0.80 to -0.45), yoga -0.55, strength training -0.49, with higher intensity working better; light therapy meta-analysis: effect size 0.84 (0.60 to 1.08) for seasonal depression, 0.53 (0.18 to 0.89) for non-seasonal depression; individual-participant-data network meta-analysis of internet cognitive behavioral therapy (online self-help courses): both guided and unguided outperformed control, with guided better for moderate-to-severe cases (PHQ-9 above 9); the 12356 national psychological assistance hotline operates no fewer than 18 hours a day
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Noetel M 等 (2024). Effect of exercise for depression: systematic review and network meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials. BMJ. <https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2023-075847>；Golden RN 等 (2005). The efficacy of light therapy in the treatment of mood disorders: a review and meta-analysis of the evidence. American Journal of Psychiatry. <https://doi.org/10.1176/appi.ajp.162.4.656>；Karyotaki E 等 (2021). ### 20. Treat the police, doctors, and bank tellers as people working by the rules, not as roles: what moves things is paperwork and deadlines, not emotion
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=小 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: 0 yuan; what changes is your expectations and the order of your actions — the first time, you have to hold back the urge to "argue them around to your point of view"
- In plain terms: A child's model is "the police catch bad guys, doctors save people, teachers are just teachers". The adult model is: they are people working by the rules of their post, with the discretion in their hands boxed in by paperwork, deadlines, and supervision; a person's attitude is individual variation and has almost nothing to do with whether your matter gets done or how many days it takes. Change the model, and your effort goes where it can move something: when reporting to the police, ask for the case-acceptance receipt on the spot; if no case is opened, demand the written notice; chase it up on the statutory deadlines; if you are not satisfied there is reconsideration, review, and procuratorate case-filing supervision (Chapter 8, item 37). When seeking care, you queue by triage level and medical need; the "priority" a red packet tries to buy is exactly what the rules name as a forbidden transaction; to thank a doctor who saved you, use a thank-you letter and the satisfaction survey (Chapter 24, item 12, and item 8). Wanting to "send something so they take more care" is, for people handling cases or enforcement, the aggravated form of bribery named in black and white (Chapter 8, item 38). Rude treatment is a separate matter — take it through the complaint channel, and don't let it pull you off a process that was already working.
- Benefit: switching how you deal with institutions from "pleading, getting angry, repeating your grievance" to "get the paperwork, note the deadlines, appeal through the channels" saves the time spent on repeated communication and emotional rumination; it is a one-time cognitive adjustment, not a daily time saving. The checkable anchors are the deadlines themselves, all written into the documents: public security cases must be handled within 30 days of acceptance, extendable by 30 days with approval of the public security organ at the next level up for major and complex cases; where a case with a complainant is decided not to be filed, the notice of non-filing must be served within 3 days, the complainant may apply for reconsideration within 7 days with the public security organ deciding within 30 days, and if still unsatisfied may apply for review to the next higher organ within 7 days
- Evidence grade: C

Bank tellers and government service windows work the same way: operations leave records, can be re-checked, and errors are traceable to a person — so what you should do is keep the receipt, ask for the slip, and complain through the channels, not guess from "will he cheat me". Legitimate priorities are written in the open — emergency care, for instance, is ranked by condition, not by first-come-first-served (Chapter 24, item 8); where you meet obvious differential treatment, that is the object of complaints and supervision, not an unwritten rule to imitate.
