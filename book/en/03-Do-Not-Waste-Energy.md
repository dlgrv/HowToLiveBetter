# 3. Don’t waste energy

This section only counts energy and time. It looks at four things: how much attention you can use each day, how fast you react, how many mistakes you make, and how long it takes to get back after an interruption. Most figures are measured directly, from experiments or from tracking a group of people over time. These numbers are not converted into lifespan or mortality. Most items in this section have evidence grade B. That is how this field is; it does not mean the advice fails. Item 19 is about expectations when dealing with police, doctors, and bank tellers; it cites regulations, not experiments, so its grade is C. Item 15 (treat pessimistic thoughts as a symptom) and item 22 (treat “others expect me to be perfect” as a symptom) cite mortality and suicide-risk figures. Their frame differs from the rest of this section; do not compare them side by side.

Items are grouped by theme below, with item numbers in parentheses.

**Attention at work**: Turn off non-essential notifications (item 1), batch-process messages each day (item 5), block brief interruptions (item 6), do one thing at a time (item 7), use earplugs in open offices (item 12), and do not keep adding weekly work hours for their own sake (item 13).

**Sleep and staying up**: Keep a fixed wake-up time (item 2), get 7 to 8 hours of sleep each night (item 3), avoid caffeine after 2 p.m. (item 4), skip glowing screens for the hour before bed (item 8), go to bed on time (item 9), make no major decisions late at night (item 10), take a 10-minute nap when drowsy in the afternoon (item 11), try cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia first for long-term insomnia (item 26), and get checked for sleep apnea if you snore, stop breathing in sleep, or are sleepy by day (item 27).

**Thoughts in the head**: Switch to a hands-on task when you keep replaying something (item 14), treat pessimistic thoughts as a symptom (item 15), halve your estimate when you feel you looked foolish (item 21), and treat “others expect me to be perfect” as a symptom (item 22).

**Anger and low mood**: Leave first when angry (item 17), do a few high-value things first when mood is low (item 18), do not vent anger by punching a bag (item 23), and write what is on your mind for several days in a row (item 24).

**People and social media**: Learn to decline requests you do not want (item 16), treat police and doctors as people who work by rules (item 19), and set limits on apps that show peers’ feeds (item 20).

**Pain in the body**: For period pain, take an ibuprofen-type painkiller (item 25).
### 1. Turn off non‑essential notifications and keep your phone out of sight while working
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: No cost at all. It takes just a few minutes to adjust the settings, and hardly any willpower is required.
- In plain terms: Even if you don’t look at or touch your phone when it rings, your performance on attention‑related tasks still drops. The drop is roughly the same as when you actually answer calls or send messages. Another scenario: even when the phone sits on the desk and you resist the urge to glance at it, the amount of mental capacity available still decreases. The more dependent a person is on their phone, the greater this drop tends to be.
- Benefit: Merely receiving a notification — without looking at or interacting with the phone — noticeably impairs performance on attention tasks. The degree of impairment is comparable to actually answering calls or sending messages. These findings come from laboratory studies involving university students. Two additional experiments also show that even when participants successfully refrain from looking at their phone, having it nearby still reduces their available mental resources; this effect is strongest among heavy phone users.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources:Stothart, Mitchum & Yehnert (2015). The attentional cost of receiving a cell phone notification. Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Perception and Performance. <https://doi.org/10.1037/xhp0000100>；Ward, Duke, Gneezy & Bos (2017). Brain Drain: The Mere Presence of One's Own Smartphone Reduces Available Cognitive Capacity. Journal of the Association for Consumer Research. <https://doi.org/10.1086/691462>
- Notes: All of these results are based on single‑task experiments conducted in a lab setting. No research has examined how these effects accumulate over days or weeks in real‑world conditions. For jobs where immediate responsiveness is essential, it may be advisable to allow notifications only from a limited set of contacts.

### 2. Keep a fixed wake-up time, even on weekends
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: No money. On weekends you have to sleep in less; that takes a little willpower.
- In plain terms: For the most irregular fifth of people, the body clock runs about 2.5 hours later than for the most regular fifth. That is like living with jet lag all the time. Students with more regular schedules also get better grades.
- Benefit: 61 university students kept records for 30 days. The least regular fifth had a body clock (circadian rhythm) about 2.5 hours later than the most regular fifth. Specifically, the time melatonin began to rise was 00:08 versus 21:32. The stretch when they felt sleepiest was also about 1.8 hours later. The more regular the schedule, the better academic performance (correlation coefficient r = 0.37).
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources:Phillips et al. (2017). Irregular sleep/wake patterns are associated with poorer academic performance and delayed circadian and sleep/wake timing. Scientific Reports. <https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-017-03171-4>
- Notes: This is the only study; the sample is small and there was no grouped control. Researchers used a model to estimate that clock differences mainly come from different daily light exposure. They suggest trying light to adjust, but did not run a trial. So besides a fixed wake-up time, getting outdoors for daylight is an inference from their estimate. How to catch up after an occasional late night is in Section 2, Item 38 (after staying up late, catch up the next night): go to bed earlier that night, do not sleep until noon the next day.

### 3. Get 7 to 8 hours of sleep each night; do not treat 6 hours as enough
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=多 毅力=些 收益=大 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: No money. You need to leave 1 to 2 more hours each day for sleep. Going to bed earlier takes a little willpower.
- In plain terms: Two weeks in a row of only 6 hours a night, and brain performance falls to about the level of two full nights with no sleep. The problem is you do not feel it. Self-rated sleepiness rises for the first few days then stops rising; people on 6 hours score as sleepy as people on 4 hours. So the sense that “6 hours is enough for me” is not reliable.
- Benefit: 48 healthy adults aged 21 to 38 slept only 6 hours a night for 14 days straight. Their cognitive performance fell cumulatively, to about the level of 2 full nights with no sleep. Self-rated sleepiness rose only in the first few days, then barely rose further. Self-rated sleepiness did not differ between the 6-hour and 4-hour groups. This was a randomized controlled trial (RCT). Separately, short total sleep loss (acute sleep deprivation, no more than 48 hours) had an effect size g = -0.776 on simple attention lapses and g = -0.125 on reasoning accuracy. That part pooled 70 studies.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources:Van Dongen, Maislin, Mullington & Dinges (2003). The cumulative cost of additional wakefulness. Sleep. <https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/26.2.117>；Lim & Dinges (2010). A meta-analysis of the impact of short-term sleep deprivation on cognitive variables. Psychological Bulletin. <https://doi.org/10.1037/a0018883>
- Notes: The main point is that “I do not feel sleepy” is not proof you have slept enough. People who sleep 6 hours long-term only feel used to it; performance has not caught up. The pooled part is about a full night awake. “Chronic short sleep” is backed by only one randomized controlled trial. How sleep duration links to death risk is counted separately; see Section 2, Item 13 (about 7 hours a night): that side uses mortality, and the 7-hour band has the lowest risk.

### 4. Do not touch caffeine after 2 p.m.
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=大 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: No money. People used to afternoon coffee have to hold off; that takes a little willpower.
- In plain terms: Taking 400 mg of caffeine 6 hours before bed, about three or four cups of coffee, showed up on instruments as more than 1 hour less sleep that night. A sleep diary you write yourself shows no difference at all. A 250 mL cup of coffee (107 mg) has to be finished more than 8.8 hours before bed if it is not to affect sleep. For someone who goes to bed at 11, 2 p.m. is the line.
- Benefit: Taking a tablet with 400 mg of caffeine 6 hours before bed, total sleep time measured by instruments was 1.1 to 1.2 hours less than with a placebo tablet. Counting a 250 mL cup at 107 mg, 400 mg is about three or four cups of coffee. The placebo tablet looked the same and contained no caffeine. The sleep diaries participants wrote themselves reported no difference; they could not tell. Pooling several studies: caffeine cut total sleep time by 45 minutes on average. The share of time in bed that was actually sleep (sleep efficiency) fell by 7%. A 250 mL cup of coffee (107 mg) has to be finished more than 8.8 hours before bed if it is not to affect sleep.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources:Drake, Roehrs, Shambroom & Roth (2013). Caffeine effects on sleep taken 0, 3, or 6 hours before going to bed. Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.5664/jcsm.3170>；Gardiner et al. (2023). The effect of caffeine on subsequent sleep: A systematic review and meta-analysis. Sleep Medicine Reviews. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2023.101764>
- Notes: The Drake study was done in only one laboratory. The 400 mg it used is on the high side; it equals drinking a whole day’s ceiling at once. The ceiling is in Section 2, Item 26 (three to four cups of coffee). People who metabolize caffeine slowly need an earlier cutoff. What you get back from less caffeine is time asleep, and it stacks with item 3 (7 to 8 hours a night).

### 5. Batch-process emails and messages a few times per day
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: No monetary cost. However, it takes some time to explain to colleagues how often you’ll be checking messages. It also requires a bit of willpower to resist the urge to check them constantly.
- In plain terms: The same group of participants was studied over two weeks. During the week when they limited email checks to just three times per day, their daily stress levels dropped noticeably compared to the unrestricted week — while the total number of emails sent and received stayed the same. Without any limits, people checked their emails an average of 15.5 times per day.
- Benefit: In a two-week self-controlled trial involving 124 adults, those who limited email checks to three times daily experienced significantly lower daily stress than when they checked freely (effect size: Cohen’s d = 0.37). The overall volume of emails received and replied to did not differ between the two conditions. Without restrictions, participants checked emails 15.5 times per day on average.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources:Kushlev & Dunn (2015). Checking email less frequently reduces stress. Computers in Human Behavior. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2014.11.005>
- Notes: Only one study has examined this approach, showing a modest effect size. It measured stress levels rather than actual work output. No direct research exists on limiting instant messaging checks; this recommendation is extrapolated from similar email studies.

### 6. When doing tasks that require continuous thinking, keep out even brief interruptions
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=大 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: No cost at all. Just close the door, put on headphones, or hang a “Do Not Disturb” sign.
- In plain terms: Even a brief interruption lasting 2.8 seconds on average doubles the chance of errors in subsequent steps of a sequential task. An interruption of 4.4 seconds triples that error rate. Field observations also show that it takes an average of 25 minutes to return to the interrupted task, with two other unrelated tasks squeezed in during that time. People who get interrupted do finish tasks faster, but they report higher levels of stress, frustration, effort, and time pressure.
- Benefit: On average, a 2.8-second interruption doubles the error rate for sequential tasks, while a 4.4-second interruption triples it. This finding comes from lab experiments. Another study observed 24 office workers (information professionals) in real-world settings. They switched topics every 11 minutes on average, and 57% of those topic switches were interrupted. It took them 25 minutes and 26 seconds on average to get back to the interrupted task, with 2.26 other tasks added in between. A separate experiment with 48 participants showed similar results: interrupted workers completed tasks faster, but reported significantly higher stress, frustration, effort, and time pressure.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources:Altmann, Trafton & Hambrick (2014). Momentary interruptions can derail the train of thought. Journal of Experimental Psychology: General. <https://doi.org/10.1037/a0030986>；Mark, Gonzalez & Harris (2005). No task left behind? Examining the nature of fragmented work. CHI 2005. <https://doi.org/10.1145/1054972.1055017>；Mark, Gudith & Klocke (2008). The cost of interrupted work: More speed and stress. CHI 2008. <https://doi.org/10.1145/1357054.1357072>
- Notes: The field study mentioned above categorized interruptions into two types: those caused by others approaching the worker, and those initiated by the worker themselves. Simply closing the door blocks only the first type of interruption. To handle the second type, combine this tip with Tip 1 (turn off non-essential notifications) and Tip 5 (batch-process messages).

### 7. Do one thing at a time — don’t reply to messages while in a meeting
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: No cost at all. It does take a bit of willpower to resist replying to messages during a meeting.
- In plain terms: As soon as you switch tasks, reactions slow and mistakes become more likely. People who often use several kinds of media at once have not trained themselves to do several things at once; they do worse on task-switching tests.
- Benefit: Switching between tasks clearly slows reaction time and raises the chance of mistakes. This conclusion comes from a review of multiple studies. One lab study involved university students. Those who frequently use several types of media at the same time are known as heavy media multitaskers. They’re more easily distracted by unrelated things and unrelated memories, and they also perform worse on task-switching tests.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources:Monsell (2003). Task switching. Trends in Cognitive Sciences. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S1364-6613(03)00028-7>；Ophir, Nass & Wagner (2009). Cognitive control in media multitaskers. PNAS. <https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0903620106>
- Notes: Exactly how much extra effort task-switching requires varies a lot depending on the task, so no single number can be given here. The Ophir study compares people at one single point in time, so it’s hard to tell whether multitasking makes attention worse, or whether people with poorer attention skills simply prefer to multitask.

### 8. Put away glowing screens an hour before bed; if you read, choose paper books or an E-ink screen with the front light off
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=是 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Free. Putting your phone down an hour before bed means fighting long-term habit, which takes willpower.
- In plain terms: In a lab trial, reading on an iPad at maximum brightness for about 4 hours before bed delayed falling asleep by about 10 minutes compared with reading a paper book. The sleep hormone melatonin was suppressed by 55%. The next day, the body clock shifted later by more than 1.5 hours, leaving people sleepier in the morning and taking longer to wake up.
- Benefit: In a laboratory crossover trial, the same participants took part in both conditions. In one condition, they read an e-book on an iPad set to maximum brightness for about 4 hours before bed for 5 consecutive nights. In the other, they read a printed book in the same dimly lit room. Reading on the glowing screen delayed falling asleep by about 10 minutes compared with a printed book (25.7 minutes vs 15.8 minutes). Evening melatonin levels were suppressed by 55%. The next day's circadian rhythm was delayed by more than 1.5 hours, with melatonin onset shifting from 21:01 to 22:31. Participants were sleepier the next morning and took longer to become fully alert.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources:Chang, Aeschbach, Duffy & Czeisler (2015). Evening use of light-emitting eReaders negatively affects sleep, circadian timing, and next-morning alertness. PNAS. <https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1418490112>
- Notes: The sample size was small, and the trial was conducted under tightly controlled laboratory conditions. At everyday phone brightness and screen times, the effect may not be as pronounced. The study did not test how helpful night mode is, nor did it test E-ink screens. An E-ink screen with its front light turned off relies on ambient room light just like a printed book, so grouping it with paper is an extrapolation from this study. An E-ink screen with its front light on still emits light, for which there are no data here.

### 9. Go to sleep on schedule without staying up for games, short videos, or pornography
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=是 收益=大 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Free. The hard part is constantly fighting the impulse to watch "just one more." Leaving your phone out of the bedroom takes the least effort.
- In plain terms: In 2025, people in China slept an average of 6.97 hours a night and fell asleep at 0:10 on average. Children and teenagers who use phones or tablets before bed are noticeably more likely to fall short on sleep and feel sleepy during the day. Keeping the devices in the bedroom without using them has almost the same effect. If you want to change this, the easiest fix is keeping your phone out of the bedroom.
- Benefit: In 2025, residents in China slept an average of 6.97 hours at night and went to sleep at 0:10 on average (Chinese Sleep Research Society). For people using portable screen devices before bedtime, the odds ratio for inadequate sleep was 2.17 (OR 2.17). (The figures in this section are odds ratios and cannot be directly interpreted as relative risk multipliers.) The odds ratio for poor sleep quality was 1.46 (OR 1.46), and for excessive daytime sleepiness it was 2.72 (OR 2.72). Even when devices were simply kept in the bedroom without being used, the odds ratios were similar: 1.79, 1.53, and 2.27 (OR 1.79/1.53/2.27), respectively. This meta-analysis pooled 20 cross-sectional studies covering 125,198 children and adolescents. Another cross-sectional study of 844 adults aged 18 to 94 found that those who used mobile phones after lights out took longer to fall asleep, had lower sleep efficiency while in bed, and experienced greater daytime fatigue.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources:Carter et al. (2016). Association Between Portable Screen-Based Media Device Access or Use and Sleep Outcomes. JAMA Pediatrics. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapediatrics.2016.2341>；Exelmans & Van den Bulck (2016). Bedtime mobile phone use and sleep in adults. Social Science & Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.socscimed.2015.11.037>；中国睡眠研究会 (2026). 2026 中国睡眠健康研究白皮书（福建省卫健委转载健康报报道）. <https://web.archive.org/web/20261001203506/https://wjw.fujian.gov.cn/jggk/csxx/xcc/mtbd/202603/t20260323_7114382.htm>（原页面已下线，链接为 2026 年 10 月 1 日的网页存档）；Grubbs JB 等 (2018). Pornography Problems Due to Moral Incongruence: An Integrative Model with a Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis. Archives of Sexual Behavior. <https://doi.org/10.1007/s10508-018-1248-x>（备注里的争议方之一）；Prause N, Pfaus J (2015). Viewing Sexual Stimuli Associated with Greater Sexual Responsiveness, Not Erectile Dysfunction. Sexual Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.1002/sm2.58>（备注里的争议方之二）
- Notes: Controversial. For the costs of staying up late itself, see item 3 of this chapter (getting 7 to 8 hours of sleep per night). There is currently no reliable evidence showing whether pornography directly impairs energy or brain function in ordinary people. A meta-analysis suggested that perceived "pornography problems" stem more from moral incongruence between a person's beliefs and their actual behavior than from the content itself. Another study pooling data from 280 men found that viewing time was not associated with erectile dysfunction during partnered sex. The recommendation here focuses solely on how it displaces sleep time. The figures of 6.97 hours and 0:10 had no official press release on the Chinese Sleep Research Society website as of September 2026; they are cited here based on a Health News report reposted by the Fujian Provincial Health Commission.

### 10. Don’t make major decisions or send important messages late at night
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=小 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: No cost at all. You simply postpone these tasks until after you’ve woken up.
- In plain terms: When well slept, people quickly learn to avoid high-risk options. After two nights in a row with no sleep, they keep picking the high-risk ones instead; the older they are, the clearer this is. How much a few hours less sleep matter was not measured directly, but important decisions should still wait until after you wake.
- Benefit: Thirty-four healthy participants completed the Iowa Gambling Task after going 49.5 hours without sleep. This task is a standard way to measure risk decision making. When well-rested, they quickly learned to steer clear of high-risk options; after sleep loss, they opted for high-risk choices far more often. Older participants showed a stronger response to this effect. This is the only study of its kind to date. The total number of participants was 34.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources:Killgore, Balkin & Wesensten (2006). Impaired decision making following 49 h of sleep deprivation. Journal of Sleep Research. <https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2869.2006.00487.x>
- Notes: The research focused on the extreme case of two nights of continuous sleep deprivation. There is no direct data on how ordinary people fare after staying awake until 2 a.m. The advice to avoid sending messages late at night is based on the authors’ personal experience (rated as Grade C evidence); it is perfectly fine to send such messages the following day instead.

### 11. Take a 10‑minute nap in the afternoon — don’t sleep half an hour  
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: It costs nothing. Just set aside 10 minutes and find a place where you can lie down or recline.  
- In plain terms: When the previous night was short, a 10-minute nap lifts alertness right away; some of that effect lasts about two and a half hours. A 5-minute nap is basically useless. A 20-minute nap only shows benefit after 35 minutes. After a 30-minute nap you first feel groggy on waking; for that stretch performance is worse than not napping.  
- Benefit: After a night of insufficient sleep, a 10‑minute nap quickly reduces drowsiness, fatigue, and improves energy and mental performance; some of these effects persist for up to 155 minutes. A 5‑minute nap yields virtually no benefit, and the advantages of a 20‑minute nap appear only after 35 minutes. A 30‑minute nap first causes sleep inertia, so performance is actually poorer right after waking, before any improvement occurs.  
- Evidence grade: B  
- Sources:Brooks & Lack (2006). A brief afternoon nap following nocturnal sleep restriction: Which nap duration is most recuperative? Sleep. <https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/29.6.831>
- Notes: Only one laboratory study exists, and its participants were healthy young adults. If you can’t fall asleep, there’s no need to force it; there’s no research showing any benefit from simply lying down with eyes closed for 10 minutes. Naps longer than an hour have been linked to higher mortality and increased risk of coronary heart disease — see Section 2, Item 38 (keep naps under 30 minutes). 〔37〕

### 12. Using earplugs or finding a quiet room for memory-heavy tasks in open offices
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=少 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=小 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Earplugs cost just a few dozen yuan. Booking a quiet meeting room takes some time and effort.
- In plain terms: When background noise rises from 39 dB to 51 dB, people remember fewer words, feel more tired, and are less motivated to work. Watching nature videos with sound during breaks helps restore energy far better than continuing to endure office noise.
- Benefit: Students working in simulated open offices showed similar effects: at 51 dB LAeq background noise, they recalled fewer words, rated themselves as more fatigued, and felt less inclined to keep working. Viewing nature videos with audio during breaks also boosted their energy levels more effectively than listening to river sounds or office noise alone.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources:Jahncke, Hygge, Halin, Green & Dimberg (2011). Open-plan office noise: Cognitive performance and restoration. Journal of Environmental Psychology. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jenvp.2011.07.002>
- Notes: Only one laboratory study exists on this topic. Among all noise types, speech that can be clearly understood has the greatest negative impact on performance. White noise headphones may help mitigate this effect, but they were not tested in this research.

### 13. Don’t keep adding to your weekly hours: past about 49 hours, each extra hour yields less and less
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=少 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=大 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: You might earn less in overtime pay. You may also need to put in effort to negotiate with your supervisor.
- In plain terms: Records from British munitions factories during World War I show that when workers kept weekly hours under 49, output rose in direct proportion to the time spent working. Once they exceeded 49 hours, the growth rate began to drop. Output from workers putting in 70 hours per week was practically identical to that of those working 56 hours — those extra 14 hours yielded no additional results. In total, this data covers a period spanning 100 years.
- Benefit: Data from British munitions factories in World War I demonstrates that output rises steadily with working hours up to 49 per week. After that threshold, the rate of growth slows. Peak output is reached at roughly 63 hours. At 70 hours, output remains essentially the same as at 56 hours, meaning those extra 14 hours are wasted effort.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources:Pencavel (2015). The Productivity of Working Hours. The Economic Journal. <https://doi.org/10.1111/ecoj.12166>
- Notes: These figures come from factories a century ago where most workers were women performing manual labor. This threshold may differ for cognitive work; the authors themselves note that other occupations could have higher or lower limits. What is reliable, though, is the overall trend: beyond a certain point, adding more hours yields almost no extra output. The specific figure of 49 hours is not proven.

### 14. When you notice yourself endlessly replaying a distressing memory, do something hands‑on instead
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: No cost at all. The hard part is first recognizing that you’re stuck in this loop – a skill that takes practice.
- In plain terms: Replaying the same bad episode deepens depression, nails negative thoughts in place, makes problems harder to solve, and makes real action harder. It also wears out people around you who would listen. When you notice you are looping in place, go do something that needs your hands.
- Benefit: A review of multiple studies shows that repetitive rumination on distressing events worsens depression. This pattern, known as rumination, also strengthens negative thinking and weakens problem‑solving abilities. It hampers real‑world action and can alienate supportive people. It is linked to anxiety, binge eating, alcohol misuse, and self‑harm.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources:Nolen-Hoeksema, Wisco & Lyubomirsky (2008). Rethinking Rumination. Perspectives on Psychological Science. <https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-6924.2008.00088.x>
- Notes: This is the hardest item in this section to quantify; the review only provides general direction. “Doing something else” reflects the author’s own approach. The literature supports one core idea: diverting attention is better than staying trapped in rumination. No research has compared which specific activities work best.

### 15. Treat thoughts like "things will definitely get worse" as symptoms, not facts
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: Free. The hard part is treating these thoughts as symptoms rather than facts.
- In plain terms: In a twin study that followed participants for 20 years, higher pessimism scores were tied to a higher risk of death. For each step up on the scale, the risk of dying was about 13% higher, and the risk of dying from cardiovascular disease was about 20% higher. Conversely, scoring high on optimism brought no matching benefit. The goal is to address pessimism, not worry about "not being positive enough."
- Benefit: In an Australian study of 2,978 twins aged 50 and older followed for an average of 20 years, 1,068 died during follow-up. Each 1 standard deviation increase on the pessimism scale was associated with an approximately 13% higher risk of death (hazard ratio 1.134, 95% CI 1.065–1.207, P=8.85×10⁻⁵). Cardiovascular mortality was about 20% higher (1.196, 1.045–1.368, P=0.0093). There was no association with cancer deaths. Optimism scores also showed no significant correlation with mortality.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources:Whitfield JB, Zhu G, Landers JG, Martin NG (2020). Pessimism is associated with greater all-cause and cardiovascular mortality, but optimism is not protective. Scientific Reports, 10, 12609. <https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-020-69388-y>
- Notes: Controversial. This was an observational study with no intervention groups. One might argue that underlying illness causes pessimism rather than the reverse; the authors countered that if so, cancer mortality should also have shown a link. Even so, unmeasured factors could be at play. Because optimism showed no protective effect, there is no need to force yourself to "think positively." The key is to stop treating pessimistic expectations as accurate predictions of the future.

### 16. Cut back on draining relationships and learn to say no to unwanted requests
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=是 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: No monetary cost. In the short term, this may cause social friction and possibly lead to losing some relationships. It also takes considerable willpower to actually say no.
- In plain terms: In a survey of widowed women aged 60 to 89, unpleasant contacts linked to well-being more strongly and more consistently than pleasant ones. So cut first, then add.
- Benefit: A survey of 120 widowed women aged 60–89 found that unpleasant interactions showed a stronger and more consistent link to lower psychological well‑being than pleasant ones. In other words, the negative impact of a draining person outweighs the positive effects of a supportive one.
- Evidence grade: C
- Sources:Rook (1984). The negative side of social interaction: Impact on psychological well-being. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology. <https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.46.5.1097>
- Notes: The study group is quite specific, and the questionnaire was administered only once, so no cause‑and‑effect sequence can be determined. It measures overall happiness rather than energy levels. The author includes this example as a speculative inference. There is no objective definition of what counts as a “draining” relationship; each person must judge that for themselves.

### 17. Walk away when angry, and treat others like weather rather than enemies
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Free. It takes practice to bite your tongue on the spot, turn and walk away, and decide later whether to address it.
- In plain terms: Looking at what just happened from another angle or putting yourself in the other person's shoes can both cool down your temper, and seeing it from their perspective works even better. Simply holding back outward facial expressions or reactions also helps a bit. But forcibly swallowing your inner feelings or trying to push the thought out of your mind does not work. People who punish an offender end up brooding over the incident longer afterward, while those who do not retaliate move on faster.
- Benefit: Combining 306 experimental comparisons (meta-analysis): reinterpreting what happened (cognitive reappraisal) produced an emotional effect size of d = 0.36. Taking the other person's perspective yielded d = 0.45. Distraction yielded d = 0.27. Suppressing outward emotional expression (not showing it) was effective, d = 0.32. Forcibly suppressing inner feelings was ineffective (d = -0.04), and trying to push the thought out of mind was also ineffective (d = -0.12). These figures combine subjective feelings, outward behavior, and physiological responses; the study abstract did not break them down separately. Three other experiments showed that people who punished an offender spent longer ruminating on them afterward, whereas those who did not punish moved on faster. People routinely overestimate beforehand how satisfying revenge will feel.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources:Webb TL, Miles E, Sheeran P (2012). Dealing with feeling: A meta-analysis of the effectiveness of strategies derived from the process model of emotion regulation. Psychological Bulletin. <https://doi.org/10.1037/a0027600>；Carlsmith KM, Wilson TD, Gilbert DT (2008). The paradoxical consequences of revenge. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology. <https://doi.org/10.1037/a0012165>
- Notes: The multi-study synthesis measured emotional intensity rather than physical stamina, with small-to-moderate effects. The revenge portion rests on only one set of experiments, hence grade B. The "empty boat" is an allegory from Zhuangzi's *Mountain Tree*: if an empty boat bumps into yours, you do not get angry, but if there is someone in it, you shout—even though the bump is the exact same impact. The title's phrase "treat them like weather" means the same thing: rain does not fall just to ruin your day, so do not take the other person's slight as a personal attack. This is an analogy, not clinical evidence. If there are real damages, pursue the legal paths in Sections 8 and 9, which cost far less than lashing out in the moment.

### 18. When mood is low, do the highest-value moves first: get moving, get more light, sleep on schedule, talk to someone, call 12356
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=中 毅力=些 收益=大 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: No money, or very little. A few brisk walks, jogs, or strength sessions a week. Go outdoors in daylight. Fix the schedule. When you cannot see a way through, make one call.
- In plain terms: Brisk walking, jogging, yoga, and strength training all ease depression; higher intensity works better. Details are in Section 22, Item 6 (when mood is bad, walk or run). Light therapy is strong for seasonal depression and also helps non-seasonal depression. Online self-help cognitive behavioral courses work; when symptoms are heavier, guided versions work better. When you cannot see a way through, call 12356 first.
- Benefit: Pooling 218 randomized controlled trials and 14170 people (network meta-analysis). The comparison group was people doing other activities. Versus them, walking or jogging had an effect size on depression of g = -0.62 (95% confidence interval -0.80 to -0.45). Yoga -0.55, strength training -0.49. Higher intensity worked better. Pooling several light-therapy studies: seasonal depression effect size 0.84 (0.60 to 1.08), non-seasonal 0.53 (0.18 to 0.89). Another analysis pooled individual-patient data across studies. It looked at internet cognitive behavioral therapy (online self-help courses). Guided and unguided both beat controls. For moderate to severe (PHQ-9 above 9), guided was better. 12356 is the national mental-health assistance hotline, open no fewer than 18 hours a day.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources:Noetel M 等 (2024). Effect of exercise for depression: systematic review and network meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials. BMJ. <https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2023-075847>；Golden RN 等 (2005). The efficacy of light therapy in the treatment of mood disorders: a review and meta-analysis of the evidence. American Journal of Psychiatry. <https://doi.org/10.1176/appi.ajp.162.4.656>；Karyotaki E 等 (2021). Internet-Based Cognitive Behavioral Therapy for Depression: A Systematic Review and Individual Patient Data Network Meta-analysis. JAMA Psychiatry. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2020.4364>；国家卫生健康委 (2024). 关于应用"12356"全国统一心理援助热线电话号码的通知（国卫医政函〔2024〕259 号）. <https://www.gov.cn/zhengce/zhengceku/202412/content_6994470.htm>
- Notes: These are adjuncts for mild to moderate depression. Moderate to severe cases and anyone with suicidal thoughts should seek care; call 12356 first (see Section 1, Item 25). Exercise trials are often small and cannot blind who is exercising the way drug trials do. Light-therapy research only runs to 2003 and samples are small. Those studies used dedicated light boxes, not sunlight. Swapping that for daytime outdoor light is an inference from light therapy. “Sleep on schedule” and “talk to someone” are author experience with no separate citation. Do not treat “get moving” as a demand on someone with depression; ten minutes of walking counts.

### 19. Treat police, doctors, and bank tellers as people who work by rules, not as roles: what moves a case is paperwork and deadlines, not emotion
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=小 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: No money. What changes is your expectations and the order of moves. The first time, hold back the urge to “argue until they agree.”
- In plain terms: Police, doctors, and bank tellers are people who work by post rules, bound by paperwork, deadlines, and oversight. How many days a matter should take, and what document you get if it cannot be done, are written in the rules. So take receipts, note deadlines, and appeal through channels; do not try to argue until they agree with you.
- Benefit: Change how you deal with institutions: from “pleading, getting angry, retelling grievances,” to “taking documents, noting deadlines, appealing through channels.” What you save is time spent in repeat talk and rumination. This is a one-time mindset shift, not daily time saved. What you can check against are the deadlines themselves, all written in the files. For public-security cases, counting from the day accepted, the handling period is no more than 30 days; for major or complex cases, with approval from the public-security organ one level up, it may extend 30 days. When a case with a complainant is not filed, the notice of non-filing must be delivered within 3 days. The complainant may apply for reconsideration within 7 days; the public-security organ decides within 30 days. If still unsatisfied, apply for review one level up within another 7 days.
- Evidence grade: C
- Sources:公安部 (2020). 公安机关办理刑事案件程序规定（公安部令第 159 号修正后，第一百七十一、一百七十八、一百七十九条）. <http://www.gov.cn/gongbao/content/2020/content_5549884.htm>；公安部 (2013/2019 修正). 公安机关办理行政案件程序规定（公安部令第 125 号、第 149 号）. <http://www.gov.cn/gongbao/content/2013/content_2321117.htm>
- Notes: Grade C because the judgment that “many people treat the person behind the window as a role, not as someone at work” has no literature to cite. What can be cited is only the rules half: deadlines, documents, and relief channels are all in writing. Every figure here comes from the two Ministry of Public Security regulations in Section 8, Item 39 (procedure after calling the police). Following process does not fix “bad attitude,” and does not guarantee the outcome; it only changes your expectations and first move. Bank tellers and government windows are the same: their actions leave a trail, need review, and errors bring accountability. So keep stubs, ask for receipts, complain through channels; do not guess from “will they cheat me.” Lawful priorities are written in the open; for example emergency care is graded by severity, not first-come-first-served (Section 24, Item 7, emergency triage). To thank a doctor who helped you, use a thank-you letter and satisfaction rating; see Section 24, Item 11. Wanting to “give something so they try harder” toward case handlers is bribery, and the law treats it as aggravated; see Section 8, Item 40. Clear differential treatment is something to complain about and supervise, not an unspoken rule to copy.

### 20. Don’t make “how others are doing” a daily habit: set limits on apps that show peers’ updates or turn them off
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=是 收益=大 口径=时间 -->

- Cost: This costs nothing. Setting time limits or deactivating an account takes just a few minutes. The real challenge is resisting the urge to open these apps again later on.

- In plain terms: People who habitually compare themselves to others online tend to feel worse emotionally and have lower self‑esteem. Meta‑analysis of over 36,000 participants shows a moderate correlation between upward social comparison and poorer psychological outcomes. Correlation does not equal causation, but randomized trials back this link: after four weeks without Facebook, participants reported a slight rise in happiness. Another study limited three major platforms to 10 minutes per day; after three weeks, loneliness and depression both declined. The trade‑off? Knowledge of current events also dropped during those four weeks of abstinence.

- Benefit: The combined results of 54 independent studies — 94 effect sizes and a total N of 36,583 — analyzed via a three‑level random‑effects model reveal that upward social comparison correlates with poorer mental health (average r = 0.330, 95% CI 0.289–0.370). Specific effects include higher fear of negative evaluation (r = 0.438), anxiety (0.382), depression (0.306), lower happiness (0.268) and lower self‑esteem (0.263). Life satisfaction increased by 0.12 standard deviations, while happiness rose by 0.08 standard deviations. Age, cultural background and data collection year do not alter this link. In a randomized trial of 2,743 U.S. Facebook users, 61% would accept a four‑week suspension at a cost of under $102; those in the suspension group gained 0.09 standard deviations in overall life satisfaction. Compared with typical psychological interventions (which raise happiness by ~0.34 SD and cut depression by ~0.23 SD), this effect is roughly 25–40% of that magnitude. After the trial ended, former suspension users still spent 0.31 fewer standard deviations per day on Facebook. Even after the trial ended, former suspension users still spent 12 fewer minutes per day on Facebook. A smaller trial of 143 college students showed similar benefits after limiting three platforms to 10 minutes daily. Additionally, knowledge of current affairs declined by 0.19 standard deviations, and political polarization fell by 0.16 standard deviations as a result of reduced app usage.

- Evidence grade: A
- Sources:Lei Y, Hu S, Sun Y, Zheng L (2026). "Looking up" linked to feeling down: a meta-analysis of online upward social comparison and psychological maladjustment. Frontiers in Psychology. <https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1825169>；Allcott H, Braghieri L, Eichmeyer S, Gentzkow M (2020). The Welfare Effects of Social Media. American Economic Review, 110(3), 629–676. <https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20190658>；Allcott H, Braghieri L, Eichmeyer S, Gentzkow M (2019). The Welfare Effects of Social Media. NBER Working Paper No. 25514（本条引用的具体数字按这一版逐字核对，刊出版的摘要不含数字）. <https://www.nber.org/papers/w25514>；Hunt MG, Marx R, Lipson C, Young J (2018). No More FOMO: Limiting Social Media Decreases Loneliness and Depression. Journal of Social and Clinical Psychology, 37(10), 751–768. <https://doi.org/10.1521/jscp.2018.37.10.751>

- Notes: The correlation findings merely indicate that these patterns tend to appear together; they do not prove causation. The randomized trials do suggest causal links, but they involve U.S. participants prior to the 2018 midterm elections, so applying the results directly to Chinese users is a stretch. The undergraduate trial involved only 143 subjects, limiting its statistical power. Overall effect sizes remain modest, so cutting off these apps alone will not resolve all emotional issues. This advice targets usage aimed at monitoring how others are faring; it does not apply to staying in touch with family, seeking information or job hunting. If strict limits prove hard to maintain, start by disabling push notifications, “people you may know” suggestions and homepage recommendations. For broader screen‑time management, see sections 4.15 and 4.16 (hard caps on short‑video apps and scrolling news). When feeling down, consider the actions outlined in section 19 (exercise, sunlight and regular sleep). For those seeking identity through purchases, see section 6.23; those striving to “move up a social rung” should review section 6.24.

### 21. When you feel “everyone saw me make a fool of myself,” cut that estimate in half
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=小 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: No money. Just ask yourself one extra question when tense: did they really see it?
- In plain terms: Walk in wearing an awkward T-shirt and you think nearly half the people can tell who is printed on it; actually fewer than a quarter can. Switch to a decent T-shirt and you still overestimate the same way. After a group discussion, people also think their verbal mistakes look more obvious to others than they do, and after a chat they often underestimate how much the other person liked them. So when you feel “everyone saw me look foolish,” it is often only half or less.
- Benefit: Experiment 1: Participants wore a T-shirt with Barry Manilow’s face and walked into a room with other participants. Wearers estimated on average that 46% of bystanders could correctly name who was on the shirt. Bystanders’ actual correct rate was 23%; the estimate was exactly twice the actual. Subtracting actual from estimate room by room, the average overestimate was 23 percentage points (95% CI 9–38). After dropping the two rooms with only two or three bystanders, it was 27 percentage points (11–43). Experiment 2: Switch to a self-chosen T-shirt the wearer saw as positive. Average estimate 48%, bystanders’ actual correct rate 8%, a sixfold gap. In a footnote the authors note wearers’ estimates were almost the same across both experiments (48% and 46%); the gap was bystander accuracy (8% and 23%). They suspect the main reason is that Experiment 2’s bystander questionnaire was harder, not that a decent shirt is easier to overestimate. Experiment 3: Undergraduates in groups of 3 to 7 discussed an assigned topic for 20 minutes, then wrote a policy proposal together. Right after, each person separately estimated how others in the group would rank everyone; lower rank numbers are better. On “how many verbal mistakes,” self-estimated rank averaged 2.30, others’ actual rank 2.96, difference −0.66 (p < .01). So people think their mistakes look more obvious to others. “Possibly offensive remarks” was the same (2.35 versus 2.90, p < .01); “remarks others would pick on” differed less (2.61 versus 2.82, p < .05). Another series had three settings: strangers paired for talk, new college roommates, and an adult workshop. People systematically underestimated how much the other liked them and how much the other enjoyed the talk. The authors call this the “liking gap.” It shows up in talks of different lengths and lasts months among new roommates.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources:Gilovich T, Medvec VH, Savitsky K (2000). The spotlight effect in social judgment: An egocentric bias in estimates of the salience of one's own actions and appearance. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 78(2), 211–222. <https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.78.2.211>；Boothby EJ, Cooney G, Sandstrom GM, Clark MS (2018). The Liking Gap in Conversations: Do People Like Us More Than We Think? Psychological Science, 29(11), 1742–1756. <https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797618783714>
- Notes: Grade B because these are small lab and campus studies. Settings are wearing a shirt, chatting with strangers or roommates, and student group discussion asked right after. There is no direct evidence for “colleagues gossiping” or “a meeting mistake still remembered days later.” The figures only show the direction and rough multiple of overestimate; they are not a coefficient to plug in. Others’ views are not all unimportant. In real evaluation settings — interviews, defenses, assessments, medical reports — the evaluation is real; prepare when you should. What to discount is only the everyday sense that “everyone is watching me”; that part is your own estimation bias.

### 22. Treat “others expect me to be perfect” as a symptom, not as a fact
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=小 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: No money. The hard part is treating this thought as something to handle, not as an accurate reading of what others really demand.
- In plain terms: People who keep feeling “people around me demand I be perfect, and if I fall short I will be rejected” are more likely to have suicidal thoughts. “I demand perfection of myself” links much more weakly to suicidal thoughts, only about one third as strong as the first. After subtracting thoughts already present, the first feeling still tracks later rises in thoughts, though the size is modest.
- Benefit: Pooling several studies (meta-analysis): 45 studies, N = 11747, samples of undergraduates, medical students, community adults, and psychiatric patients, random-effects model. Below are weighted average correlations with suicidal thoughts. Perfectionistic concerns r = .28 (95% CI .24–.32; 42 studies, 7936 people). Perfectionistic concerns is a bundle including socially prescribed perfectionism, concern over mistakes, doubts about actions, and gaps between reality and standards. Socially prescribed perfectionism alone is also r = .28 (.25–.32, 30 studies, 3640 people). Perfectionistic striving r = .10 (.07–.13, 31 studies, 4588 people), a bundle of self-oriented perfectionism and personal standards. Parental criticism r = .20 (.11–.29). Then links to suicide attempts. Perfectionistic concerns r = .12 (.07–.17, 15 studies, 5275 people). Socially prescribed perfectionism r = .19 (.08–.29, 8 studies, 689 people). Then the follow-up part. After subtracting suicidal thoughts present at the start, socially prescribed perfectionism still moved in the same direction as later follow-up suicidal thoughts, modestly. Self-oriented and other-oriented perfectionism showed no link.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources:Smith MM, Sherry SB, Chen S, Saklofske DH, Mushquash C, Flett GL, Hewitt PL (2018). The perniciousness of perfectionism: A meta-analytic review of the perfectionism-suicide relationship. Journal of Personality, 86(3), 522–542. <https://doi.org/10.1111/jopy.12333>
- Notes: Grade B because most included studies are one-time questionnaires, so sequence is unclear. Correlation strength is moderate (r about .28), explaining only a few percent to about one tenth of the variance. What is measured is suicidal thoughts and attempts, not death itself, so benefit magnitude is logged as small for “surrogate endpoint only.” The authors also say the perfectionistic-concerns and attempt set looks like publication bias. Publication bias means studies with unflattering results were not published. After filling in estimated missing studies, the direction holds. High standards themselves are not the problem, and you need not lower them for that reason. The difference is where the standard comes from, and whether falling short feels like “I as a person am finished.” Like the pessimistic forecasts in item 15 (treat thoughts like “things will get worse” as a symptom), this is something that can be handled as a symptom. For suicidal thoughts, call 12356 first and move lethal means farther away; see Section 1, Item 25. What you can do when someone close shows such thoughts is in Section 8, Item 15. Depression screening for children is in Section 30, Item 8.

### 23. When angry, don’t try to vent by smashing things, punching a bag, or going for a run — first calm your body down
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=时间 -->

- Cost: No cost at all. When you’re angry, try a few minutes of activities that slow your heart rate: breathe slowly, sit down, and quiet your mind. It’s hard to resist the urge to vent right then and there.

- In plain terms: Punching a bag, cycling, or going for a run — ways of venting that amp the body up — generally do not cool anger. Slow breathing, mindfulness, and meditation — ways that settle the body — clearly cut anger and the urge to strike.

- Benefit: A meta-analysis of 154 studies involving 184 sample groups and 10,189 participants shows that calming techniques like deep breathing, mindfulness, and meditation lower both anger and aggression. The effect size was g = -0.63, with a 95% CI ranging from -0.82 to -0.43. This result held true across genders, races, ages, and cultures. In contrast, activities that increase physical arousal — such as punching a bag, jogging, or cycling — showed virtually no effect; their effect size was g = -0.02, with a 95% CI from -0.13 to 0.09. The authors conclude that neither venting anger nor going for a run is an effective way to defuse it.

- Evidence grade: A
- Sources:Kjærvik SL, Bushman BJ (2024). A meta-analytic review of anger management activities that increase or decrease arousal: What fuels or douses rage? Clinical Psychology Review. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cpr.2024.102414>

- Notes: These findings measure anger and aggression, not overall energy levels. The authors categorize the time spent on calming activities as “minutes saved from dealing with fallout after an argument.” Immediate techniques you can use right now include the “circular sighing” method described in Section 22, Item 9, and “stepping away when angry” in Section 24, Item 18. Running isn’t useless in the long run — it helps ease low mood, as noted in Section 22, Item 7 (“When feeling down, go for a walk or run”). It simply isn’t a good short-term fix for anger. While angry, avoid driving, sending messages, or making important decisions. If someone is physically attacking you, leave the scene and call the police; your priority then is safety, not emotional regulation — see Section 8, Item 43 (“If you’re being abused”).

### 24. When something is weighing on you, you can write thoughts and feelings down for several days in a row, but do not expect it to cure disease
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=小 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: No money. A common study setup is writing 3 to 4 times, 15 to 20 minutes each. Write about something that hurts, and write your deepest thoughts and feelings.
- In plain terms: Writing down a bad experience has a small average benefit. Pooled conclusions also disagree; two pooled analyses found no clear effect. It costs almost nothing, so you can try it. When emotional problems are heavy, do not use it instead of seeing a doctor.
- Benefit: One analysis pooled 146 randomized studies (meta-analysis). People wrote or spoke about something important to them. The average effect was positive and significant, but small, effect size r = .075. Another pool took only randomized controlled trials; 30 trials together showed no significant effect on physical or mental health. Sixteen randomized trials in people with cancer and survivors, pooled, were non-significant on psychological, physical, and quality-of-life outcomes. On the psychological side g = 0.04, 95% CI -0.06 to 0.14.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources:Frattaroli J (2006). Experimental disclosure and its moderators: a meta-analysis. Psychological Bulletin. <https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.132.6.823>；Mogk C, Otte S, Reinhold-Hurley B, Kröner-Herwig B (2006). Health effects of expressive writing on stressful or traumatic experiences - a meta-analysis. Psycho-Social Medicine. <https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC2736499/>；Zachariae R, O'Toole MS (2015). The effect of expressive writing intervention on psychological and physical health outcomes in cancer patients--a systematic review and meta-analysis. Psycho-Oncology. <https://doi.org/10.1002/pon.3802>
- Notes: Contested. Support comes from the 146-study pool, with a positive but small effect. Opposition comes from two pools limited to randomized controlled trials, neither finding a significant effect. How many times and how long per session in the Cost line come from the earliest experimental design, noted in the abstract of Mogk’s pool. It is included because it costs almost no money and little time, and one person can do it alone. When you do not want to talk or have no one to talk to, you can use it. What is measured is not energy; calling the frame “time” is the author’s classification. If writing makes you feel worse and worse, stop and call 12356; see Section 1, Item 25 (12356).

### 25. Take painkillers like ibuprofen for menstrual cramps instead of toughing it out; see a gynecologist if pain disrupts work or school or persists after three months of medication
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=少 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=时间 人群=女性 -->
- Cost: A box of ibuprofen costs a few dollars, and you only take it during the painful days. If you need to see a gynecologist, an appointment and an ultrasound cost some money and take half a day.
- In plain terms: For menstrual cramps, taking anti-inflammatory painkillers like ibuprofen increases the proportion of women with noticeable relief from under 20% to about half. They work better than acetaminophen, though with slightly more side effects. If pain disrupts work or school, causes deep pain during sex, or cannot be controlled after three months of medication, see a gynecologist to check for endometriosis. In a 10-country study, diagnosing this condition took an average of 6.7 years from the first symptoms.
- Benefit: A Cochrane systematic review pooled 80 randomized controlled trials involving 5,820 women with primary dysmenorrhea (menstrual pain without an identified underlying disease). Compared with placebo, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) provided superior pain relief (OR 4.37, 95% CI 3.76–5.09). In absolute terms, 18% of those taking placebo achieved moderate or greater relief, compared with 45% to 53% on NSAIDs. The trade-off was slightly more side effects (OR 1.29, 95% CI 1.11–1.51): 10% on placebo experienced side effects compared with 11% to 14% on NSAIDs, primarily gastrointestinal discomfort (OR 1.58) and neurological reactions (OR 2.74). Compared with acetaminophen, NSAIDs were also more effective for pain relief (OR 1.89, 95% CI 1.05–3.43), though based on only 3 trials. No clear difference in efficacy was found among different individual NSAIDs. The UK NICE guideline (updated in 2024) lists circumstances that warrant suspicion of endometriosis: chronic pelvic pain, period pain affecting daily activities and quality of life, deep pain during or after sexual intercourse, painful bowel movements during periods, blood in the urine or painful urination during periods, and subfertility associated with any of these symptoms. This includes girls aged 17 and under. The guideline recommends an initial trial of acetaminophen or an NSAID, such as for 3 months; if pain relief is inadequate, refer for further investigation. Normal pelvic examination and ultrasound findings do not rule out endometriosis. A study across 16 hospitals in 10 countries investigated 1,418 women undergoing laparoscopy: the average delay from symptom onset to surgical confirmation of endometriosis was 6.7 years, with most of the delay occurring in primary care. Women diagnosed lost an average of 10.8 hours of work per week, mostly due to reduced productivity while working (presenteeism). An online survey in the Netherlands surveyed 32,748 women aged 15 to 45: 13.8% had taken time off work or school due to menstruation, averaging 1.3 days per year; 80.7% reported reduced productivity while working through periods, equivalent to 8.9 lost workdays per year.
- Evidence grade: A

Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, (7):CD001751. <https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD001751.pub3>；National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (2017, updated 2024). Endometriosis: diagnosis and management (NG73), recommendations 1.3.1, 1.4.2, 1.4.3, 1.5.4. <https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng73/chapter/Recommendations>；Nnoaham KE, Hummelshoj L, Webster P, et al.; World Endometriosis Research Foundation Global Study of Women's Health consortium (2011). Impact of endometriosis on quality of life and work productivity: a multicenter study across ten countries. ### 26. Try cognitive behavioral therapy first for chronic insomnia (more than three nights a week for three months or more) instead of starting right away on long-term sleeping pills
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=少 时间=中 毅力=是 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Going to a hospital sleep clinic, psychiatry department, or psychology department requires registration and therapy fees. Online self-help courses are much cheaper. A course typically runs 6 to 8 weeks and requires keeping a daily sleep diary. What you can start on your own is stimulus control: go to bed only when sleepy, and get out of bed if you lie awake unable to sleep. Use the bed only for sleep and sex, get up at the same time every day, and avoid daytime naps. The hardest part is sleep restriction: in the first few weeks, you must cut your time in bed down, which makes you feel sleepier during the day.
- In plain terms: After finishing this therapy, people fall asleep an average of about 19 minutes faster and spend about 26 fewer minutes awake in the middle of the night. The benefits gradually fade, but they are still present after 12 months. Online self-help courses work about as well as in-person sessions. Sleeping pills offer little help for adults over 60: for every 6 people who take them, 1 experiences side effects, and only 1 in 13 sleeps better.
- Benefit: The 2020 diagnostic and treatment guidelines from the National Health Commission outline the key diagnostic criteria for insomnia disorder based on international classifications: first, difficulty falling asleep, frequent nighttime awakenings, or early morning awakening; second, daytime fatigue, sleepiness, and impaired functioning; third, these symptoms occur at least 3 times a week and persist for at least 3 months. Symptoms lasting under 3 months are classified as short-term insomnia disorder. The guidelines designate cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) as the first-line treatment. It comprises five components: sleep restriction, stimulus control, cognitive therapy, relaxation training, and sleep hygiene, typically delivered over 6 to 8 weeks. The guidelines state that its short-term efficacy is comparable to medications, while its long-term outcomes are superior. They also specify that sleeping pills should generally not be taken continuously for more than 4 weeks, with monthly reassessments if continued. The 2016 American College of Physicians guidelines strongly recommend that all adult patients with chronic insomnia receive this therapy first; if it alone is insufficient, discuss with a doctor whether to add short-term medication. One analysis pooled 20 randomized controlled trials with 1,162 participants (meta-analysis). Following treatment, sleep onset latency decreased by 19.03 minutes (95% CI 14.12–23.93). Wake after sleep onset decreased by 26.00 minutes (15.48–36.52). Sleep efficiency (the percentage of time in bed actually spent asleep) increased by 9.91% (8.09%–11.73%). Total sleep time increased by only 7.61 minutes, which was not statistically significant (−0.51 to 15.74). None of the trials reported adverse events. Another analysis pooled 30 randomized controlled trials to evaluate how long benefits last. Compared with no treatment, improvements in insomnia severity diminished over time: Hedges' g = 0.64 at 3 months, 0.40 at 6 months, and 0.25 at 12 months. While the effect gradually weakened, the authors concluded it remained clinically meaningful within one year. For online interventions, an analysis pooled 11 randomized controlled trials with 1,460 participants. All sleep metrics improved, with effect sizes (g) ranging from 0.21 to 1.09; the authors noted outcomes were comparable to face-to-face therapy, with larger effects observed in longer courses and programs with human support. Another online randomized trial of 1,711 participants compared digital therapy against receiving sleep hygiene education alone; those receiving digital therapy experienced large improvements in sleep-related quality of life, alongside small improvements in daytime physical and psychological well-being. Looking at sleeping pills: a meta-analysis pooled 24 randomized controlled trials involving 2,417 insomnia patients aged 60 and older, each trial lasting at least 5 consecutive nights. Compared with placebo, medication increased total sleep time by 25.2 minutes and reduced nocturnal awakenings by 0.63 times. For every 13 people treated, 1 experienced improved sleep quality. For every 6 people treated, 1 experienced an adverse event (NNH 6, 4.7 to 7.1). Cognitive and memory-related adverse events were more frequent than with placebo, with an odds ratio of 4.78 (OR 4.78, 1.47–15.47), as was daytime fatigue, with an odds ratio of 3.82 (OR 3.82, 1.88–7.80).

### 27. If you snore loudly, gasp in your sleep, or feel drowsy while driving, get a sleep study for sleep apnea, and use a CPAP machine at night if it is moderate to severe

- Cost: Start by visiting a sleep clinic, pulmonology department, or ENT department. Diagnosis requires an overnight sleep study, either sleeping at the hospital or taking equipment home to wear overnight. If you need a continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) machine, you generally have to buy it yourself, which is a substantial expense. The hardest part is sticking with it: you must sleep wearing a mask every night. Many people find it uncomfortable in the first few weeks and take it off after just a few hours.
- In plain terms: Sleep apnea occurs when breathing repeatedly stops or becomes shallow during sleep, waking you up gasping and leaving you exhausted the next day. Using a CPAP machine noticeably relieves daytime drowsiness and cuts car crashes by about 70%. However, clinical trials have not shown that it prevents heart attacks or lowers mortality—the main reason to use it is to stay alert and functional during the day.
- Benefit: The 2017 American Academy of Sleep Medicine diagnostic guidelines describe who is most likely to have moderate to severe sleep apnea: excessive daytime sleepiness on most days, plus at least two of the following three criteria: frequent loud snoring; witnessed breathing pauses, gasping, or choking during sleep; and diagnosed hypertension. The guidelines require an objective sleep study for diagnosis, rather than questionnaires alone. Having 5 or more breathing pauses or shallow breathing events per hour with symptoms qualifies as sleep apnea; 15 or more events per hour qualifies even without symptoms. A 2019 American Academy of Sleep Medicine systematic review pooled 38 randomized controlled trials: compared with no treatment, CPAP users reduced their self-reported daytime sleepiness by 2.4 points (95% CI 1.9–2.8) on the Epworth Sleepiness Scale (scored 0 to 24, higher scores mean sleepier). Looking only at people who were not sleepy to begin with, 5 trials found a reduction of 1.0 point, which the authors considered clinically insignificant. The same review pooled 10 before-and-after studies: compared with before treatment, the relative risk of motor vehicle crashes after starting CPAP was 0.3 (RR 0.3, 95% CI 0.2–0.4), a drop of about 70%. Another 2010 meta-analysis pooled 9 similar studies and found an RR of 0.278 (0.22–0.35); it noted that daytime sleepiness noticeably improved after just 1 night, and driving simulator performance improved within 2 to 7 days. A 2009 analysis by the same research group estimated that untreated patients have roughly 1.21 to 4.89 times the crash risk of healthy drivers. As for cardiovascular health and longevity: the SAVE trial enrolled 2,717 patients aged 45 to 75 with moderate to severe sleep apnea and established coronary heart disease or prior stroke, following them for an average of 3.7 years after randomization. Major cardiovascular events occurred in 17.0% of the CPAP group and 15.4% of the control group, showing no difference (HR 1.10, 95% CI 0.91–1.32). Patients in the CPAP group used the device for an average of only 3.3 hours per night; snoring, daytime sleepiness, quality of life, and mood all improved. A 2017 meta-analysis pooled 10 randomized trials with 7,266 participants and found no difference in overall mortality (RR 1.13, 95% CI 0.99–1.29). A 2019 modeling study estimated that among Chinese adults aged 30 to 69, approximately 176 million have sleep apnea (23.6%), including roughly 65.52 million with moderate to severe disease (8.8%), the highest absolute number worldwide.
