# 29. After a major blow

Losing a relative, being told of a serious illness, losing a job, or a divorce. In the research these events share a finding: what goes wrong is not only the mood, but the body. The stretch of highest risk sits in the first weeks through the first year. This section does not explain how to "get over it." It only says which things someone has to watch during this stretch, which decisions have to be put off, and when to go and register at a clinic. Most items are scored on the chance of death. The items that talk about spending and about benefits are scored on money. They are: do not start by paying for grief counseling (item 9), call 12356 and register at a mental-health clinic (item 11), push irreversible big decisions back (item 12), and do not treat death as a way to pay a debt (item 13). These kinds of figures are not converted into each other. If there is no relative and no friend to turn to, how "find someone to watch" actually gets done is in item 6. If you yourself went through a traffic crash, an assault, or a disaster and the nightmares and flashbacks are still there, see item 14 (trauma-focused psychological treatment).

The paperwork at the time a relative dies, the body, and the money to collect are in section 25. Guardianship and a will for an elder while alive are in section 17. What you can receive after losing a job is in section 7. Exercise and daylight when mood is low are in section 3 and section 22. If suicidal thoughts appear, call 12356 first; see section 1, item 25. The time scale of the thoughts is in section 1, item 32. Lasting harm after a person is brought back is in section 1, item 33. This section does not repeat any of that.
### 1. Do not stay alone in the first few days after losing a loved one; keep taking meds for heart disease, hypertension, or diabetes; call emergency services for crushing chest pain
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=大 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: Free. Do not stay alone during these days, and keep taking your medication as usual.
- In plain terms: On the first day after losing a loved one, the risk of a heart attack is 21.1 times higher than usual, decreasing day by day afterward. Among people aged 60 to 89, 0.16% suffer a heart attack or stroke within 30 days of losing a partner, compared with 0.08% of their non-bereaved peers. If you feel crushing chest pain, shortness of breath, or sudden weakness in an arm or leg on one side, call emergency services immediately.
- Benefit: In a study of 1,985 patients with acute myocardial infarction, researchers compared each person with themselves across different time windows before and after the event. Of these, 270 (13.6%) had lost a significant person within 6 months before the attack, and 19 had lost someone within 1 day prior. Within 24 hours of losing a loved one, the incidence rate of acute myocardial infarction increased by 21.1 times (95% CI 13.1–34.1, meaning the true increase is roughly between 13 and 34 times). It declined day by day thereafter. In absolute numbers: among people with a 10-year heart attack risk of 5%, there is 1 extra case within a week for every 1,394 newly bereaved individuals. Among those with a 20% risk, there is 1 extra case within a week for every 320 individuals. Another UK study compared 30,447 bereaved individuals aged 60 to 89 with 83,588 matched controls. Within 30 days of bereavement, 50 people (0.16%) in the bereaved group suffered a heart attack or stroke, compared with 67 people (0.08%) in the control group. The incidence rate ratio (IRR) was 2.20 (1.52–3.15), meaning the risk was about 2.2 times that of the controls. Specifically, for myocardial infarction, the IRR was 2.14 (1.20–3.81), or about 2.1 times; for stroke, 2.40 (1.22–4.71), or about 2.4 times. The risk attenuated after 30 days. Two other conditions were examined within 90 days: non-infarction acute coronary syndrome (acute compromised blood supply to the heart falling short of a full heart attack), with an IRR of 2.20 (1.12–4.29); and pulmonary embolism (blood clot blocking a pulmonary artery), with an IRR of 2.37 (1.18–4.75). To recognize these emergencies, the UK National Health Service (NHS) lists warning signs requiring immediate emergency calls. A heart attack typically presents as crushing or squeezing chest pain that may radiate to the arm, neck, or jaw, accompanied by shortness of breath. A stroke presents as one side of the face drooping, weakness or numbness in one arm or side of the body, and slurred speech.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources:Mostofsky E, et al. (2012). Risk of acute myocardial infarction after the death of a significant person in one's life: the Determinants of Myocardial Infarction Onset Study. Circulation. <https://doi.org/10.1161/CIRCULATIONAHA.111.061770>；Carey IM, et al. (2014). Increased risk of acute cardiovascular events after partner bereavement: a matched cohort study. JAMA Internal Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2013.14558>；NHS. Heart attack: symptoms. <https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/heart-attack/symptoms/>；NHS. Symptoms of a stroke. <https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/stroke/symptoms/>
- Notes: Continuing medication as usual for hypertension and diabetes is the author's clinical judgment. Neither study compared taking medication versus stopping it. The study showing the 21.1-fold increase only converted heart attack baseline risks into extra case counts. Hand over your pill organizer to someone else; do not rely on your own memory during grief. Avoid driving long distances during these first few days. Do not rely on alcohol to cope; see Section 2 for the true toll of alcohol. For recognizing heart attacks and strokes and calling an ambulance, see Section 13. Both studies were retrospective population analyses, making it impossible to separate how many people would have fallen ill anyway, but both point in the same direction and time frame. If you have no one to hand your pill organizer to and must stay alone in the house during these days, see Item 6 of this section. 〔120〕 〔120〕

### 2. On the week you receive a cancer diagnosis, don’t go alone to pick up the results — postpone non‑treatment decisions
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=大 口径=死亡率 -->

- Cost: No cost at all. Simply arrange for someone to accompany you when you collect the results, and put any major decisions unrelated to treatment off for a few days.

- In plain terms: In the first week after receiving a cancer diagnosis, the risk of suicide is roughly 12.6 times higher than among people without a diagnosis, while the risk of dying from cardiovascular disease is about 5.6 times higher. By the end of the first year this drops to roughly 3.1 times; the effect is especially pronounced for harder‑to‑treat cancers. The greatest risk exists during those first few days, after which it falls quickly. Therefore, bring a companion when picking up the results and avoid being alone at home. Decisions such as quitting your job or selling your house can wait until your next appointment.

- Benefit: Data from Sweden’s national registers tracked over 6.07 million people (6,073,240 individuals) from 1991 to 2006. Compared with cancer‑free controls, the relative risk of suicide in the first week after diagnosis was 12.6 (95% CI 8.6–17.8), meaning roughly 12.6 times higher. The true value lies somewhere between 8.6 and 17.8. The incidence rate during that week was 2.50 per 1,000 person‑years — about 2.5 cases per 1,000 people each year. By the end of the first year this fell to 3.1 (2.7–3.5; 0.60 per 1,000 person‑years), or roughly 3.1 times higher. Cardiovascular mortality in the first week was 5.6 times higher (5.2–5.9; 116.80 per 1,000 person‑years), and over the first four weeks it remained at 3.3 times higher (3.1–3.4; 65.81 per 1,000 person‑years). This elevated risk declines rapidly within the first year; the effect is more pronounced for cancers with poorer prognoses. Comparisons of the same patients over time yielded consistent results.

- Evidence grade: A
- Sources:Fang F, et al. (2012). Suicide and cardiovascular death after a cancer diagnosis. New England Journal of Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1110307>

- Notes: These figures apply specifically to cancer patients. No comparable data exist for other serious diagnoses. The actual number of people affected remains low: a total of 6,073,240 individuals were included in the study, and the first‑week suicide rate is 2.50 per 1,000 person‑years, i.e., only 2.5 cases per 1,000 people each year. This information is provided not to frighten, but to emphasize the importance of having someone present during those initial days. Long‑term management, insurance coverage, and follow‑up care are discussed in Sections 16 and 24. For those unable to find a companion, see point 6 in this section, which suggests enlisting neighbours or community contacts instead.

### 3. After losing your job, lock in your daily routine, yibao (basic medical insurance), and job search schedule instead of staying home all day
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=大 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: Free. Register as unemployed, claim unemployment benefits, and build a set schedule for your job search. The hard part is waking up on time when nobody is making you.
- In plain terms: Compared to employed people, those who lose their jobs face about a 60% higher risk of dying later on; the difference is most pronounced in men and younger adults, and peaks during the first ten years. Studies that accounted for smoking and drinking found a weaker link, but that does not prove changing your habits will erase the risk. Register as unemployed first and claim your unemployment benefits.
- Benefit: Combined data from 42 studies, 235 mortality risk estimates, and over 20 million people. After adjusting for age and other covariates, the mean hazard ratio (HR) for all-cause mortality among the unemployed was 1.63—about a 60% higher risk of death compared to employed individuals. Men faced higher risk than women. The effect was pronounced in early and mid-career, but smaller in late career. Risk was highest during the first 10 years of follow-up and declined thereafter. In the subset of studies that also controlled for health behaviors like smoking and drinking, the average HR was 24% lower than in studies that did not control for them. This reflects a comparison between study subsets, not evidence that the excess mortality risk drops by 24%, nor does it show how much an individual's risk decreases by changing lifestyle habits.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources:Roelfs DJ, et al. (2011). Losing life and livelihood: a systematic review and meta-analysis of unemployment and all-cause mortality. Social Science & Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.socscimed.2011.01.005>
- Notes: These are retrospective population studies and cannot establish causality; individuals already in poor health are also more likely to lose their jobs. The 24% lower average HR in studies controlling for lifestyle habits cannot be treated as the effect of an individual changing their behavior. Keeping a set routine, maintaining yibao, and avoiding staying home all day are the author's practical advice, not interventions evaluated separately in the studies. For what unemployment benefits you can claim, where to apply, and how to maintain social insurance, see Section 7. For severance pay and why you should avoid signing voluntary resignations, see Section 19.

### 4. Seek professional help if a loved one dies by suicide, accident, or homicide—do not tough it out alone
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=大 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: Calling 12356 is free. For medical care, seeing a specialist at a psychological clinic costs the same as a standard outpatient registration. The hardest part is taking the first step to reach out.
- In plain terms: When a spouse dies by suicide, the surviving partner's chance of being diagnosed with a mental disorder within five years is roughly 80% higher for men and 70% higher for women compared to the general public. Even compared to people whose spouses died of other causes, the risk is still 70% to 100% higher. Those who lose someone this way are also more likely to feel alienated or carry a sense of shame. That is precisely why you should actively reach out for support instead of waiting to heal on your own.
- Benefit: A Danish national registry tracked 6.7 million adults from 1980 to 2014, covering over 136 million person-years in total. Compared with the general population, people bereaved by spousal suicide faced a higher risk of being newly diagnosed with a mental disorder within 5 years: the incidence rate ratio (IRR) was 1.8 (95% CI 1.6–2.0) for men (about 80% higher) and 1.7 (1.6–1.8) for women (about 70% higher). Compared to those whose spouse died of other causes, the IRR was 1.7 (1.5–1.9) for men and 2.0 (1.9–2.2) for women (70% to 100% higher). Within this group, suicidal behavior, mortality, and the need for municipal welfare support were also higher. Physical health risks such as cirrhosis and sleep disorders rose as well. A narrative synthesis of 57 studies (without meta-analytic pooling) linked suicide bereavement to four specific outcomes: increased suicide risk in surviving partners; higher risk of psychiatric hospital admission for parents after a child's suicide; higher suicide risk for mothers after an adult child's suicide; and elevated depression risk for offspring after a parent's suicide. Compared with bereavement from other violent deaths, people bereaved by suicide more frequently reported feelings of alienation and shame. Another review focused specifically on sudden, violent bereavement, including losses from disasters and war: rates of post-traumatic stress disorder, depression, and prolonged grief disorder were all higher than after death from physical illness, and recovery was slower, though reported prevalence rates varied widely across individual studies.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources:Erlangsen A, et al. (2017). Association Between Spousal Suicide and Mental, Physical, and Social Health Outcomes: A Longitudinal and Nationwide Register-Based Study. JAMA Psychiatry. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2017.0226>；Pitman A, et al. (2014). Effects of suicide bereavement on mental health and suicide risk. The Lancet Psychiatry. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S2215-0366(14)70224-X>；Kristensen P 等 (2012). Bereavement and mental health after sudden and violent losses: a review. Psychiatry. <https://doi.org/10.1521/psyc.2012.75.1.76>（备注里那份综述）
- Notes: Sudden, violent deaths refer to accidents, homicides, and disasters. Following these events, rates of post-traumatic stress disorder, depression, and prolonged grief are higher than after death from illness, and recovery takes longer. If nightmares, flashbacks, and avoidance persist after more than a month, see Item 14 in this section (trauma-focused psychotherapy). For managing personal suicidal thoughts, see Section 1, Item 25.

### 5. In the first six months after losing a spouse, have one designated person keep track of meals, sleep, chronic disease medications, and follow-up appointments
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=大 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: Free. Find one specific person—an adult child, sibling, or friend—and have them visit or call regularly. The hardest part is speaking up and asking for this support.
- In plain terms: In the first six months after losing a spouse, the risk of death is about 40% higher than for those who have not lost a partner. After six months, that excess risk drops to about 15%. Men face a steeper rise; for women, one pooled study found no clear difference, while another found the risk was about 15% higher. Put daily medications, meals, and follow-up visits into the hands of one specific person—do not count on remembering them on your own.
- Benefit: In a pooled analysis of 15 prospective cohort studies totaling 2,263,888 people, the relative risk of death within the first 6 months of widowhood was RR 1.41 (95% CI 1.26–1.57)—about 40% higher than for non-widowed individuals. After 6 months, it was 1.14 (1.10–1.18), dropping to about 15% higher. For men, RR was 1.23 (1.18–1.28), roughly 23% higher. For women, it was 1.04 (1.00–1.08, not statistically significant, meaning the difference may be due to chance). There was no significant difference between those under 65 and those 65 or older. Another meta-analysis pooled 123 studies with 1,377 risk estimates covering over 500 million people. After adjusting for confounders, the hazard ratio was HR 1.23 (1.19–1.28), an overall 23% higher risk of death. The risk remained higher in men than in women: for men, HR 1.27 (1.19–1.35), about 27% higher; for women, HR 1.15 (1.08–1.22), about 15% higher.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources:Moon JR, et al. (2011). Widowhood and mortality: a meta-analysis. PLoS ONE. <https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0023465>；Shor E, et al. (2012). Widowhood and mortality: a meta-analysis and meta-regression. Demography. <https://doi.org/10.1007/s13524-012-0096-x>
- Notes: These are observational studies looking back at population data, so confounding factors cannot be completely eliminated. Spouses often share similar lifestyles and health conditions to begin with. The finding that the risk is much higher in the first six months comes from the first meta-analysis, which stratified by time since widowhood. The second meta-analysis did not stratify by time since bereavement, but by follow-up duration: studies with only 6 months of follow-up pooled to HR 1.58 (1.32–1.88), while those following participants for 16 to 20 years showed HR 1.22 (1.02–1.47). The authors concluded that risk is highest in the earliest years, though they noted most studies did not start follow-up at the exact moment of bereavement. Both analyses point in the same direction, making the first six months a critical care window. Men face higher risk and are also less likely to ask for help. If an older relative has lost a spouse, sort out their financial accounts, guardianship, and wills according to Section 17. If no such person is available (living alone, childless, or having lost adult children), delegate this tracking to community resources and smartphone reminders, as covered in item 6 of this section.

### 6. If you have neither family nor friends, replace "the person watching over you" with three things: a neighbor who can enter your home, the community check-in list, and emergency contacts in your phone
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: Free. The hard part is speaking up first: telling a neighbor and the residents' committee, "I live alone, and something just happened in my family."
- In plain terms: When you have neither family nor friends, take care of two things: making sure someone finds you if something goes wrong, and taking your medicine and eating on time every day. Put your meals and medication on a written checklist and set alarms—do not rely on memory. Making sure someone finds you relies on three things: a spare key left with a neighbor, emergency contacts set up on your phone, and the community check-in list. That list covers older adults who live alone or in empty nests; you can ask about it at your residents' committee.
- Benefit: In the REACH international registry, 44,573 outpatients with established atherothrombosis or multiple risk factors (plaques in blood vessels or prone to blood clots) were enrolled from December 2003 to December 2004 across multiple countries and followed for 4 years. Among them, 8,594 (19%) lived alone. Over 4 years, all-cause mortality was 14.1% for those living alone versus 11.1% for those living with others. Cardiovascular death was 8.6% versus 6.8% (both log-rank P < 0.01, meaning the difference is unlikely to be accidental). The gap varied across age groups (interaction P = 0.03). For ages 45 to 65, all-cause mortality was 7.7% living alone versus 5.7% living with others; after adjusting for other factors, the hazard ratio (HR) was 1.24 (95% CI 1.01–1.51), or roughly a 24% higher risk of death. For ages 66 to 80, mortality was 13.2% versus 12.3%, HR 1.12 (95% CI 1.01–1.26), roughly a 10% higher risk. For ages over 80, mortality was 24.6% versus 28.4%, HR 0.92 (95% CI 0.79–1.06), showing no increase. A 2022 policy opinion issued by ten Chinese government agencies, including the Ministry of Civil Affairs, mandates check-in and care services. Targets include older adults who live alone, empty-nesters, left-behind seniors, individuals with disabilities or severe impairments, and families who lost their only child. Service formats include "regular home visits, phone or video calls, and remote monitoring." Subdistricts and townships conduct baseline screening with assistance from village and residents' committees, logging basic information and the resident's "willingness to accept check-in and care services" into the grassroots grid-management system (assigned by zone to dedicated personnel). By the end of 2025, the policy aims to "ensure a 100% monthly check-in rate for seniors facing special difficulties" and "no less than one check-in per month for disabled seniors facing special difficulties." The document also encourages households to install smart emergency call systems, smart water and electricity meters, health monitoring products, and eldercare monitoring devices: "Once an abnormality is detected, an alert should be issued promptly, with notification sent to emergency contacts at the same time." When service personnel discover an emergency, they "must assist in calling emergency services immediately."
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources:Udell JA, et al. (2012). Living alone and cardiovascular risk in outpatients at risk of or with atherothrombosis. Archives of Internal Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.1001/archinternmed.2012.2782>；民政部等十部门 (2022). 关于开展特殊困难老年人探访关爱服务的指导意见（民发〔2022〕73 号）. <https://www.gov.cn/zhengce/zhengceku/2022-10/13/content_5718017.htm>
- Notes: How to set up these three safeguards in practice. First, give a spare key or temporary door lock code to a neighbor or building property management, and tell them, "I will be alone for the next few days." You do not need deep friendship, just someone who knows you are inside. Second, visit your community residents' committee, explain that you live alone, and ask whether you can be placed on the check-in and care list. Seniors living alone, empty-nesters, or those who have lost an only child are explicit targets of official policy; ask as well whether your locality offers free installation of smart emergency call buttons or smart water meters. Third, set up emergency contacts and a Medical ID card on your phone; keep it unmuted and powered on during these days. Two reasons for grade B: First, the link between living alone and mortality comes from observational cohort data, which cannot rule out reverse causation (people in poorer health or with fewer resources may be more likely to live alone); the original authors noted that the finding warrants further confirmation. Second, the official check-in policy only covers older adults; there is no dedicated system for people under 60. The only recourse is a provision in another policy requiring community grid workers and social workers to "promptly identify psychological crisis risks arising from family upheaval, unemployment, or school dropout" (see item 11 in this section on calling 12356 if you need someone to talk to). Why "having someone in the house" matters: see Section 13, Item 1 (chest compressions). Among out-of-hospital cardiac arrests, 79.2% occur at home. When a bystander performs chest compressions, 16.1% survive to hospital discharge, compared to only 3.9% when no one is present. Being alone defaults you to the lower tier. Two other practical tips: Managing funeral arrangements already requires running between several service counters—the funeral home, community health service center, public security bureau, and housing provident fund (see Section 25). Schedule these as a fixed daily outing to structure your days. Crisis hotlines can be dialed repeatedly, not just once (see item 11 in this section; call 12356 if you need someone to talk to). Living alone is also a documented long-term risk factor (odds ratio for mortality 1.32; see Section 22, Item 9 on treating regular social contact as a health expenditure). But for now, focus on getting through the immediate few days; the long-term balance sheet can be settled later.

### 7. After a parent dies, tell the child the truth, and let the child attend the farewell
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=大 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: No money. The hard part is that you are hurting too, and you still have to say it clearly to the child.
- In plain terms: A child who loses a father or a mother before 18 later dies at a rate about 50% higher than a child who did not go through that. If the parent died by accident or by suicide, it is about 80% higher. If the parent died of illness, it is about 30% higher. The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends telling the child what happened, truthfully, and supporting the child in attending the funeral and the farewell.
- Benefit: Everyone born in Denmark from 1968 to 2008, and everyone born in Sweden from 1973 to 2006, was counted in. Added to that was 89.3% of people born in Finland from 1987 to 2007. Together, over 7300000 people. Of them, 189094 lost a parent before 18. Mortality from all causes together in these children was 50% higher than in people who had not been bereaved (mortality ratio 1.50, 95% CI 1.43–1.58, that is, about 1.5 times). When the parent died of an unnatural cause such as an accident or suicide, it was 1.84 (1.71–2.00), about 80% higher. When the parent died of a natural cause such as illness, it was 1.33 (1.24–1.41), about 30% higher. It was highest when the child's cause of death and the parent's cause of death were the same class. A 2016 clinical report from the American Academy of Pediatrics, written for pediatricians, requires that a talk with a bereaved child state clearly what happened and what it means, and correct wrong information and misunderstandings the child has heard. The report also gives advice on how to support the child in attending the funeral and other farewell rites.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources:Li J, et al. (2014). Mortality after parental death in childhood: a nationwide cohort study from three Nordic countries. PLoS Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pmed.1001679>；Schonfeld DJ, Demaria T, Committee on Psychosocial Aspects of Child and Family Health, Disaster Preparedness Advisory Council (2016). Supporting the Grieving Child and Family. Pediatrics, 138(3), e20162147. <https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2016-2147>
- Notes: When you tell the child, do not invent "they went away on a long trip." Guardianship and the arrangement for raising the child have to be put on paper. Whether to change schools, or to send the child to live somewhere else, has no direct study or guideline text that was found. The author's view is that fewer changes in the child's life are better. The grade is B not because the numbers are soft. This is a follow-up of the whole population of Denmark, Sweden, and Finland, and the numbers themselves would be enough for grade A. The grade is B because, from this number to the specific practice of how to tell the child and whether the child should attend the farewell, there is no direct trial evidence. The authors also state that data on the parent-child relationship after the death, and on the child's way of living, are both missing. The rise includes a part that is inborn constitution, and a part that is the long effect left by the bereavement. Naming a guardian and arranging property are in section 17. The child's support and schooling are in section 18.

### 8. Still stuck in grief after half a year, unable to move on — when to see a psychiatrist or clinical psychologist
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=少 时间=中 毅力=些 收益=大 口径=死亡率 -->

- Cost: This includes registration fees and treatment costs. Treatment is delivered in sessions; on average, 16 sessions are given over 19 weeks in clinical trials. The main challenge is simply mustering the courage to make that first appointment.

- In plain terms: About 1 in 10 bereaved adults gets stuck. After more than half a year they still miss the person so much that they cannot eat, cannot go to work, and cannot look after a child. They feel numb, and life seems to have no point. This is not a lack of strength. It has diagnostic criteria, and it has a treatment made for it. Targeted treatment works in 51% of cases, ordinary psychotherapy in 28%, and the targeted treatment also starts to work sooner.

- Benefit: Fourteen studies involving adults whose loved ones died of non‑violent causes and who had not previously sought mental‑health care reveal that prolonged grief disorder affects 9.8% of this group (95% CI 6.8–14.0), meaning about 10 out of every 100 bereaved adults are impacted. The rate rises with age. Another study tested the diagnostic criteria on 291 bereaved individuals through interviews at 0–6, 6–12, and 12–24 months after loss. The core symptom is intense longing for the deceased; at least five of nine additional criteria must also be present. The first five criteria are emotional numbness, shock, feeling life lacks meaning, distrust of others, and distress caused by the loss. The remaining four are difficulty accepting the loss, trouble defining one’s own identity, avoidance of reality, and an inability to carry on daily life. All these symptoms must appear daily or be severe enough to impair functioning, and they must persist for at least six months after the death. A randomized trial of 95 participants receiving 16 therapy sessions over 19 weeks found a 51% success rate for specialized treatment versus 28% for standard interpersonal psychotherapy (P = 0.02, a statistically significant difference). Consequently, only 4.3 patients need to be treated for one additional person to experience improvement.

- Evidence grade: A
- Sources:Lundorff M, et al. (2017). Prevalence of prolonged grief disorder in adult bereavement: A systematic review and meta-analysis. Journal of Affective Disorders. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jad.2017.01.030>；Prigerson HG, et al. (2009). Prolonged grief disorder: Psychometric validation of criteria proposed for DSM-V and ICD-11. PLoS Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pmed.1000121>；Shear MK, et al. (2005). Treatment of complicated grief: a randomized controlled trial. JAMA. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.293.21.2601>

- Notes: Don’t fixate on exact time limits. The real indicator is whether grief interferes with eating, sleeping, work, or childcare — if so, it’s time to seek help. The appropriate clinics are psychiatry, psychology, or clinical psychology departments, all of which are available at public psychiatric hospitals. This condition is known as prolonged grief disorder or complex grief in various sources; required duration varies from six to twelve months across different guidelines. The 9.8% prevalence figure stems from 14 studies with differing methodologies, so authors caution that it may not apply universally to all populations.

### 9. Do not start by paying for grief counseling; first see whether your grief is really stuck (the signs in item 8)
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: No money. The other way around, you save a counseling fee.
- In plain terms: Arranging psychological counseling for every bereaved person brings a small benefit, and after a while it can no longer be seen, because most people would have eased on their own. For people who already clearly cannot adapt, treatment works as well as treatment for other psychological problems. So check item 8 first (grief that is stuck, go to psychiatry). Spend this money only if it matches.
- Benefit: 61 controlled studies taken together. Giving psychological intervention to bereaved people in general shows a very small benefit just when treatment ends, and looking again after an interval shows no benefit left. Interventions that enroll only bereaved people who "already clearly cannot adapt" work as well as psychological treatment for other psychological problems. Studies that do no screening beforehand come out poorly, because the group that got no intervention would also have eased on its own over time. Another 14 trials that assigned people by lot, taken together: treatment for people who already had a problem was clearly better than the control both after treatment and at later visits, and after an interval the effect was still growing. Preventive intervention given in general to people who did not yet have a problem did not work. Another study followed 205 people from before the spouse died through 6 and 18 months after. That study separated 5 paths. They were ordinary grief, prolonged grief, prolonged depression, getting better during the bereavement, and resilience (able to hold up). Resilience was the most common. Ordinary grief was relatively uncommon.

### 12. In the first months after a blow, push back every big decision that cannot be undone
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: No money. You only push the decision back, and you may miss some opportunities because of that. The hard part is that at the time it feels as if the opportunity will not wait.
- In plain terms: In this stretch your judgment of every "opportunity" is at its worst. In an experiment, sad people were willing to take a bit over 10% less money, and up to a bit over 30% less, so they would not have to wait 3 months. Anything larger than a month of income, or anything that would cost a lot to undo, gets pushed back for 3 months. Tell it first to a person who has no stake in the matter. Do not decide the same day.
- Benefit: Avoid making a property disposal you cannot undo, in the stretch just after the blow, when judgment is at its worst. This kind of thing includes selling a home, quitting a job, putting a pension or a compensation payment out as a lump sum, remarrying in a hurry, and standing guarantee for someone. The amount of this kind of decision is usually on the scale of several tens of thousands of yuan up to a home, so the benefit is set at medium. Official documents likewise list "a family blow, job loss, and dropping out of school" as psychological-crisis risks that the community should go and look for on its own initiative. The state of this stretch is already treated as a state that needs an outsider to lend a hand. The grief guide of the US National Institute on Aging (NIA) lists "making decisions is hard" as a common feature of grief. It suggests: if you can choose, push major life decisions back until you feel better. While you are grieving and may not be able to think clearly, it is better not to make a large change such as selling a home or quitting a job. A study ran a set of experiments. The researchers assigned people at random, put some of them into a sad state, and then had them choose "take the money now" or "take the money after 3 months." Compared with people in a calm mood, sad people were willing to take 13% to 34% less money so they would not have to wait 3 months. This was sadness brought on in an experiment, not a real bereavement. In 2023 the US Federal Trade Commission (FTC) warned about a scam: someone poses as a funeral home and tells the family that if they do not pay more at once, the funeral will be cancelled. The first step it gives is to resist the pressure to "do it right now." A proper business will give you time to decide.

### 13. Do not treat death as a way out of debt: life insurance does not pay within two years, work injury claims do not apply, and debts are still deducted from the estate first

### 14. Do not rely on an immediate one-off debriefing after a crash, assault, or disaster; if nightmares, flashbacks, and severe hyperarousal persist after a month, seek trauma-focused psychotherapy in psychiatry
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=少 时间=中 毅力=些 收益=大 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: Outpatient consultation fees plus psychotherapy fees, typically taking 8 to 12 sessions. The hardest part of therapy is having to revisit the very memories you dread facing the most.
- In plain terms: After a major trauma, having nightmares and jumping at loud noises during the first few weeks is common, and most people recover on their own. If severe distress lasts past a month, it may be post-traumatic stress disorder, which responds well to specialized trauma-focused therapy. Pulling someone in right after the incident to recount everything in a single debriefing does not prevent the disorder; in one trial, those who underwent it were actually worse off three years later.
- Benefit: According to the National Health Commission's *Guideline for Diagnosis and Treatment of Mental Disorders (2020 Edition)*, an average of about 8% of people exposed to psychological trauma develop post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD). Symptoms fall into three core clusters: recurrent intrusive memories such as nightmares and flashbacks; active avoidance of thoughts, places, or people related to the event; and persistent hyperarousal, being easily startled, and insomnia. A diagnosis is made only when symptoms persist for more than 1 month and significantly impair family, academic, or occupational functioning. The guideline notes in its section on acute stress disorder that most people recover through self-adjustment or acute crisis intervention. For treatment efficacy, a 2020 meta-analysis of 114 randomized controlled trials with 8,171 participants found that trauma-focused cognitive behavioral therapy (TF-CBT), compared with waitlist or usual care, had a standardized mean difference (SMD) of −1.32 (95% CI −1.57 to −1.08), with moderate-quality evidence. Eye movement desensitization and reprocessing (EMDR) had an SMD of −1.23 (−1.69 to −0.76), with low-quality evidence. SMD and Cohen's d share the same scale, where values above 0.8 are considered large effect sizes; both modalities exceeded this benchmark. Non-directive supportive counseling showed no clear difference compared with waitlist (−0.43, −0.90 to 0.04). A 2013 Cochrane review by the same authors (70 trials, 4,761 participants) showed consistent directions: TF-CBT had an SMD of −1.62 (−2.03 to −1.21) and EMDR was −1.17 (−2.04 to −0.30), though evidence quality was rated very low at the time. The UK NICE 2018 guidelines recommend the following: offer individual TF-CBT to adults with severe symptoms within 1 month of the trauma. For those diagnosed or with significant symptoms after 1 month, offer individual TF-CBT as well, typically 8 to 12 sessions. For non-combat trauma that has not resolved at 3 months, EMDR is also offered. For mild symptoms not meeting diagnostic criteria, active monitoring is advised during the first month, with a follow-up scheduled within that month. The Chinese guideline lists similar psychotherapies: prolonged exposure, cognitive processing therapy, and EMDR. Regarding single-session debriefings immediately after an event, three sources provide clear evidence. A 2002 Cochrane review of 11 trials found that a single individual debriefing session did not reduce distress or prevent PTSD; at 3 to 5 months, the odds ratio (OR) was 1.22 (0.60–2.46), showing no benefit. One trial followed participants to 1 year and found a higher incidence of PTSD in the debriefed group, with an OR of 2.88 (1.11–7.53). The authors concluded that compulsory debriefing of trauma victims should cease. Another randomized trial followed hospitalized motor vehicle crash victims; at 3 years, the debriefed group had significantly worse psychiatric symptoms, travel anxiety, pain, physical problems, overall functioning, and financial difficulties. Among participants with severe intrusive and avoidance symptoms at baseline, those who were debriefed still had symptoms at 3 years, whereas non-debriefed participants had recovered. The NICE guidelines explicitly advise against offering psychologically focused debriefings for the prevention or treatment of PTSD, citing a lack of benefit in both individual and group formats, along with signs that outcomes may be worse than doing nothing.
