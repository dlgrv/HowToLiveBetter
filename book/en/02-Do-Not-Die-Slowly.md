# 2. Avoid a slow death

This section covers just two types of long‑term health risks: those that have a big impact on overall mortality and those backed by solid evidence. The items are listed in order of cost‑effectiveness, from best to worst. A few entries are marked “randomized trials,” meaning participants were randomly assigned to different groups for comparison — results from such studies are the most reliable. All other figures come from observational studies that simply track people over time without any group assignment. In these studies, risk ratios such as HR or RR can be skewed by two main factors: natural differences between individuals and the fact that some people develop health problems before the study even begins. Because of this, these numbers should be interpreted as giving a general sense of direction and magnitude, not as a guaranteed “discount” you can count on by following a certain course of action.
### 1. Quitting smoking — the earlier, the better
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=是 收益=大 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: It costs nothing; in fact, it saves money. Smoking a pack a day costs roughly $3–$4, which you can keep after quitting. The hard part is getting through the withdrawal period, which usually lasts a few weeks to several months.
- In plain terms: On average, smokers live more than 10 years less than non-smokers. Quitting before age 40 wipes out about 90% of the extra death risk caused by continued smoking. The earlier you quit, the more years you gain.
- Benefit: A U.S. study that tracked a large group of people (without assigning them to groups) found that current smokers have an expected lifespan more than 10 years shorter than those who never smoked. Quitting before age 40 eliminates roughly 90% of this extra mortality risk. Quitting between ages 25–34 adds about 10 years to life expectancy, 35–44 adds about 9 years, and 45–54 adds about 6 years (numbers are reported in years of life gained). Similar Chinese research from the 2010s shows that urban male smokers have a 1.65 times higher death risk than non-smokers (RR 1.65), while rural male smokers have a 1.22 times higher risk. After 10 years of sustained abstinence, this extra risk linked to smoking nearly disappears.
- Evidence grade: A
- Notes: Breathing in secondhand smoke is also deadly. In 2004, an estimated 603,000 people worldwide died from exposure to secondhand smoke — that’s about 1% of all global deaths that year. Even if you don’t smoke yourself, you should avoid secondhand smoke, especially to protect children. This section only outlines the benefits of quitting; specific quitting methods are covered in the following four sections: Section 3 (smoking cessation medications), Section 4 (picking a quit date), Section 5 (visiting a smoking cessation clinic), and Section 6 (e-cigarettes).
- Sources:Jha P 等 (2013). 21st-century hazards of smoking and benefits of cessation in the United States. NEJM. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMsa1211128>；Chen Z 等 (2015). Contrasting male and female trends in tobacco-attributed mortality in China: evidence from successive nationwide prospective cohort studies. Lancet. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(15)00340-2>；Oberg M 等 (2011). Worldwide burden of disease from exposure to second-hand smoke: a retrospective analysis of data from 192 countries. ### 2. Don’t smoke at home or in the car, and don’t let guests smoke indoors.
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=大 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: There’s no cost or time involved. The hard part is simply asking family members and guests to refrain from smoking at home.
- In plain terms: Around 600,000 people worldwide die each year from secondhand smoke exposure, nearly 30% of whom are children. Long-term exposure to secondhand smoke raises the risk of high blood pressure, heart disease, and stroke by roughly 30%. Smoke inhaled at home is even more harmful than smoke inhaled outside.
- Benefit: In 2004, secondhand smoke caused 603,000 deaths globally — accounting for about 1.0% of all worldwide deaths, with children making up 28% of those fatalities. A pooled analysis of 57 studies found that people exposed to secondhand smoke have a 1.28 times higher risk of developing high blood pressure, 1.39 times higher risk of heart disease, 1.50 times higher risk of myocardial infarction, and 1.36 times higher risk of stroke compared to non-exposed individuals. The risk of harm from secondhand smoke is greater at home than outside.
- Evidence grade: A
- Notes: Focus first on home environments. People spend more time at home, and pooled research shows that secondhand smoke exposure at home carries greater health risks than exposure outside. Children bear 61% of the overall health burden caused by secondhand smoke. For tips on quitting smoking yourself, see item 1 in this section.

### 3. Quitting smoking isn’t just about willpower — medication can more than double success rates
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=少 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=大 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: Nicotine patches and gum are over-the-counter products available at any pharmacy. A full course lasts 8–12 weeks and costs anywhere from a few hundred to over a thousand yuan. Varenicline and bupropion are prescription drugs, so they must be obtained through a smoking cessation clinic or a pulmonology department. These costs are roughly offset by the money saved from buying fewer cigarettes during the same period.
- In plain terms:
Relying solely on willpower rarely works for most smokers. People taking varenicline are more than twice as likely to quit compared to those taking a placebo. Those using nicotine replacement products are about 50% more likely to succeed than those not using any medication at all. Combining patches with gum or lozenges can increase success rates by another 20% or so.
- Benefit:
Data from 41 randomized trials involving 17,395 participants show that varenicline raises smoking cessation rates to 2.32 times those seen with placebo (RR 2.32, 95% CI 2.15–2.51; high‑certainty evidence). Varenicline also outperforms bupropion (RR 1.36, roughly 36% higher success) and single‑type nicotine replacement therapy (RR 1.25, about 25% higher). Across 133 trials with 64,640 participants, nicotine replacement products alone boost success rates by roughly 55% (RR 1.55, 95% CI 1.49–1.61). Adding a fast‑acting form such as gum or lozenges to patches further raises success rates by about 27% (RR 1.27, 95% CI 1.17–1.37; high‑certainty evidence from 16 trials with 12,169 participants).
- Evidence grade: A
- Notes: In China, these three types of medications are currently available for smoking cessation; only nicotine replacement products are sold without a prescription. Common side effects of varenicline include nausea, vivid dreams, and trouble sleeping; anyone with a psychiatric history should discuss this with their doctor. A typical treatment course lasts 8–12 weeks, and users should taper off gradually under medical guidance rather than stopping abruptly. Medication helps ease withdrawal symptoms during those early weeks but does not eliminate cravings triggered by specific situations, so it works best when combined with the other strategies outlined in this chapter — such as picking a quit date (section 4) and seeking professional help at a smoking cessation clinic (section 5).

### 4. Pick a quit date and stop smoking on that day — don’t taper gradually
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=是 收益=中 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: No cost at all. You simply choose a date on the calendar and let family and coworkers know in advance.
- In plain terms: Many people plan to cut back a little each day until they reach zero. One randomized trial involving 697 participants compared these two approaches. After six months, 22% of those who stopped on a set quit date remained smoke‑free, compared with only 15.5% of those who tapered down gradually. Even people who initially preferred tapering did better when they switched to a single‑day quit plan.
- Benefit: In the UK, 697 smokers were randomly assigned to either quit on a predetermined date or to reduce their cigarette intake by 75% during the two weeks prior to that date. Both groups received nursing support and used nicotine replacement therapy around the quit day. At four weeks, 49.0% (95% CI 43.8–54.2) of the single‑day quitters stayed smoke‑free, versus 39.2% (34.0–44.4) of the tapering group; the relative risk was 0.80 (0.66–0.93). By six months, 22.0% (18.0–26.6) of the single‑day quitters remained abstinent, compared with 15.5% (12.0–19.7) of the tapering group; the relative risk was 0.71 (0.46–0.91). Those who originally favored tapering also had lower success rates at four weeks when they followed the single‑day quit plan (38.3% vs. 52.2%).
- Evidence grade: A
- Notes: This finding is somewhat controversial. An earlier Cochrane review that directly compared the two methods concluded they produced similar outcomes (RR 0.94, 95% CI 0.79–1.13, based on 10 trials with 3,760 participants), but that review was withdrawn in 2019 and never updated. Consequently, the current best evidence comes from the trial described above, which favors a single‑day quit date. Moreover, both groups in that trial used nicotine replacement therapy starting two weeks before the quit day; evidence shows this early use raises success rates by roughly 25% (RR 1.25, 95% CI 1.08–1.44, based on 9 trials with 4,395 participants, moderate‑certainty evidence). Therefore, the most effective approach appears to be picking a quit date, beginning nicotine replacement therapy two weeks ahead, and then stopping smoking entirely on that day.
- Sources:Lindson-Hawley N, Banting M, West R, Michie S, Shinkins B, Aveyard P (2016). Gradual Versus Abrupt Smoking Cessation: A Randomized, Controlled Noninferiority Trial. Annals of Internal Medicine, 164(9), 585–592. <https://doi.org/10.7326/M14-2805>；Theodoulou A, Chepkin SC, Ye W, et al. (2023). Different doses, durations and modes of delivery of nicotine replacement therapy for smoking cessation. Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, 6, CD013308（提前用尼古丁替代品那一段）. <https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD013308.pub2>

### 5. Visit a smoking cessation clinic or call 12320 to find local services
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=少 时间=中 毅力=些 收益=大 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: A single clinic visit costs just a few to several dozen yuan. Behavioral support typically involves 4–8 sessions lasting 30–60 minutes each, totaling under 5 hours. Calling the hotline is completely free.
- In plain terms: Medications and regular follow-up from professionals are two separate but complementary tools — together they work far better than either alone. People who take medication while receiving dedicated follow-up are nearly twice as likely to quit compared to those who only receive brief advice. Phone support also helps: those who proactively call the cessation hotline have a 20–30% higher chance of quitting than people who only get printed materials or a single conversation.
- Benefit: Across 52 trials involving 19,488 participants, groups receiving both medication and behavioral support had a 1.83 times higher quit rate than groups receiving standard care or brief advice (RR 1.83, 95% CI 1.68–1.98; high-quality evidence). Among 43 trials conducted in medical settings, this advantage rose to 1.97 times (95% CI 1.79–2.18). For phone-based support specifically: among people who called the hotline themselves, those receiving multiple follow-up calls had a 38% higher quit rate than those only given materials or a single call (RR 1.38, 95% CI 1.19–1.61; 14 trials, 32,484 participants). For people who did not call the hotline but received phone support from researchers, the advantage was 25% higher (RR 1.25, 95% CI 1.15–1.35; 65 trials, 41,233 participants). Both findings represent moderate-quality evidence.
- Evidence grade: A
- Notes: How to find local services in China: Search “China Smoking Cessation Platform” on WeChat to access a list of local hotlines and clinics, or call 12320 to ask about nearby options. Most of the above trials were conducted abroad, and the intensity of services offered at clinics varies widely across regions; these figures should be viewed as general guidelines only. Details on recommended medications can be found in Section 3 of this chapter (Smoking cessation medications).
- Sources:Stead LF, Koilpillai P, Fanshawe TR, Lancaster T (2016). Combined pharmacotherapy and behavioural interventions for smoking cessation. Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, 3, CD008286. <https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD008286.pub3>；Matkin W, Ordóñez-Mena JM, Hartmann-Boyce J (2019). Telephone counselling for smoking cessation. Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, 5, CD002850. <https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD002850.pub4>；中国疾病预防控制中心 (2021). 「中国戒烟平台」微信小程序正式上线：「在健康中国行动控烟行动工作组指导下中国疾病预防控制中心和世界卫生组织驻华代表处联合制作了中国权威戒烟资源库『中国戒烟平台』微信小程序。小程序内容主要包括：戒烟热线、戒烟门诊详细信息，以及线上戒烟服务资源等」；同一站点页脚标注「健康咨询电话：12320」. <https://www.chinacdc.cn/jkyj/yckz/gzdt/202203/t20220310_296389.html>

### 6. Only consider e‑cigarettes if you’ve already tried quitting; non‑smokers should avoid them
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=少 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: The device plus cartridges cost anywhere from a few dozen to several hundred yuan per month. In China, only tobacco‑flavored products are legally available, and they can only be purchased at stores holding a tobacco retail license.
- In plain terms: Switching to nicotine‑containing e‑cigarettes does raise the odds of quitting compared to using nicotine patches — this finding comes from high‑quality randomized trials. Yet they’re not harmless: long‑term safety data remain limited. For people who’ve never smoked, there’s absolutely no benefit to using them.
- Benefit: A regularly updated review examined 80 randomized trials involving 29,861 participants. Compared with nicotine replacement therapy, e‑cigarettes with nicotine raised quit rates by roughly 61% (RR 1.61, 95% CI 1.23–2.12; high‑certainty evidence from 11 trials with 4,114 participants). In absolute terms, that translates to about 4 extra quitters per 100 people (range: 1–7). Serious adverse events occurred at similar rates between groups (risk difference 0.01, 95% CI −0.01–0.02; moderate‑certainty evidence). When compared with behavioral support alone or no support at all, e‑cigarettes increased quit rates by roughly 75% (RR 1.75, 95% CI 1.39–2.20; low‑certainty evidence).
- Evidence grade: A
- Notes: This topic is controversial. The World Health Organization does not endorse e‑cigarettes as a quitting aid, citing insufficient long‑term safety data and concerns they might draw youth into nicotine use. The UK, however, includes them in its quit‑smoking services. In China, e‑cigarettes aren’t fully banned but are tightly regulated under the “E‑Cigarette Management Measures.” Only tobacco‑flavored products may be sold, and only at physical stores holding a tobacco retail license; fruit flavors and refillable tanks are prohibited. Sales via any online platform or social media outside the state‑run e‑cigarette trading platform are illegal, and they may not be sold to minors. Unregulated fruit‑flavored e‑cigarettes thus circulate through illicit channels, including those laced with synthetic cannabinoids — see Section 22, Item 4 (“Don’t accept unknown cartridges”). The review referenced here covers only legally regulated nicotine‑containing products; unverified e‑liquid sources aren’t included. As a first step, it’s advisable to try the medication approach described in Item 3 of this section, which boasts stronger evidence and lower cost.
- Sources:Lindson N, Livingstone-Banks J, Butler AR, et al. (2026). Electronic cigarettes for smoking cessation. Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, 8, CD010216. <https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD010216.pub11>；国家烟草专卖局 (2022). 电子烟管理办法（国家烟草专卖局公告 2022 年第 1 号，2022 年 5 月 1 日施行）第十八条：从事电子烟零售业务，要向烟草专卖行政主管部门申领烟草专卖零售许可证。第二十二条：「禁止向未成年人出售电子烟产品。」第二十三条第二款：「任何个人、法人或者其他组织不得通过本办法规定的电子烟交易管理平台以外的信息网络销售电子烟产品、雾化物和电子烟用烟碱等。」第二十六条：「禁止销售除烟草口味外的调味电子烟和可自行添加雾化物的电子烟。」. <http://www.gov.cn/gongbao/content/2022/content_5697988.htm>

### 7. Not drinking sugary drinks — switching to sugar-free versions doesn’t help either
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=大 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: There’s no cost involved, nor does it take any extra time. Switching to plain water or sugar-free tea can even save money. The real challenge is breaking the habit of drinking sweet beverages, including sugar-free ones.
- In plain terms: People who drink more than two sugary drinks per day have a roughly 20% higher chance of dying during the same period compared to those who rarely drink them. Those who have one to two per day also face a more than 10% higher risk. Switching to sugar-free versions brings no benefit: people who consume two or more sugar-free drinks daily actually have a roughly 25% higher risk of death.
- Benefit: Two large U.S. studies followed large groups of people without any subgroup assignments; together they included about 118,000 participants and 36,000 deaths. Those drinking two or more sugary drinks daily had a 21% higher risk of death compared to those consuming less than one per month (HR 1.21). For those drinking one to two per day, the risk was 14% higher (HR 1.14). The European EPIC study followed 450,000 people across 10 countries without any subgroup assignments. Compared to those drinking less than one sugary drink per month, those drinking two or more per day had a 8% higher risk (HR 1.08, 95% CI 1.01–1.16). For artificially sweetened drinks — i.e., sugar-free versions — the risk was 26% higher (HR 1.26, 1.16–1.35). When all soft drinks were considered together, the risk was 17% higher (HR 1.17, 1.11–1.22).
- Evidence grade: A
- Notes: There’s some controversy regarding sugar-free drinks. The association with artificially sweetened drinks is actually stronger than with sugary ones, which likely reflects reverse causality: people who are already overweight or have diabetes tend to switch to sugar-free versions. Therefore, this shouldn’t be taken as proof that artificial sweeteners are more harmful. At the very least, it shows there’s no evidence supporting the claim that “switching to sugar-free drinks is fine.” Plain water or sugar-free tea remains the safest choice. Additionally, these studies are purely observational and lack randomized controlled trials; people who drink more sugary drinks generally have poorer overall lifestyles, so these numbers may be somewhat inflated.
- Sources:Malik VS 等 (2019). Long-Term Consumption of Sugar-Sweetened and Artificially Sweetened Beverages and Risk of Mortality in US Adults. Circulation. <https://doi.org/10.1161/CIRCULATIONAHA.118.037401>；Mullee A, Romaguera D, Pearson-Stuttard J, et al. (2019). Association Between Soft Drink Consumption and Mortality in 10 European Countries. JAMA Internal Medicine, 179(11), 1479-1490. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2019.2478>

### 8. Not chewing betel nut
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=大 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: There is no cost or time investment required; it even saves money that would otherwise be spent on betel nut. The real challenge is breaking the habit of chewing it.
- In plain terms: Compared to non-chewers, people who chew betel nut have a roughly 20% higher chance of dying during the same period. Their risk of developing diabetes and metabolic syndrome is nearly 50% higher. Additional risks of oral cancer and esophageal cancer also apply.
- Benefit: Based on 17 Asian studies involving 388,000 participants, chewing betel nut raises overall mortality risk by about 21% compared to non-chewers (relative risk 1.21, P=0.02). This mortality figure applies to 179,600 of those participants. The risk of diabetes rises by roughly 47% (1.47), and metabolic syndrome risk increases by about 51% (1.51). Betel nut itself is also a known risk factor for oral and esophageal cancers.
- Evidence grade: A
- Notes: The longer and more frequently one chews betel nut, the higher the risk of oral cancer becomes. After quitting, this risk gradually declines over time. Betel nut products containing tobacco are even more hazardous.
- Sources:Yamada T, Hara K, Kadowaki T (2013). Chewing betel quid and the risk of metabolic disease, cardiovascular disease, and all-cause mortality: a meta-analysis. PLoS One, 8(8), e70679. <https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0070679>

### 9. Switch household salt to low‑sodium (potassium‑rich) salt  
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=少 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: A bag of low‑sodium salt costs a few yuan more than regular salt. Switching is easy when you shop, and it adds little extra time. The taste stays almost the same.  
- In plain terms: In a randomized trial of 20,000 participants, those who switched to low‑sodium salt at home had a roughly 12% lower chance of dying within five years and a roughly 14% lower chance of having a stroke. Because the groups were randomly assigned, these numbers are more reliable than data from simple observational studies.  
- Benefit: This randomized trial was conducted in rural China with 20,995 participants — all either stroke survivors or hypertensive adults aged 60 or older — and followed them for 4.74 years. Compared with people using regular salt, those using low‑sodium salt had a 12% lower risk of death (RR = 0.88), a 14% lower risk of stroke (RR = 0.86), and a 13% lower risk of major cardiovascular events (RR = 0.87). No statistically significant difference emerged between the groups regarding episodes of high blood potassium.  
- Evidence grade: A  
- Notes: This finding is somewhat controversial. The PURE study only tracked participants over time without random assignment; it reported that people excreting less than 3 g of sodium per day had a roughly 27% higher risk of death or cardiovascular events, while those excreting more than 7 g per day faced a roughly 15% higher risk. Thus both very low and very high sodium intake appear detrimental, with moderate intake being preferable. However, the Chinese rural trial only partially replaced regular salt with low‑sodium salt, so sodium levels did not drop to the extremely low levels examined in PURE. Moreover, the trial involved high‑risk elderly individuals; the health benefits for healthy younger adults would likely be smaller. People with impaired kidney function or those taking potassium‑sparing medications should consult a physician before switching to low‑sodium salt.
- Sources:Neal B 等 (2021). Effect of Salt Substitution on Cardiovascular Events and Death. NEJM. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa2105675>；O'Donnell M 等 (2014). Urinary sodium and potassium excretion, mortality, and cardiovascular events. NEJM. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1311889>（争议方 PURE）

### 10. Brush teeth thoroughly and clean between teeth daily; replace missing teeth promptly
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=少 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: Dental floss or interdental brushes cost just a few dozen yuan per year, while professional teeth cleaning runs around 100–200 yuan per session. It also requires an extra two to three minutes each day. The real challenge is making this routine a daily habit.
- In plain terms: People who clean between their teeth daily have about a 10% lower risk of dying over time. For elderly individuals with no natural teeth, the risk is roughly 90% higher. Those with fewer than 20 natural teeth face double the risk compared to others.
- Benefit: A Japanese study followed 9,676 participants for six years. Those who used tools to clean between their teeth saw an 11% reduction in mortality risk (hazard ratio 0.89). People who cleaned their tongue also experienced a 23% drop in risk (0.77). When multiple studies of community-dwelling seniors were combined, individuals without any natural teeth had a 1.87-fold higher risk of death (95% CI 1.35–2.59). Those with fewer than 20 teeth faced a 2.04-fold increase (95% CI 1.67–2.49).
- Evidence grade: B
- Notes: This topic remains somewhat controversial. These studies merely track outcomes over time. People with poor dental health often also have worse overall health and lower financial stability. It’s unclear whether bad teeth negatively affect overall health, or if poor health leads to dental problems. Still, the cost of maintaining good oral hygiene is very low, and gum disease or missing teeth certainly make eating more difficult.
- Sources:Wang K, Matsuyama Y, Kiuchi S, et al. (2026). Routine oral health practices and all-cause mortality. Journal of Dentistry. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jdent.2026.106789>；Ko MJ, Seo S, So JS, et al. (2026). Deteriorated oral health and function as risk factors for physical disability and mortality in community-dwelling older adults: a systematic review and meta-analysis. European Geriatric Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.1007/s41999-025-01319-4>

### 11. Walking 7,000–8,000 steps per day
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=多 毅力=些 收益=大 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: No cost at all. Spending 60–90 minutes each day walking can easily be fit into a commute or grocery shopping. The hard part is staying consistent day after day.
- In plain terms: People who walk 5,800 steps daily have roughly a 40% lower chance of dying during the same period compared to those who walk only 3,500 steps. When they reach 7,800 steps, that risk drops by about 45%. Going beyond that still lowers the risk a bit, but only up to a certain point. For adults over 60, 6,000–8,000 steps per day is sufficient.
- Benefit: Fifteen separate studies tracking large groups of people were combined for analysis without any subgroup sorting. These studies involved 47,471 participants and 3,013 deaths. Participants were divided into four groups based on daily step counts; the lowest group had a median of 3,553 steps per day, while the other three groups averaged 5,801, 7,842, and 10,901 steps per day respectively. Compared to the lowest group, mortality risk in those groups was reduced by roughly 40%, 45%, and 53% (HR values of 0.60, 0.55, and 0.47). For people over 60, benefits level off after reaching 6,000–8,000 steps; for younger adults, benefits level off after 8,000–10,000 steps. Another analysis of multiple studies showed benefits start at around 3,867 steps per day; beyond that point, each additional 1,000 steps per day lowers mortality risk by roughly 15%.
- Evidence grade: A
- Notes: The jump from 4,000 to 7,000 steps offers the greatest benefit, so there’s no need to aim for 10,000 steps exactly. It’s important to remember these studies merely track participants over time: the group with the fewest steps includes many individuals who were already ill and physically limited. Their lower step counts reflect poor health rather than causing earlier death. This can inflate the observed risk differences. Still, the overall trend of better outcomes with more walking remains clear. This recommendation aligns with Recommendation 14 (150–300 minutes per week of moderate exercise); both approaches describe the same health benefit, so meeting either target is sufficient.
- Sources:Paluch AE 等 (2022). Daily steps and all-cause mortality: a meta-analysis of 15 international cohorts. ### 12. For people with hypertension or high cholesterol, follow your doctor’s instructions and take medication regularly — never stop on your own.

- Cost: Generic antihypertensive drugs and statins cost only a few to several dozen yuan per month. They’re taken once daily, so they don’t require much time or effort. The real challenge is sticking with them long-term; many patients stop once they feel better.

- In plain terms: For every 10 mmHg drop in blood pressure, the risk of death falls by roughly 13%, and stroke risk drops by about 27%. Each 1 mmol/L reduction in LDL cholesterol thanks to statins lowers death risk by roughly 10%. People who take their meds as prescribed have a 30% to 45% lower chance of dying compared to those who often miss doses.

- Benefit: Pooled data from multiple randomized trials show that a 10 mmHg drop in systolic blood pressure reduces overall death risk by about 13% (RR 0.87). Major cardiovascular events drop by roughly 20% (RR 0.80), stroke risk by about 27% (RR 0.73), and heart failure risk by around 28% (RR 0.72). Statins also lower LDL cholesterol; each 1.0 mmol/L reduction brings a 10% drop in death risk (RR 0.90) and a 22% drop in major vascular events (RR 0.78). Observational studies further reveal that patients who adhere to treatment at ≥80% have a 45% lower death risk when taking statins (RR 0.55) and a 29% lower risk when using antihypertensive drugs (RR 0.71).

- Evidence grade: A

- Notes: This advice applies only to patients whom doctors deem in need of medication; healthy individuals don’t require it. The figures regarding “consistent medication use” come from observational data; such patients tend to be more disciplined overall, which may inflate the perceived benefits. The numbers derived from randomized trials are more reliable. This section does not address blood sugar control separately, as evidence linking lower glucose levels to reduced mortality isn’t as consistent as that for blood pressure and cholesterol.

### 13. Getting roughly 7 hours of sleep each night and keeping a regular schedule
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=多 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: No cost involved. People simply need to allocate time for sleep by cutting back on phone usage. The real challenge is maintaining consistent bedtime and wake-up times.
- In plain terms: Individuals who regularly get less than 7 hours of sleep have a roughly 10% higher risk of death over time. This risk rises further with greater irregularity in sleep patterns. Those with the most consistent schedules have a 20%–50% lower risk compared to those with the least consistent habits. In other words, how regular your sleep schedule is matters more than the total number of hours you sleep when it comes to mortality risk.
- Benefit: An analysis of 16 observational studies involving 1.38 million participants and 113,000 deaths showed that people sleeping fewer hours had a 12% higher risk of death (RR 1.12), while those sleeping longer had a 30% higher risk (RR 1.30). Another pooled analysis by sleep duration found that the lowest risk group slept exactly 7 hours per night. For every hour less than 7 hours of sleep, the risk rose by 6% (RR 1.06), and for every extra hour beyond 7 hours, the risk climbed by 13% (RR 1.13). Data from the UK Biobank on 61,000 participants showed that those in the top four categories of sleep regularity had a 20%–48% lower risk of death than those in the least regular category. This indicates that sleep regularity is a stronger predictor of mortality than total sleep duration. A further UK Biobank study tracking 51,562 participants over time found that 3,853 developed cardiovascular disease. Social jetlag — the difference between average weekday and weekend bedtime — was also linked to higher risk: people with a social jetlag of over 2 hours had a 30% higher risk of cardiovascular disease (HR 1.30, 95% CI 1.11–1.54), even among those with normal sleep duration.
- Evidence grade: A
- Notes: The main issues to address are getting too little sleep and having an irregular schedule; there is no need to deliberately cut back on total sleep time. The higher risk seen in people sleeping longer is likely due to reverse causation: depression, chronic illness, and sleep apnea often lead to increased sleep duration. Currently, only two observational studies support the link between sleep regularity and mortality, both of which only recorded data without subgroup analysis, so this evidence is classified as grade B on its own. For guidance on how to recover sleep after staying up late, see item 39 in this section.

### 14. 150–300 minutes of moderate-intensity exercise per week, such as brisk walking
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=多 毅力=些 收益=大 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: No cost involved. It takes 20–45 minutes each day. The hard part is staying consistent over time.
- In plain terms: People who walk briskly for 150–300 minutes each week have a mortality risk that is roughly 30% lower than those who don’t exercise at all. Even if they fall short of this target, their risk is still about 20% lower. When they reach 3–5 times this amount, the risk drops to its lowest level — roughly 40% lower. Exceeding this amount further won’t cut the risk any further, but it does no harm either.
- Benefit: When multiple longitudinal studies are pooled together (without any subgroup analysis), individuals whose activity levels reach 1–2 times the recommended minimum have a mortality risk that is about 31% lower than non-exercisers (HR 0.69). This activity level corresponds to 7.5–15 MET·hours per week, which is roughly equivalent to 150–300 minutes of brisk walking each week. Those who fail to meet this minimum still enjoy a roughly 20% lower risk (HR 0.80). For people whose activity levels reach 3–5 times the minimum, the risk drops to its peak level of about 39% lower (HR 0.61). Any further increase in activity levels beyond this point won’t lower the risk any further, though it remains harmless (for levels exceeding 10 times the minimum, HR is 0.69). Another pooled study that used accelerometers to measure physical activity found that the top quarter of participants with the highest levels of moderate-to-vigorous activity had a mortality risk that was roughly 48% lower than the bottom quarter (HR 0.52). Accelerometers are wearable devices used to measure actual activity levels.
- Evidence grade: A
- Notes: You can choose either this recommendation or Recommendation 11 (walking 7,000–8,000 steps per day). It is important to note that these studies are purely observational in nature. The studies that used accelerometers to measure activity levels had relatively short follow-up periods and included a disproportionately older participant pool; these results also contain some bias from the fact that some participants only became inactive after developing health issues. The actual health benefits are not as dramatic as the claim that mortality risk can be reduced to just 0.52 times that of non-exercisers.
- Sources:Arem H 等 (2015). Leisure time physical activity and mortality: a detailed pooled analysis of the dose-response relationship. ### 15. Playing racket sports three times a week for 45 minutes each session

- Cost: The cost of court fees is typically a few dozen yuan per session. This amounts to roughly 2 hours of activity per week. The main challenge is arranging enough participants and securing a court, plus maintaining a weekly routine.
- In plain terms: People who regularly play tennis, badminton, or table tennis have about half the risk of dying during the same period compared to non-players. Their risk of cardiovascular death is roughly 56% lower. Swimmers show a similar benefit, with a roughly 28% lower risk. No significant difference was observed between runners and football players in this dataset.

Racket sports in this context include tennis, badminton, and table tennis. For swimmers, these figures were 0.72 and 0.59 respectively; for aerobic exercise enthusiasts they were 0.73 and 0.64, and for cyclists 0.85. No statistically significant differences were found between runners and football players.

- Notes: This finding is somewhat controversial. Such studies rely on observational tracking rather than controlled trials. ### 16. Accumulating brief bouts of strenuous activity like stair climbing or brisk walking to total 4–5 minutes per day
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=大 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: There is no cost involved, nor is any extra workout time required. The challenge lies in remembering to climb stairs or walk briskly instead of taking shortcuts.
- In plain terms: For individuals who normally do not exercise at all, engaging in three brief bouts of strenuous activity each day—each lasting one to two minutes—reduces their overall risk of death by roughly 40% compared to those who do none. Their risk of cardiovascular death drops by nearly half. Such strenuous activities include stair climbing and brisk walking.
- Benefit: A study conducted by the UK Biobank involved 25,200 participants who did not engage in regular exercise. After an average follow-up period of 6.9 years, 852 participants passed away. Those who performed three short bursts of strenuous activity daily—each lasting 1–2 minutes—showed a 38%–40% reduction in overall mortality risk and cancer-related death risk compared to those who did no such activity. Their risk of cardiovascular death fell by 48%–49%. For participants who accumulated 4.4 minutes of such activity daily, the reductions were 26%–30% for overall mortality and cancer death risk, and 32%–34% for cardiovascular death risk.
- Evidence grade: A
- Notes: This finding remains somewhat controversial. While this study utilized wearable devices to accurately measure physical activity levels—making it more reliable than self-reported questionnaire data—it still relied on observational tracking rather than a randomized controlled trial, and the follow-up period was only 6.9 years. Moreover, these results apply only to individuals who currently do not exercise; they may not hold true for people already engaged in regular physical activity.

### 17. Doing 30–60 minutes of strength training per week
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=中 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: Bodyweight squats and push-ups cost nothing. Aim for 1–2 sessions per week, each lasting 20–30 minutes. The real challenge is fitting this routine into your weekly schedule.
- In plain terms: People who perform 30–60 minutes of strength training each week have a 10–20% lower risk of dying during that period compared to those who don’t train at all. Training beyond this amount does not bring any extra benefit. Combining strength work with cardio yields the best results.
- Benefit: When multiple observational studies are pooled together (without subgroup analysis), individuals who engage in strength training show a 10%–17% reduction in mortality risk relative to non‑trainers. The greatest drop — roughly 10%–20% — occurs at around 30–60 minutes of weekly training; more time does not further lower the risk. Those who also do aerobic exercise enjoy an even lower death risk than those who do neither type of activity.

### 18. Don’t sit for too long at a time — get up and move every now and then
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=大 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: There’s no cost involved; getting up a few times doesn’t take much time at all. The real challenge is that once you sit down, you tend to lose track of time, so setting a reminder is necessary.
- In plain terms: People who sit the longest have a 2.6 times higher chance of dying during the same period compared to those who sit the least. Sitting continuously for long stretches adds an extra layer of risk. However, if you get 60–75 minutes of moderate-intensity activity each day, this extra risk from prolonged sitting is largely offset.
- Benefit: A U.S.-based observational study used accelerometers to measure activity levels without grouping participants. It divided people into four groups based on total daily sitting time. Those in the group that sat the most had a 2.63 times higher mortality risk than those who sat the least (HR 2.63). When grouped by duration of continuous sitting, the group that sat the longest had a 1.96 times higher risk than those who sat the least (HR 1.96). This shows that both total daily sitting time and duration of continuous sitting are independently linked to risk. Another large-scale meta-analysis included millions of participants. Those who sat over 8 hours daily and did little to no exercise had a 1.59 times higher mortality risk compared to the most active group, which sat under 4 hours daily. Getting 60–75 minutes of moderate activity each day can cancel out the extra risk from prolonged sitting. Even in the most active group, those who sat over 8 hours daily still only had a HR of 1.04, showing no statistically significant difference.
- Evidence grade: A
- Notes: The key takeaway is that how much you move matters more than how long you sit — when activity levels are sufficient, the risks of prolonged sitting largely disappear. Keep in mind these studies are purely observational; many of those who sit the most already have underlying health issues, so part of the “2.63 times higher risk” figure reflects pre-existing conditions. Additionally, the same meta-analysis found that watching TV for over 3 hours daily increases risk across all activity levels; for the most active group, this threshold rises to 5 hours.

### 19. Eat less processed meat (ham, bacon, sausages, luncheon meat)

- In plain terms: People who eat the most ham, bacon, sausages, and similar processed meats have a roughly 20% higher chance of dying during the same period compared to those who eat the least. For every extra serving of processed meat consumed daily, that risk rises by another 20%.

### 20. Drink less or no alcohol
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=大 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: There is no cost at all — it saves money and time. The only difficulty is feeling a bit awkward when refusing drinks in social settings.
- In plain terms: After consuming 100 g of pure alcohol per week, the more you drink, the earlier you’re likely to die. 100 g of pure alcohol equals roughly 2.5 L of beer. At age 40, people who drink 100–200 g per week lose about half a year of life expectancy; those drinking 200–350 g lose 1–2 years; and those drinking over 350 g lose 4–5 years. Overall, the most beneficial amount is to drink none at all.
- Benefit: An analysis of 83 follow-up studies involving 600,000 drinkers showed that the lowest death risk occurs when weekly alcohol intake stays at or below 100 g of pure alcohol — roughly 2.5 L of beer at 5% alcohol content, or 300 mL of 40-proof liquor. At age 40, drinking 100–200 g per week shortens life expectancy by about 6 months; 200–350 g shortens it by 1–2 years; and over 350 g shortens it by 4–5 years. The Global Burden of Disease Study 2016 also identifies zero alcohol consumption as the safest level overall. One adjusted analysis found that daily intake of 1.3–24 g of alcohol correlates with a 7% lower death risk (RR 0.93) compared to lifelong abstainers, though this difference is not statistically significant. Daily intake of 45–64 g raises risk by 19% (RR 1.19), while 65 g or more raises it by 35% (RR 1.35).
- Evidence grade: A
- Notes: This topic remains controversial. Proponents of the “moderate drinking is beneficial” view cite a meta-analysis of 34 long-term studies showing that light drinkers have up to 17–18% lower overall mortality rates; this benefit is observed at up to 4 drinks per day for men and 2 drinks per day for women. Critics argue that the “non-drinker” group includes people who quit drinking due to illness or those with pre-existing health issues, which skews results. After adjusting for such factors, the protective effect of moderate drinking disappears. Given all evidence, the safest conclusion is that drinking less does not meaningfully extend life, while drinking more definitely shortens it. There is no scientific basis for starting to drink for health reasons. For tips on cutting back, see section 22; individuals who drink daily should not attempt to quit abruptly — see section 21 for guidance.

### 21. People who drink daily and experience tremors and anxiety when they stop should not try to quit on their own
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=少 时间=中 毅力=些 收益=大 口径=死亡率 -->

- Cost: Seeing a psychiatrist or addiction specialist costs just a few to several dozen dollars. In severe cases, a few days of hospitalization may be needed.
- In plain terms: Individuals who consume large amounts of alcohol every day can suffer from withdrawal symptoms when they suddenly stop. Mild cases involve tremors, sweating, anxiety, and insomnia; severe cases may lead to seizures or delirium tremens — a life-threatening emergency. A Norwegian study followed over 30,000 people and found that 8% of those who had experienced delirium tremens died each year thereafter. If you wish to quit, consult a doctor first and clearly describe how much you drink daily.
- Benefit: A 2026 clinical review confirms that chronic heavy drinkers often develop withdrawal syndrome after abruptly cutting back or stopping entirely. Symptoms include tremors, autonomic hyperactivity, anxiety, insomnia, perceptual disturbances, seizures, and delirium tremens — conditions recognized as “common and potentially fatal” in general medical practice. Norway’s national registry included 36,287 individuals diagnosed with alcohol dependence, withdrawal states, or delirium tremens between 2009 and 2015. The annual mortality rate was 8.0% among those with delirium tremens, 5.0% among those in withdrawal states, and 3.6% among alcohol-dependent individuals. The standardized mortality ratio for the delirium tremens group was 9.8 (95% CI 8.9–10.7), meaning their risk of death was roughly 9.8 times higher than that of age-matched peers in the general population.
- Evidence grade: B
- Notes: How to tell if you’re at risk: If you drink almost every day, experience tremors, sweating, anxiety, or insomnia after a single day without alcohol, or feel better after having a drink in the morning, you should not attempt to quit cold turkey. Standard medical treatment involves using benzodiazepines to manage early withdrawal days and supplementing with thiamine (vitamin B1). This evidence grade is set at B because the Norwegian data describe outcomes among people who already suffered from delirium tremens rather than directly testing outcomes of self-directed abstinence; thus, some inference is required. This information is not intended to encourage continued heavy drinking; rather, it emphasizes safer methods for quitting. For guidance on moderate drinking levels, see item 20; for strategies to reduce intake, see item 22, which recommends tracking daily consumption first and then consulting a physician.

### 22. Want to drink less? First tally your weekly intake, then chat briefly with a doctor.
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=小 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: No cost at all. Simply count the number of drinks each day and jot it down. Asking your doctor about this during a routine visit costs nothing extra.
- In plain terms: A doctor can spend a few minutes reviewing your drinking habits, explaining the risks, and helping you set a target. After a year, people typically cut their weekly intake of pure alcohol by 20 grams — roughly half a liter of beer. Longer consultations do not bring any extra benefits. For those already struggling to quit, two medications have proven effective abroad: on average, one extra person out of every 12 treated stops drinking excessively after taking them.

Regarding medications: 122 trials with 22,803 participants indicate that acamprosate helps one extra person out of every 12 treated (95% CI: 8–26) to abstain from any drinking; oral naltrexone at 50 mg daily achieves the same result for one extra person out of every 12 (95% CI: 8–26).

- Notes: The benefit is classified as “small” because both sets of findings measure alcohol intake rather than mortality; per our guidelines, outcomes based on surrogate endpoints receive this rating. The drug trials were conducted mainly abroad, with participants often meeting criteria for alcohol dependence and receiving psychosocial support alongside medication; the reported figures represent the additional benefit above that baseline. Whether these medications are approved for use in China and can be prescribed depends on your doctor and the official drug labels — do not purchase them online without professional guidance. If you experience withdrawal symptoms, refer to item 21 in this section (do not attempt to quit on your own).

### 23. Eating a small handful of nuts each day
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=少 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=大 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: Consuming 28 grams daily costs roughly 200–300 yuan per year. It’s easy to fit into your routine without taking extra time.
- In plain terms: People who regularly eat nuts have about 20% lower chances of dying during the same period compared to those who don’t. Even eating nuts just once a week makes a difference — mortality risk drops by roughly 10%. The greatest benefit is seen when nuts are eaten daily.
- Benefit: Two large U.S. studies tracked over 119,000 participants for many years, covering more than 3 million person-years. Compared to non-nut eaters: those consuming nuts less than once a week saw a 7% reduction in death risk (hazard ratio 0.93, 95% CI 0.90–0.96). Those eating nuts once a week had a 11% lower risk (0.89, 0.86–0.93). For consumption of 2–4 times weekly, risk dropped 13% (0.87, 0.83–0.90); 5–6 times weekly, 15% lower risk (0.85, 0.79–0.91). Daily consumption resulted in roughly 20% lower risk (0.80, 0.73–0.86).
- Evidence grade: A
- Notes: Choose unsalted, plain nuts. They’re calorie-dense, so avoid eating them freely as snacks. Keep in mind these studies only track long-term habits — nut eaters generally lead healthier lifestyles, so these figures should be interpreted with some caution.
- Sources:Bao Y, Han J, Hu FB, et al. (2013). Association of nut consumption with total and cause-specific mortality. New England Journal of Medicine, 369(21), 2001-2011. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1307352>

### 24. Swapping some red meat for fish or poultry
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=小 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: There’s no extra cost or time involved. You’re simply replacing one type of meat with another, not buying additional portions. The real challenge is adjusting your taste preferences.
- In plain terms: Eating two extra servings of processed or red meat each week raises the risk of death by roughly 3% compared to eating the same amount of fish or poultry. This effect is relatively small within this section, so it’s an easy swap to make without much effort.
- Benefit: Data from six U.S. studies involving nearly 30,000 participants show that eating two extra servings of processed meat weekly increases mortality risk by about 3% (hazard ratio 1.03; 95% CI 1.02–1.05). The same increase applies to unprocessed red meat (1.03; 1.01–1.05). In contrast, substituting poultry or fish for those portions lowers the risk to 0.99 (95% CI 0.97–1.02 and 0.97–1.01 respectively), with no statistically significant difference between the two.
- Evidence grade: A
- Notes: The impact of swapping just two servings per week is minimal, so don’t expect a dramatic increase in lifespan from this change. Its main value lies in helping you decide which type of meat to choose when buying meat products.
- Sources:Zhong VW, Van Horn L, Greenland P, et al. (2020). Associations of Processed Meat, Unprocessed Red Meat, Poultry, or Fish Intake With Incident Cardiovascular Disease and All-Cause Mortality. JAMA Internal Medicine, 180(4), 503-512. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2019.6969>

### 25. Swap some refined rice and flour for whole grains
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=少 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=大 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: Whole grain options like brown rice, oats, and whole wheat flour cost slightly more than refined varieties and require no extra preparation time. The main challenge is getting used to their different taste and texture.
- In plain terms: People who consume an extra 90 g of whole grains daily have a roughly 17% lower risk of dying during the same period. That amount equals about three servings. The risk continues to drop when intake reaches around 200 g per day.
- Benefit: Pooled data from multiple observational studies show that eating an additional 90 g of whole grains each day lowers mortality risk by about 17% (RR 0.83). This benefit stays evident up to roughly 210–225 g per day. Another analysis found that each extra daily serving reduces risk by about 8% (RR 0.92).
- Evidence grade: A
- Notes: You do not need to replace all refined grains with whole grains; switching to half is already within the range where benefits are most pronounced. Keep in mind these studies are purely observational, so participants who ate more whole grains tended to be healthier overall, which may inflate the numbers. There is also considerable variation across studies, as shown by an I² value of 83% (higher values indicate greater inconsistency in results).
- Sources:Aune D 等 (2016). ### 26. Drinking tea more than three times a week
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=少 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: It costs only a few dozen to a few hundred yuan per year. Brewing a cup takes just a few minutes, so it doesn’t take up much time at all.
- In plain terms: People who regularly drink tea have a roughly 15% lower chance of dying during the same period. Starting at age 50, they can expect to live 1.26 years longer on average, and 1.41 extra years without suffering from cardiovascular disease.
- Benefit: A large Chinese study called China-PAR involved over 100,900 participants, with half of them tracked for 7.3 years. Those with a regular tea-drinking habit showed a 15% lower risk of death (hazard ratio 0.85, 95% CI 0.79–0.90, which is a reliable range). Starting at age 50, these individuals gain 1.41 extra years free from atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease and 1.26 extra years of life overall.
- Evidence grade: A
- Notes: There are some caveats to keep in mind. This type of study relies on observational data, and Chinese men who drink tea tend to smoke and drink more than average. Researchers have adjusted for some of these factors, but not all of them. Also, avoid drinking tea that is piping hot; please refer to the section on hot drink temperatures for more details. Some people worry about mycotoxins in tea. A test of 158 samples of dark tea found aflatoxin in just two of them; even when accounting for typical tea consumption, the intake level remains well below internationally accepted safety limits. Another study examined 352 tea samples for 16 different mycotoxins and found that only ochratoxin A in dark tea exceeded the permitted limit on average. Given how much tea most Chinese people drink, neither study indicated any dietary risk. Store tea in a dry place and discard any bags that have become damp or moldy. Tea extract capsules are a different story: consuming large amounts of concentrated catechins at once can damage the liver, but this risk does not apply to regular brewed tea. Therefore, this recommendation applies only to brewed tea — do not substitute it with tea extract supplements.
- Sources:Wang X, Liu F, Li J, et al. (2020). Tea consumption and the risk of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease and all-cause mortality: The China-PAR project. European Journal of Preventive Cardiology, 27(18), 1956-1963. <https://doi.org/10.1177/2047487319894685>；茶叶霉菌毒素：Cui P 等 (2020). Quantitative analysis and dietary risk assessment of aflatoxins in Chinese post-fermented dark tea. Food and Chemical Toxicology. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.fct.2020.111830>；Zhou H 等 (2022). ### 27. Drinking three to four cups of coffee daily, without sugar or cream
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=少 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=大 口径=死亡率 -->

- Cost: Making coffee at home costs just one or two yuan per day. It takes only a few minutes to brew each cup, so it doesn’t take much time at all.
- In plain terms: People who drink three to four cups of coffee each day have a roughly 17% lower chance of dying during the same period compared to those who don’t drink coffee at all.
- Benefit: One review combined results from 201 separate studies; these original studies simply recorded data without any subgroup analysis. The overall finding was that daily coffee drinkers (3–4 cups) faced a 17% lower mortality risk than non-drinkers (relative risk 0.83, 95% CI 0.79–0.88 — a fairly reliable range).
- Evidence grade: A
- Notes: There is some debate here: the authors note that almost all evidence comes from observational tracking data, and they point out that “reliable randomized controlled trials are needed to confirm a causal link.” In other words, to prove cause and effect we’d need to randomly assign people to drink or not drink coffee. For this benefit to apply, coffee should be consumed black; adding sugar or cream would negate these advantages. This recommendation does not apply to pregnant women, or to people suffering from arrhythmia, anxiety, or insomnia.
- Sources:Poole R, Kennedy OJ, Roderick P, et al. (2017). Coffee consumption and health: umbrella review of meta-analyses of multiple health outcomes. BMJ, 359, j5024. <https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.j5024>

### 28. Eating 5 servings (about 400 g) of fruits and vegetables daily
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=少 时间=中 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: It costs only a few to a dozen yuan per day. You also need to spend some time washing and chopping them. The real challenge is buying and eating the required amount every single day.
- In plain terms: Consuming an extra 200 g of fruits and vegetables daily lowers the chance of death by roughly 10% during the same period. People who eat 5 servings daily have a mortality risk that is about 13% lower than those who eat only 2 servings. Eating more than that does not bring any further reduction in risk.
- Benefit: When multiple long‑term studies are pooled together (without any subgroup analysis), an additional 200 g of fruits and vegetables per day reduces the overall risk of death by about 10% (RR 0.90). This protective effect persists up to 800 g per day. A combined analysis of two large U.S. cohort studies and 26 other studies shows that individuals consuming 5 servings daily have a mortality risk that is roughly 13% lower than those eating just 2 servings (HR 0.87). The optimal mix is 2 servings of fruit and 3 servings of vegetables; any extra amount beyond that does not lower the risk further.
- Evidence grade: A
- Notes: Eating 5 servings is sufficient; there is no need to reach 10 servings, as both studies agree on this point. Keep in mind that these studies are purely observational, so many confounding factors are present: people who eat more vegetables generally have higher incomes, better education, and more physical activity. Therefore, the reported “10% reduction in risk” represents an upper limit rather than an exact figure.
- Sources:Aune D 等 (2017). ### 29. Eat fewer ultra-processed foods (chips, instant noodles, pastries, ready meals)
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=多 毅力=是 收益=大 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: There’s no direct monetary cost, though you’ll need to spend time shopping for different options. Preparing meals at home or selecting minimally processed ingredients also takes daily effort. The real challenge is overcoming the long-standing habit of opting for convenience over healthier choices.
- In plain terms: People who consume the most ultra-processed foods have roughly a 20% higher risk of dying during the same period compared to those who eat the least. Their risk of cardiovascular-related death is about 50% higher. However, this evidence is rated as uncertain, so these figures should be interpreted with caution.
- Benefit: One review combined results from multiple studies, even though those original studies did not group participants by ultra-processed food intake. The findings show that individuals eating more ultra-processed foods face a 21% higher risk of death (RR 1.21) and a 50% higher risk of cardiovascular death (RR 1.50) compared to those eating less. These results are classified as “highly suggestive” and “convincing” respectively. Yet, when assessed via the standard GRADE criteria, the overall certainty of this evidence is low or very low.
- Evidence grade: A
- Notes: There are three main points of debate. First, the NOVA classification groups foods with vastly different nutritional profiles into the same category. Second, ultra-processed foods overlap heavily with sugary drinks and processed meats (topics covered in items 7 and 19), making it hard to isolate which factor is responsible for the observed effects. Third, GRADE ratings for this evidence are also low. Consequently, it’s unclear how much additional benefit this measure provides after already following the recommendations in items 7 and 19. The opposing evidence is simply the low GRADE rating from the same review; no original studies have produced contradictory results to date. For now, focus on avoiding sugary drinks and limiting processed meat intake before considering further reductions in ultra-processed food consumption.
- Sources:Lane MM 等 (2024). Ultra-processed food exposure and adverse health outcomes: umbrella review of epidemiological meta-analyses. BMJ. <https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2023-077310>

### 30. Switching from coal and wood to electricity or gas for cooking and heating
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=多 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=大 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: Rural households need to replace their stoves and pay higher fuel costs; this adds several hundred to over a thousand yuan per year. The switch is a one‑time effort and doesn’t require ongoing time investment. In cities, most people already use electricity or gas.
- In plain terms: People who cook with coal or wood have a roughly 10% higher chance of dying during the same period; those who heat their homes with coal or wood have a roughly 14% higher risk. Those who have already switched to electricity or gas face a 13%–33% lower risk of death compared to those still using coal or wood.
- Benefit: A Chinese longitudinal study tracked 271,000 adults without cardiovascular disease, recording outcomes without any grouping. People who cooked with solid fuels such as coal or wood had an 11% higher risk of death than those using electricity or gas (HR 1.11). Those who used solid fuels for heating had a 14% higher risk (HR 1.14). Individuals who switched from solid fuels to cleaner alternatives saw their cooking‑related risk drop by about 13% (HR 0.87) and their heating‑related risk drop by about 33% (HR 0.67). Outdoor PM2.5 exposure is a separate issue. When data from 104 studies are pooled, each additional 10 µg/m³ of long‑term PM2.5 exposure raises the risk of death from natural causes by roughly 8% (RR 1.08). Natural causes refer to non‑accidental deaths.
- Evidence grade: A
- Notes: These studies merely provide observational data. Households that switch fuels often have higher incomes, so part of the “risk dropping to 0.67 times” effect may be due to socioeconomic factors. Regarding outdoor PM2.5, individual actions are limited to moving, wearing masks, or using air purifiers. No studies have examined mortality rates in relation to air purifiers, so no quantitative figures are given here.
- Sources:Yu K 等 (2018). Association of Solid Fuel Use With Risk of Cardiovascular and All-Cause Mortality in Rural China. JAMA. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2018.2151>；Chen J, Hoek G (2020). Long-term exposure to PM and all-cause and cause-specific mortality: A systematic review and meta-analysis. Environment International. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.envint.2020.105974>

### 31. Let hot drinks cool down before drinking; avoid tea, soup, and coffee that are piping hot
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=大 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: No money is required; you just need to wait two to three extra minutes each time. The hard part is resisting the urge to drink when you’re craving a hot drink.
- In plain terms: Drinking tea that is extremely hot raises your risk of esophageal cancer by 8 times compared to drinking lukewarm tea. Even drinking tea that is merely “hot” doubles this risk. If you drink tea less than two minutes after pouring it, your risk is 5 times higher than if you wait at least four minutes to drink it.
- Benefit: In a high-risk esophageal cancer region in northern Iran, researchers compared 300 patients with esophageal cancer to 571 healthy individuals. All results below are compared against people who drink lukewarm tea. Those who drink “hot” tea have roughly 2.07 times the risk of developing squamous cell esophageal cancer (95% CI: 1.28–3.35). For those who drink “extremely hot” tea, this risk rises to 8.16 times (95% CI: 3.93–16.9). People who drink tea less than two minutes after pouring it have 5.41 times the risk of developing esophageal cancer compared to those who wait at least four minutes to drink it (95% CI: 2.63–11.1).
- Evidence grade: A
- Notes: The International Agency for Research on Cancer classifies hot drinks above 65°C as Group 2A carcinogens, meaning they are likely to cause cancer. The same assessment also confirms that coffee itself does not cause cancer. In China, high rates of esophageal cancer in regions such as Chaoshan and the Taihang Mountains are closely linked to the habit of drinking tea while it is still very hot.
- Sources:Islami F, Pourshams A, Nasrollahzadeh D, et al. (2009). Tea drinking habits and oesophageal cancer in a high risk area in northern Iran: population based case-control study. BMJ, 338, b929. <https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.b929>；Loomis D, Guyton KZ, Grosse Y, et al. (2016). Carcinogenicity of drinking coffee, mate, and very hot beverages. ### 32. Getting some sun during the day instead of staying indoors all the time
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: No cost at all. Just a few minutes each day — during your commute or lunch break is enough.
- In plain terms: Women who deliberately avoid sunlight can expect to live 0.6–2.1 years less than those who get the most sun exposure. The original text also notes that non-smokers who avoid sunlight tend to have similar life expectancies to smokers who get plenty of sun.
- Benefit: A Swedish study followed nearly 29,500 women over 20 years. Compared to the group getting the most sun, those who actively avoided sunlight had a lower life expectancy by 0.6 to 2.1 years. The authors concluded that “non-smokers who avoid sunlight have life expectancies comparable to smokers in the group getting the most sun exposure.”
- Evidence grade: B
- Notes: There are some caveats: Sweden has high latitudes and limited sunlight, so these findings may not directly apply to China. It’s also possible that people avoiding sunlight tend to be less physically active or prefer staying indoors. Also, avoid getting sunburned — use protection during peak midday sunlight, as excessive sun exposure raises skin cancer risk.

### 33. Keep your BMI between 20 and 25; lose weight if it’s higher.
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=多 毅力=是 收益=大 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: There’s no monetary cost, but diet and exercise require daily effort. This is the toughest step in the whole chapter: staying consistent with healthy eating and regular activity is harder than losing weight in the first place.
- In plain terms: The chance of dying is lowest when BMI stays in the 20–25 range. At BMI 27.5–30, the risk rises by roughly 20%; at 30–35 it goes up by about 45%; at 35–40 it nearly doubles. People of East Asian descent are more sensitive to being overweight — for every 5‑point increase in BMI, their risk climbs by roughly 39%.
- Benefit: This conclusion comes from pooling individual data from 239 long‑term studies (no subgroup analysis was done). Only three groups of participants were kept for the final analysis: non‑smokers, those without chronic illnesses at enrollment, and people who survived at least five years after joining the study. The results show that the lowest mortality risk occurs at BMI 20–25. At BMI 25–27.5 the risk is about 7% higher (HR 1.07); at 27.5–30 it’s 20% higher (HR 1.20); at 30–35 it’s 45% higher (HR 1.45); at 35–40 it’s 94% higher (HR 1.94); and at 40–60 it’s 2.76 times higher (HR 2.76). For East Asian individuals, each additional 5 kg/m² of BMI raises the risk by roughly 39% (HR 1.39).
- Evidence grade: A
- Notes: This topic is debated. Some researchers argue that being slightly overweight actually extends life; their pooled data show a roughly 6% lower death risk for overweight people and a 5% lower risk for those with mild obesity. The main disagreement is whether to exclude three types of data: smokers, participants with pre‑existing illnesses, and those who died within the first few years of the study. Those groups are excluded because seriously ill patients tend to lose weight quickly. When these three groups were removed from the analysis of the 239 studies, the higher‑BMI mortality risk reappeared. It’s also important to note that no randomized trials have proven that weight loss itself lowers overall mortality. The benefit figures above are based on comparisons between people of different weights; they shouldn’t be taken as a guarantee that losing weight will directly bring those risk reductions. Those aiming to lose weight don’t need to overhaul their breakfast habits — neither regular breakfasts nor the 16:8 intermittent fasting method offers any extra advantage; see Section 6, Item 26 for more details.
- Sources:Global BMI Mortality Collaboration (2016). Body-mass index and all-cause mortality: individual-participant-data meta-analysis of 239 prospective studies in four continents. Lancet. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(16)30175-1>；Flegal KM 等 (2013). Association of all-cause mortality with overweight and obesity using standard body mass index categories: a systematic review and meta-analysis. JAMA. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2012.113905>（争议方）

- In plain terms: People who eat chili peppers more than four times a week have a roughly 23% lower chance of dying during the same period compared to those who rarely eat them; their risk of dying from cardiovascular causes is about one‑third lower.

### 35. Drinking one or two servings of milk or yogurt daily
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=少 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=大 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: Only a few yuan per day, with no extra time required.
- In plain terms: People who consume two or more servings of milk or yogurt each day have a roughly 17% lower chance of dying during the same period compared to those who do not drink any.
- Benefit: The PURE study followed over 136,000 participants across 21 countries for an average of 9.1 years; 6,796 of them passed away. Those who ate two or more dairy servings daily showed a 17% lower mortality risk than non-consumers (hazard ratio 0.83). The 95% confidence interval for this figure is 0.72–0.96, with a trend significance level of P=0.0052.

### 36. Eggs don’t need to be avoided, but don’t eat three or four daily  
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=小 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: No cost and no extra time required.  
- In plain terms: Eating an extra half whole egg each day raises the overall risk of death by roughly 7% over time. Swapping that half egg for egg whites, fish, poultry, or nuts actually lowers the risk of death across all categories. There’s no need to cut eggs out entirely; just avoid consuming three or four every day.  
- Benefit: A U.S. study tracking over 521,000 people found that 129,000 participants died during the follow-up period. Those who ate an extra half whole egg daily saw a 7% higher risk of death (hazard ratio 1.07, 95% CI 1.06–1.08). Replacing that half egg with an equal amount of egg whites, poultry, fish, dairy, or nuts and legumes further reduced death risk, especially for cardiovascular disease, cancer, and respiratory conditions.  
- Evidence grade: B  
- Notes: This topic remains highly debated: other large long-term studies and meta-analyses have found no link between egg consumption and mortality. The U.S. Dietary Guidelines have also removed any daily cholesterol limit. This recommendation specifically advises against eating three or four eggs daily; one egg per day poses no cause for concern.
- Sources:Zhuang P, Wu F, Mao L, et al. (2021). Egg and cholesterol consumption and mortality from cardiovascular and different causes in the United States: A population-based cohort study. PLoS Medicine, 18(2), e1003508. <https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pmed.1003508>

### 37. Take baths when you can — don’t rely on showers alone
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=少 时间=中 毅力=否 收益=大 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: It costs a bit more in water and electricity; each bath takes about half an hour.
- In plain terms: People who bathe almost daily have roughly 28% lower risk of cardiovascular events and about 46% lower risk of brain hemorrhage compared to those who bathe fewer than twice a week.
- Benefit: A Japanese study involving 30,000 participants followed for 19 years recorded 2,097 cardiovascular events. Those who bathed almost daily showed roughly 28% lower risk of cardiovascular events than those bathing no more than twice a week (hazard ratio 0.72, 95% CI 0.62–0.84). Their risk of coronary heart disease was about 35% lower (0.65, 0.45–0.94), stroke risk about 26% lower (0.74, 0.62–0.87), and brain hemorrhage risk roughly 46% lower (0.54, 0.40–0.73).
- Evidence grade: B
- Notes: These findings apply to Japanese populations with a strong bathtub culture; most Chinese households lack the conditions for daily baths. Also, water should not be too hot and baths should not last too long. For older adults and those with cardiovascular disease, excessively hot water or prolonged soaking can be dangerous — in Japan, many elderly people die suddenly in baths each year.
- Sources:Ukai T, Iso H, Yamagishi K, et al. (2020). Habitual tub bathing and risks of incident coronary heart disease and stroke. Heart, 106(10), 732-737. <https://doi.org/10.1136/heartjnl-2019-315752>

### 38. Keep naps under 30 minutes — no longer than an hour. If you need one or two hours to feel rested, it’s time to get checked out.
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=死亡率 -->

- Cost: It costs nothing. Just set an alarm before you nap; you barely need any willpower at all.  
- In plain terms: People who nap for less than an hour show no increase in death risk or chronic disease risk, and their mental clarity actually improves. Those who nap longer than an hour have a roughly 30% higher risk of coronary heart disease, and about a 20% higher risk of diabetes and obesity. So set an alarm and wake up after half an hour. If you regularly need a one- or two-hour nap just to function, that’s a clear sign you should see a doctor.  
- Benefit: One umbrella review pulled together 16 meta-analyses covering 244 health outcomes. The bottom line: folks who nap under 60 minutes see no rise in overall mortality or chronic disease risk, and their cognitive function improves the most (SMD 0.69, 95% CI 0.37–1.00 — a reliable range). Naps lasting 20–30 minutes boost physical performance the most (SMD 0.99, 0.67–1.31). On the flip side, naps exceeding an hour raise coronary heart disease risk by roughly 30%, and diabetes/obesity risk by about 20%. Another study used wrist monitors on 1,338 seniors aged 56 and older, tracking them for up to 19 years; 926 participants died in that span. For every extra hour of napping, death risk rose by 13% (HR 1.13, 95% CI 1.04–1.23). Each additional daily nap added another 7% to that risk (HR 1.07, 1.02–1.13). Nappers in the morning faced a 30% higher risk than those who napped earlier in the afternoon (HR 1.30, 1.03–1.64).  
- Evidence grade: B  
- Notes: This area remains somewhat controversial. Both of the major studies cited here merely recorded nap duration without separating participants into groups. People who habitually nap longer often suffer from sleep apnea, anemia, hypothyroidism, depression, or other chronic conditions; thus it’s hard to isolate how much of the higher mortality is truly caused by napping itself. Two Mendelian randomization studies (involving 453,000 and 541,000 participants) found only one consistent link: the more often someone naps, the slightly higher their blood pressure and waist circumference become. No causal link to coronary disease or diabetes was detected. Consequently, don’t count “short naps” as a reliable way to lower mortality. The advice to “get checked out” is essentially the author’s recommendation and falls under Grade C: if daytime fatigue forces you to nap for long periods, first evaluate nighttime sleep quality; screen for sleep apnea if you snore or experience breathing pauses during sleep, and run blood tests plus thyroid checks. For quick energy boosts via short naps, see Section 3, Item 11 (a 10‑minute nap in the afternoon works wonders). Ideal nighttime sleep duration is covered in Section 13 of this chapter (aim for 7 hours per night).

### 39. Catch up on sleep the night after a late night — don’t wait until the weekend
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: No cost at all. Those extra hours of sleep you make up are exactly the ones you’d have wanted to get anyway.
- In plain terms: Over 80,000 people wore wristbands to track their daily sleep. Those who failed to make up for lost sleep the following night had a roughly 15% higher risk of death compared to people with regular sleep patterns. For those who lost far more sleep and still didn’t catch up, the risk rose by about 42%. In contrast, the two groups that did make up the lost sleep showed no elevated risk at all. So, if you stay up late, go to bed early the next night.
- Benefit: This study involved 85,618 participants in the UK Biobank, with an average age of 61.8 years. They wore wristbands to record daily sleep, and the follow-up period averaged 8 years. The researchers first identified “sleep‑deficit nights” for each person — nights when they slept more than 2.5 hours less than their personal need. The first night after such a deficit was defined as a “catch‑up night” if they slept more than required. Based on this, five distinct sleep patterns were identified. Compared to regular sleepers, those who lost sleep without catching up had a 15% higher overall mortality risk (HR 1.15, 95% CI 1.01–1.31). People who accumulated more than 3.5 hours of deficit and still didn’t make it up saw a 42% higher risk (HR 1.42, 1.24–1.63). Neither of the two groups that did catch up showed any significant risk increase; for the group that lost a moderate amount of sleep and then caught up, the HR was 1.12 (0.98–1.28). Among individuals who normally sleep less, not making up for lost sleep raised the risk by about 19% (HR 1.19, 1.01–1.40), while severe deficit without recovery increased it by roughly 38% (HR 1.38, 1.17–1.63). These findings were replicated in a separate U.S. health survey involving 4,586 participants.
- Evidence grade: B
- Notes: This was an observational study without any form of randomization. People who couldn’t make up lost sleep might simply have busier schedules or poorer health, and this factor wasn’t fully accounted for. The statement that “no risk increase was observed in the catch‑up groups” means no statistically significant difference was found; it does not mean there is zero risk, as the point estimates remained above 1. Therefore, the advice is to catch up on sleep as soon as possible after a late night, not to stay up late without any consequences. Two points must be distinguished: the “catch‑up” described here refers to adding extra sleep on the very next night, not saving it up for a weekend binge. Adopting a fixed weekly rhythm where weekdays involve sleep deficit and weekends involve massive catch‑up is a different phenomenon known as social jetlag, which itself is linked to cardiovascular disease — see item 13 in this section (aim for roughly 7 hours of sleep each night with consistent timing). The recommendation to wake up at the same time on weekends, mentioned in item 2 of Section 3, does not conflict with this advice; it simply encourages a regular wake‑time schedule. What this item stresses is going to bed early on the night following a late night, not sleeping until noon the next day.
- Sources:Li X, Zhang M, Li Z, 等 (2026). Acute sleep rebound following sleep restriction is associated with reduced mortality risk. Nature Communications. <https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-026-72461-1>

### 40. The longer you work night shifts, the higher your cardiovascular risk — consider switching jobs early if possible
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=多 时间=中 毅力=是 收益=大 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: Switching jobs or changing positions might mean a pay cut, and you’ll lose any night shift allowances. It also takes time and determination to find a new role. If you factor this cost into your decision when choosing a job, the actual cost is zero.
- In plain terms: People who work night shifts have roughly 13% more cardiovascular diseases and 27% more cardiovascular-related deaths than those who don’t. This risk rises the longer you work nights: for every additional 5 night shifts worked, the risk of cardiovascular disease goes up by about 7%. So, when calculating the long-term impact, it’s better to switch jobs as early as possible. As for claims linking night shifts to cancer, the evidence supporting them is far weaker than commonly believed.
- Benefit: A pooled analysis of 23 observational studies showed that night shift workers face roughly 13% higher risk of cardiovascular events (RR 1.13, 95% CI 1.10–1.16). Cardiovascular-related deaths also rise by about 27% (RR 1.27, 1.18–1.36). Over time, each extra 5 night shifts increases cardiovascular disease risk by roughly 7% (RR 1.07, 1.04–1.09) and cardiovascular deaths by about 5% (RR 1.05, 1.03–1.06). Specific conditions show similar trends: coronary heart disease risk rises by 22% (RR 1.22, 1.16–1.28), coronary heart disease deaths by 22% (RR 1.22, 1.10–1.36), ischemic heart disease deaths by 39% (RR 1.39, 1.06–1.84), and stroke deaths by 49% (RR 1.49, 1.04–2.12). The risk increase for stroke incidence was not statistically significant (RR 1.06, 0.95–1.18).
- Evidence grade: A
- Notes: All these findings come from observational studies without any subgroup analysis. Night shift workers also tend to differ from others in smoking habits, weight, and income, and these factors are not fully accounted for in the data. Evidence linking night shifts to cancer is much weaker than popular belief. A pooled analysis of 12 studies involving 12,132 breast cancer cases found only a 5% higher risk among night shift workers, with a confidence interval that includes 1 (RR 1.05, 0.96–1.14). The risk rose to 25% for those with 20+ years of night shift experience (RR 1.25, 1.01–1.55), but this figure lost statistical significance after accounting for publication bias, with the true association “far from established.” Another long-term study of 8,202 Chinese men over 16.1 years found no link between night shifts and overall cancer risk or most common cancer types; only pancreatic cancer risk rose by 59% for those with 11–20 years of night shift experience (HR 1.59, 1.09–2.31). Therefore, this entry focuses on cardiovascular risks rather than cancer. It does not advocate that everyone must leave night shift jobs, but simply lays out the long-term risk trends for personal consideration. For current night shift workers, several steps can help reduce risk: quit smoking as soon as possible (see item 1 in this section), control blood pressure and cholesterol per medical advice (see item 12), and make up for lost sleep after night shifts (see item 39). Two other strategies worth trying are eating most meals during daytime and resetting your circadian rhythm using bright light during the day and near-total darkness at night. The latter is the only proven method to shift circadian rhythm: working six consecutive night shifts under normal indoor lighting has no effect, but exposure to 7,000–12,000 lux of light during the day and near-total darkness at night flips the rhythm within four days. All three steps — bright nighttime light, strict daytime darkness for sleep, and wearing sunglasses on the way home to block morning light — are essential; skipping any one of them negates the benefits. Both strategies have only been tested for effects on metabolic markers and sleep quality, with no studies proving they reduce cardiovascular risk. Even if you follow these steps, returning to a regular daytime schedule on rest days will reset your circadian rhythm back to its original pattern. Full details on dosage, raw data, and how the body regulates circadian rhythm can be found in [docs/生物钟和夜班.md](../../docs/生物钟和夜班.md).
