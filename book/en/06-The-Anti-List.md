# 6. The anti-list

This section collects things that look cheap and effective. Once the original studies are opened, their benefit is close to zero, equal to zero, or in some cases negative.

The items are grouped by subject below. Item numbers are in parentheses.

**Supplements**: multivitamins (item 1), ordinary fish oil (item 2), vitamin D supplements (item 3), antioxidant supplements to prevent cancer (item 4), chondroitin for knee osteoarthritis (item 5), vitamin C to prevent colds (item 6), spending a lot on health products (item 10), taking probiotics long term (item 11), buying detox and colon-cleansing products (item 13), loading calcium after a fracture (item 30), relying on melatonin to cure long-term insomnia (item 34).

**Check-ups and small findings**: a whole-body PET-CT and a tumour-marker panel (item 7), a general check-up package (item 18), do not take medicine for high uric acid if it has never hurt (item 19), do not have a gallbladder stone removed if it has never hurt (item 20), do not give up calcium to prevent kidney stones (item 21), do not have arthroscopic clean-out for degenerative knee pain (item 33).

**Health sayings**: counting on a wristband to help you lose weight (item 8), buying organic food (item 9), cold showers to strengthen immunity (item 12), not forcing yourself to drink water (item 14), blue-light glasses (item 16), treating blood donation as health care (item 17), breakfast or intermittent fasting to control weight (item 26), counting on a correct lifting posture to prevent low-back pain (item 29), demanding an IV drip for a cold or fever (item 35).

**Spending money on luck and on mood**: paying for fortune-telling and horoscopes (item 15), buying crystals, pixiu, and similar objects (item 22), buying things to improve your mood (item 23), spending more in order to move up a tier (item 24), not saving because you think people can die at any time (item 32).

**Willpower and sex**: willpower is like a muscle (item 25), masturbation harms the body (item 27), conversion of sexual orientation (item 28).

**Answers from AI**: judging your own illness with an AI chatbot (item 31).
### 1. Don’t take multivitamins just to live longer or protect your heart and blood vessels  
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: About 0.5–2 yuan per day, or 200–700 yuan per year. Taking them takes almost no time — just one pill each day. Over time, though, people can become dependent, feeling they’ve missed out on something important if they skip a day.  
- In plain terms: 14,400 male doctors took multivitamins for 11 years. The number of serious heart or blood vessel problems and deaths among them was no different from those who didn’t take them. When nine studies involving over 50,000 people were combined, there was still no effect on overall mortality. Skipping multivitamins saves you 200–700 yuan each year. This does not apply to people who truly lack certain nutrients or pregnant women needing folic acid.  
- Benefit: The PHS II trial followed 14,641 male doctors for a median of 11.2 years. Both groups — those taking multivitamins and those who didn’t — showed the same rate of major cardiovascular events. The hazard ratio was 1.01 (meaning no real difference), with a 95% confidence interval of 0.91–1.10. Similarly, overall mortality rates were identical, with a hazard ratio of 0.94 (95% CI 0.88–1.02). In 2022, USPSTF evaluated nine randomized controlled trials involving 51,550 participants and concluded there is no link between multivitamin use and reduced mortality; they assigned it a Grade I rating, meaning evidence is insufficient to draw any firm conclusions. The idea that multivitamins are a good deal stems partly from the convenience of “one pill for all nutrients” and their low daily cost.  
- Evidence grade: A  
- Sources:Sesso HD et al. (2012). Multivitamins in the prevention of cardiovascular disease in men: the Physicians' Health Study II randomized controlled trial. JAMA. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2012.14805>；US Preventive Services Task Force (2022). Vitamin, Mineral, and Multivitamin Supplementation to Prevent Cardiovascular Disease and Cancer. JAMA. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2022.8970>；Gaziano JM 等 (2012). Multivitamins in the Prevention of Cancer in Men. JAMA. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2012.14641>（备注里那项癌症结果）
- Notes: A point of debate: among these same male doctors, cancer rates were slightly lower among multivitamin users — about 8% fewer cases (95% CI 0.86–0.998, P=0.04, just barely significant). Deaths from cancer were also reduced by roughly 12% (95% CI 0.77–1.01), though this range includes 1, so it may simply be a coincidence. Any such benefit is very small and has only been observed in male doctors. It does not apply to people with proven nutrient deficiencies, pregnant women needing folic acid, or those with extremely poor diets. 〔14000〕

### 2. Don’t take regular fish oil just to prevent heart disease  
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: Roughly 1–3 yuan per day, or 400–1,000 yuan per year. It requires no extra time to take.  
- In plain terms: Two large trials enrolled 26,000 and 15,000 people with diabetes respectively. Those who took 1 gram of fish oil daily showed no difference from non-users in terms of major cardiovascular events or death rates. The fact that eating fish is healthy does not mean fish oil capsules are equally beneficial. Skipping them can save you 400–1,000 yuan each year.  
- Benefit: The VITAL trial followed 25,871 participants for 5.3 years; those taking 1 gram of fish oil daily showed no difference in major cardiovascular events or mortality compared to non-users. The hazard ratio for major events was 0.92 (95% CI 0.80–1.06), well below 1, indicating no benefit. All-cause mortality showed a similar pattern (HR 1.02, 95% CI 0.90–1.15). The ASCEND trial, involving 15,480 diabetic patients over 7.4 years, also found no difference in severe vascular events (RR 0.97, 95% CI 0.87–1.08) or mortality (RR 0.95, 95% CI 0.86–1.05). These results appear favorable only because many assume “eating fish is healthy” equates to “fish oil capsules are beneficial.”  
- Evidence grade: A  
- Sources:Manson JE et al. (2019). Marine n-3 Fatty Acids and Prevention of Cardiovascular Disease and Cancer. NEJM. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1811403>；ASCEND Study Collaborative Group (2018). Effects of n-3 Fatty Acid Supplements in Diabetes Mellitus. NEJM. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1804989>；Bhatt DL 等 (2019). Cardiovascular Risk Reduction with Icosapent Ethyl for Hypertriglyceridemia. New England Journal of Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1812792>（备注里那项高纯度处方鱼油试验）
- Notes: Controversy exists: One trial did show benefits. The REDUCE-IT trial included 8,179 high-risk patients with existing cardiovascular disease or diabetes, already on statins and with elevated triglyceride levels (135–499 mg/dL). Those taking 4 grams of prescription-grade pure EPA daily saw a 25% lower risk of events (HR 0.75, 95% CI 0.68–0.83). However, this was a high-dose prescription medication for a specific high-risk group, not ordinary fish oil supplements for the general population. This discussion excludes the health benefits of eating fish itself. 〔17.2〕

### 3. Don’t give vitamin D supplements for longevity to people who are not deficient
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: About 0.2 to 0.5 yuan a day, or 100 to 200 yuan a year. The money is not much; the real cost is thinking you have already done prevention.
- In plain terms: People who were not deficient and then supplemented for 5 years had the same numbers of cancers, cardiovascular disease, and deaths as those who did not. Adults who rarely go out and get little sun may take 400 IU a day and should not exceed 2000 IU a day. At high doses, bone density falls more and falls increase. Fatigue alone does not mean vitamin D deficiency; if you have been tired for weeks with no clear reason, see a doctor.
- Benefit: The VITAL trial enrolled 25871 people who took 2000 IU daily and followed them for a median of 5.3 years. Cancer, cardiovascular disease, and death showed no difference. For cancers that spread, HR was 0.96 (HR is a hazard ratio; 1 means the two sides match; 95% CI 0.88–1.06 is the credible range). For major cardiovascular events, HR was 0.97 (0.85–1.12). For all-cause death, HR was 0.99 (0.87–1.12). The D-Health trial enrolled 21315 Australians aged 60 and above who took 60000 IU monthly and followed them for 5 years. That trial also showed no difference: all-cause death HR 1.04 (0.93–1.18). Death rates were 5.3% versus 5.1%. It looks worthwhile because some studies only record and never intervene, find that people with low vitamin D die more often, and that is misread as “supplementing works.” People who rarely go out have a separate recommendation. The UK Department of Health advises adults who rarely go out, live in care homes, or cover most of their skin when outdoors to take 10 micrograms of vitamin D daily year-round. The NHS states that 1 microgram equals 40 IU, so 10 micrograms is 400 IU. China’s health industry standard recommends 10 micrograms a day for adults aged 18 to 64 and 15 micrograms for those 65 and above. The same standard sets the tolerable upper intake at 50 micrograms a day (2000 IU), because too much raises blood calcium. The NHS adult upper limit is wider: 100 micrograms (4000 IU). Fatigue has many causes; the NHS lists insufficient sleep, stress and depression, iron-deficiency anemia, diabetes, hyperthyroidism, and sleep apnea. It advises seeing a doctor if fatigue lasts several weeks without a clear reason, or if it interferes with daily life. More is not better. VITAL also counted fractures: people on 2000 IU daily matched those who did not supplement (HR 0.98, 0.89–1.08). VITAL also looked at depression among 18353 of the participants aged 50 and above who were not depressed at the time. Those who supplemented had 12.9 cases of depression or relapse per 1000 person-years; those who did not had 13.3; no difference (HR 0.97, 0.87–1.09). Mood scores also did not differ. The authors state that the results do not support adults using vitamin D to prevent depression. A Canadian trial enrolled 311 healthy people aged 55 to 70 without osteoporosis, in three groups taking 400, 4000, or 10000 IU daily for 3 years. The higher the dose, the more bone density fell at the wrist radius: 1.2%, 2.4%, and 3.5% respectively. Bone compressive strength showed no difference. The authors conclude that this does not support high-dose vitamin D helping bone. A Swiss trial enrolled 200 people aged 70 and above who had fallen; they took 24000 IU or 60000 IU monthly for a year. The 60000 IU group’s leg function did not improve, and more of them fell within the year (66.9% versus 47.9%). An Australian trial enrolled 2256 women aged 70 and above who took 500000 IU once each autumn–winter. Their falls were about 15% higher (RR 1.15, 1.02–1.30) and fractures about 26% higher (RR 1.26, 1.00–1.59).
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources:Manson JE et al. (2019). Vitamin D Supplements and Prevention of Cancer and Cardiovascular Disease. NEJM. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1809944>；Neale RE et al. (2022). The D-Health Trial: a randomised controlled trial of the effect of vitamin D on mortality. Lancet Diabetes Endocrinol. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S2213-8587(21)00345-4>；NHS. Vitamin D. <https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/vitamins-and-minerals/vitamin-d/>；NHS. Tiredness and fatigue. <https://www.nhs.uk/symptoms/tiredness-and-fatigue/>；国家卫生健康委员会. WS/T 578.4—2018 中国居民膳食营养素参考摄入量 第4部分：脂溶性维生素，表1. <https://www.nhc.gov.cn/wjw/yingyang/201805/3dcd74635d8e40bcb5d834d3b2f66964/files/1739783549670_23371.pdf>；LeBoff MS et al. (2022). Supplemental Vitamin D and Incident Fractures in Midlife and Older Adults. NEJM. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa2202106>；Burt LA et al. (2019). Effect of High-Dose Vitamin D Supplementation on Volumetric Bone Density and Bone Strength: A Randomized Clinical Trial. JAMA. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2019.11889>；Bischoff-Ferrari HA et al. (2016). Monthly High-Dose Vitamin D Treatment for the Prevention of Functional Decline: A Randomized Clinical Trial. JAMA Intern Med. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2015.7148>；Sanders KM et al. (2010). Annual high-dose oral vitamin D and falls and fractures in older women: a randomized controlled trial. JAMA. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2010.594>；Munns CF et al. (2016). Global Consensus Recommendations on Prevention and Management of Nutritional Rickets. J Clin Endocrinol Metab. <https://doi.org/10.1210/jc.2015-2175>；Mondal K et al. (2014). A Randomized controlled trial on safety and efficacy of single intramuscular versus staggered oral dose of 600 000IU Vitamin D in treatment of nutritional rickets. J Trop Pediatr. <https://doi.org/10.1093/tropej/fmt105>；Okereke OI et al. (2020). Effect of Long-term Vitamin D3 Supplementation vs Placebo on Risk of Depression or Clinically Relevant Depressive Symptoms and on Change in Mood Scores: A Randomized Clinical Trial. JAMA. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2020.10224>；Mikola T et al. (2023). The effect of vitamin D supplementation on depressive symptoms in adults: A systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials. Crit Rev Food Sci Nutr. <https://doi.org/10.1080/10408398.2022.2096560>；Ghaemi S et al. (2024). The effect of vitamin D supplementation on depression: a systematic review and dose-response meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials. Psychol Med. <https://doi.org/10.1017/s0033291724001697>
- Notes: Controversy: Neither VITAL nor D-Health specially recruited people who were truly vitamin D deficient, so the conclusions cover only “people who were not already deficient.” People confirmed deficient, people with osteoporosis, and infants and young children should still supplement as their doctor says. Severe deficiency does not have to mean an injection. The 2016 global consensus on rickets recommends oral dosing because oral raises levels faster than intramuscular injection. One trial enrolled 61 children with rickets: one group got a single injection of 600000 IU; the other took 60000 IU orally each week for 10 weeks. At 12 weeks, labs and imaging did not differ between groups. After finishing the dose the doctor prescribed, recheck; if levels still do not rise, have the doctor look for an absorption problem. People who get little sun taking 400 IU daily are preventing deficiency. The health industry standard states that vitamin D deficiency causes rickets, osteomalacia, and osteoporosis. That does not mean it prevents cancer or heart disease, or that it treats fatigue. If a doctor has prescribed a different dose, follow the doctor. Whether supplementing helps people who already have depression is mixed. A 2023 analysis pooling 41 trials saw a small drop in depression scores, but results varied widely across trials and the authors rated the evidence quality in the lowest tier. A 2024 analysis split by duration found that trials followed for a year or more showed no effect. If you have depression, go to psychiatry or clinical psychology first; do not replace treatment with vitamin D. This item looked at all-cause death, cancer, cardiovascular disease, bone, falls, and depression; nothing else. How infants should supplement is in Section 20, Item 16 (400 international units of vitamin D daily).

### 4. Don’t take antioxidant supplements to prevent cancer (beta-carotene, vitamins E and A)
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: Approximately 0.5 to 2 yuan per day. For smokers, the real cost is a higher risk of lung cancer.
- In plain terms: Antioxidant supplements are not merely useless. Pooling 78 trials and nearly 300000 people, in the more rigorously designed trials those who took them had a slightly higher death rate. In two large trials done specifically on smokers, beta-carotene raised the share who got lung cancer by 18% to 28%. Deaths were also higher by 8% to 17%. Smokers and people exposed to asbestos especially should not take them.
- Benefit: Cochrane reviewed 78 randomized controlled trials with 296,707 participants total. The overall risk ratio for mortality among supplement users was 1.02 (95% CI 0.98–1.05). When only higher-quality trials were considered, the risk ratio rose to 1.04 (1.01–1.07). Specifically, beta-carotene raised mortality risk to 1.05 (1.01–1.09) and vitamin E to 1.03 (1.00–1.05). The ATBC trial tracked 29,133 male smokers who took 20 mg daily; it found a 18% increase in lung cancer incidence (3%–36%) and an 8% rise in overall mortality (1%–16%). The CARET trial involved 18,314 smokers or asbestos-exposed individuals; it showed a 28% higher risk of lung cancer (RR 1.28, 1.04–1.57) and a 17% higher risk of all-cause mortality (RR 1.17, 1.03–1.33). The perception that these supplements are beneficial stems from the flawed logic that antioxidants prevent aging, which in turn prevents cancer — each step of this reasoning seems intuitive but is untrue.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources:Bjelakovic G et al. (2012). Antioxidant supplements for prevention of mortality in healthy participants and patients with various diseases. Cochrane Database Syst Rev. <https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD007176.pub2>；The Alpha-Tocopherol, Beta Carotene Cancer Prevention Study Group (1994). The effect of vitamin E and beta carotene on the incidence of lung cancer and other cancers in male smokers. NEJM. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJM199404143301501>；Omenn GS et al. (1996). Effects of a combination of beta carotene and vitamin A on lung cancer and cardiovascular disease. NEJM. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJM199605023341802>
- Notes: The U.S. Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF) rated both beta-carotene and vitamin E as “D” in 2022, meaning they advise against their use. Smokers and asbestos-exposed individuals have already suffered harm from taking them. Naturally occurring antioxidants found in fruits and vegetables are not included in this recommendation; you may continue eating those as normal.

### 5. Don’t expect glucosamine/chondroitin to treat knee osteoarthritis
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: About 2 to 5 yuan a day, or 700 to 1800 yuan a year.
- In plain terms: People with knee osteoarthritis who took a placebo for 24 weeks also had about six in ten say the pain eased. Those on glucosamine or chondroitin were only four or five percentage points above that — small enough that it may be chance. Spending 700 to 1800 a year buys an effect that is basically the same as placebo.
- Benefit: The GAIT trial enrolled 1583 people with knee osteoarthritis for 24 weeks. Among those on placebo (fake medicine), 60.1% also said pain eased. Glucosamine was only 3.9 percentage points higher (P=0.30). Chondroitin was 5.3 percentage points higher (P=0.17). Both together were 6.5 percentage points higher (P=0.09). These gaps are too small to rule out chance. In the same trial the real control drug celecoxib was 10.0 percentage points higher (P=0.008). It looks worthwhile because “cartilage building blocks” sound on-target, and placebo alone already helped six in ten people say it worked.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources:Clegg DO et al. (2006). Glucosamine, chondroitin sulfate, and the two in combination for painful knee osteoarthritis. NEJM. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa052771>
- Notes: Controversy: In the same trial, among those who already hurt badly at the start, both together had a response rate of 79.2% versus 54.3% in the control group (P=0.002). The authors say this was dug out of the data afterward and is only a lead, not a conclusion. What actually has evidence for knee osteoarthritis is losing weight and building muscle; see Item 33 in this section (arthroscopic clean-out).

### 6. Don’t rely on vitamins C to prevent colds
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=小 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: Each effervescent tablet costs roughly 1–2 RMB; annual expense ranges from 300 to 700 RMB.
- In plain terms: Taking vitamin C every day will basically not make you catch fewer colds, and starting only after you already have a cold works only sometimes. Daily use can shorten one cold by about 8%; whether 300 to 500 a year is worth it is your call. Marathon runners and soldiers marching in cold regions are the exception: among them, those who take it get about half as many colds.
- Benefit: Cochrane reviewed 29 randomized controlled trials involving 11,306 participants. For ordinary people, regular vitamin C consumption does not meaningfully reduce overall cold incidence; the risk ratio here is 0.97, meaning there is virtually no difference between groups. The 95% confidence interval for this figure is 0.94–1.00. Trials where vitamin C was taken only after cold symptoms appeared showed mixed results: some indicated a benefit, others did not. For the general population, long-term daily intake does shorten cold duration by about 8% in adults (95% CI 3%–12%) and 14% in children (95% CI 7%–21%). Marathon runners, skiers, and soldiers in cold climates again form an exception: 5 trials with 598 participants showed their cold incidence dropped by roughly half, with a risk ratio of 0.48 (95% CI 0.35–0.64). The perceived value of vitamin C for cold prevention is largely driven by its low cost, pleasant sweet-and-sour taste, and the fact that this claim has been widely circulated for half a century.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources:Hemilä H, Chalker E (2013). Vitamin C for preventing and treating the common cold. Cochrane Database Syst Rev. <https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD000980.pub4>
- Notes: Controversy note: The same Cochrane review confirms that regular vitamin C intake does shorten the duration of cold symptoms. For adults, this reduction is about 8% (95% CI 3%–12%); for children, it is 14% (95% CI 7%–21%). This translates to roughly half a day of reduced illness per cold episode. Marathon runners, skiers, and soldiers in cold climates are again the exception: 5 trials with 598 participants showed their cold incidence fell by about half (risk ratio 0.48, 95% CI 0.35–0.64). The vast majority of people do not fall into these special groups. 〔500〕

### 7. Don’t get a whole-body PET-CT or tumor marker panel just because you have no symptoms  
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: A whole-body PET-CT costs roughly 7,000–10,000 RMB per scan and exposes you to ionizing radiation. Tumor marker panels cost a few hundred RMB. The real cost comes later: if a test flags an “abnormality” that turns out to be a false positive, you’ll need follow-up tests, biopsies, and possibly surgery — all of which add up financially and physically.  
- In plain terms: When people without symptoms get screened, what turns up most often is a false alarm. Over eight in ten who get a whole-body CT have at least one “abnormality” noted, and nearly four in ten are called back for more tests, yet most findings are benign. Women screened with ovarian-cancer markers do not die less of ovarian cancer, and some have had surgery for nothing after a false alarm.  
- Benefit: The USPSTF, which evaluates preventive measures in the U.S., gave a D rating to using CA-125 and ultrasound to screen asymptomatic women for ovarian cancer — meaning it does not recommend this screening. Their basis is the PLCO trial, which showed a 0.34% death rate from ovarian cancer in the screened group versus 0.29% in the control group (RR 1.18, 95% CI 0.82–1.71). Screening offers no real benefit. The rate of unnecessary surgery due to false positives ranges from 0.2% to 3.25% across studies, with up to 15% of those patients experiencing serious complications. Another study screened 1,192 symptom-free individuals with whole-body CT; 86% had at least one “abnormality” noted, and 37% were advised to undergo further tests. Most of these findings were benign. The idea that screening is “worth it” because it enables early detection stems largely from intuition rather than solid evidence.  
- Evidence grade: A  
- Sources:US Preventive Services Task Force (2018). Screening for Ovarian Cancer: US Preventive Services Task Force Recommendation Statement. JAMA（同一份建议另有 USPSTF 官方页）. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2017.21926>、<https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/recommendation/ovarian-cancer-screening>；Furtado CD et al. (2005). Whole-body CT screening: spectrum of findings and recommendations in 1192 patients. Radiology. <https://doi.org/10.1148/radiol.2372041741>
- Notes: The tumor marker portion of this recommendation is supported by randomized controlled trials. In contrast, the evidence for whole-body CT and PET-CT relies mainly on observational follow-ups without proper control groups, so it falls to a B rating. Other proven screening methods — such as tests for colorectal and cervical cancer, breast cancer screening, or low-dose chest CT for high-risk individuals — are discussed in Section 1. For people already showing symptoms or diagnosed with cancer, PET-CT is a diagnostic tool, not a screening measure, and is handled separately.

### 8. Don’t expect fitness trackers to help you lose weight
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: Devices cost between 200 and 2,000 RMB. Plus, you have to constantly check those numbers every day.
- In plain terms: Overweight people on the same diet and exercise plan lost 3.5 kg in two years if they wore a fitness band, while those without one lost 5.9 kg. To lose weight, the device is not money you have to spend.
- Benefit: The IDEA trial involved 471 overweight or obese adults and followed them for 24 months. Everyone followed identical diet and exercise routines; the only difference was whether they wore wearable devices. The group wearing trackers lost 3.5 kg (95% CI 2.6–4.5). The other group lost 5.9 kg (95% CI 5.0–6.8), a difference of 2.4 kg (95% CI 1.0–3.7, P=0.002). It seems cost-effective only because the idea “you must measure to manage” sounds logical, and the devices are a one-time purchase.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources:Jakicic JM et al. (2016). Effect of Wearable Technology Combined With a Lifestyle Intervention on Long-term Weight Loss: The IDEA Randomized Clinical Trial. JAMA. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2016.12858>
- Notes: This is just one trial, using devices from the early 2010s worn on the arm; we can’t conclude that all wearable devices are useless. Exercise itself remains effective, as discussed in Section 2. The bottom line: buying fitness trackers isn’t necessary if you want to lose weight.

### 9. Don’t pay a premium for organic food just to be “healthier”
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: Typically 1 to 3 times the price of regular versions of the same foods.
- In plain terms: There is no strong evidence that organic food is more nutritious. Only 3 studies looked at how healthy people were after eating it, and none found a difference. Organic produce does have pesticide residues detected 30 percentage points less often, but a detection is not the same as exceeding the limit.
- Benefit: One systematic review combined findings from 17 human studies and 223 studies analyzing food composition. Its conclusion was that “there is insufficient evidence to show organic food is significantly more nutritious than conventional food.” Of those human studies, just three looked at actual health outcomes such as allergies and symptomatic infections; no differences were observed between organic and conventional diets. The perception that organic food offers better value stems from the common-sense chain of reasoning: “no pesticides means greater safety, and greater safety means better health.”
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources:Smith-Spangler C et al. (2012). Are organic foods safer or healthier than conventional alternatives?: a systematic review. Ann Intern Med. <https://doi.org/10.7326/0003-4819-157-5-201209040-00007>
- Notes: Very few studies have directly examined how organic food impacts human health. Therefore, the conclusion is “there is no evidence that organic food is superior,” rather than “it has been proven to offer no benefits.” Environmental impact, animal welfare, and taste are not addressed here. Eating more fruits and vegetables remains beneficial regardless of whether they are organic or not.

### 10. Don’t spend a lot on health products, herbal pastes, or tonics to “tone the body”
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=大 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: From a few hundred yuan to over 10000 yuan. Packaged as gifts, the price often multiplies several times.
- In plain terms: Multivitamins, fish oil, vitamin D, and other common ingredients have already been checked one by one in the first six items of this section; none showed a benefit. The line on the pack — “Health food is not a drug and cannot replace drug treatment of disease” — is required by the agency that regulates health foods. If the money is meant as a gift for parents brought to the door, the intention is fine; only the product is the wrong choice.
- Benefit: What you get back is the money saved. The reasons are in the first 6 items of this section. The most common ingredients in health products — multivitamins, fish oil, vitamin D, antioxidants, glucosamine, vitamin C — showed no expected benefit in randomized controlled trials. The numbers for each are in those 6 items’ Benefit lines. Regulatory rules only say what may be claimed; they are not evidence that a product is useless. ### 11. Don’t take probiotics long-term if you have no gut problems

### 12. Don’t force yourself into cold showers to “boost immunity”
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=小 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: No money. You have to endure 30 to 90 seconds of discomfort each day; sticking with it in winter takes willpower. People with cardiovascular disease should also be careful: cold stress is a risk for them.
- In plain terms: In a trial of 3018 people, those who took cold showers had 29% fewer sick-leave occasions. But the total days of leave matched everyone else, so it does not mean less illness. A 2025 pooling of 11 trials found that after cold water, markers of inflammation rose briefly and immune markers did not change. Wash if you like; just do not treat it as a health investment.
- Benefit: A Dutch randomized controlled trial enrolled 3018 people who, after a hot shower, rinsed with cold water for 30, 60, or 90 seconds. Their sick-leave occasions fell by 29% (IRR 0.71, a rate ratio for counts). But the paper states there was “no significant between-group difference in sick-leave days”: total days ill were the same on both sides. Quality of life, work productivity, and anxiety also showed no meaningful differences. A 2025 systematic review pooled 11 randomized trials and 3177 people. Right after cold water and 1 hour later, markers of inflammation rose instead. Immune markers did not change. Stress fell only at the 12-hour time point. The authors themselves say the evidence is “limited by few RCTs and small samples,” meaning such randomized trials are scarce and each enrolls few people. It looks worthwhile because it costs nothing and the intuition “suffering works” is smooth.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources:Buijze GA et al. (2016). The Effect of Cold Showering on Health and Work: A Randomized Controlled Trial. PLOS ONE. <https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0161749>；Cain T et al. (2025). - Notes: Controversy: Sick-leave occasions did fall by 29%, and that gap does not look like chance. But total days of illness did not fall, so you cannot say people truly got sick less. Cold water for athletes’ recovery after training is a different question; this item does not cover it. Cold showers show no clear harm in healthy people; just do not treat them as a health investment.

### 14. Don’t force yourself to drink water just to hit “8 glasses a day”

### 15. Don’t pay for fortune-telling, tarot, or horoscopes to make decisions
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=金钱 -->

- In plain terms: In a classic classroom experiment, the “personal personality reading” students got was the same for everyone, yet most thought it was accurate. ### 17. Don’t treat blood donation as health care: “detox, lower lipids, prevent heart attack” has no randomized-trial support; donating too often does cause iron deficiency
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=中 毅力=些 收益=小 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: No money. One donation — forms, the draw, plus staying to rest and be observed — takes about an hour. For weeks afterward the body keeps replacing the iron lost.
- In plain terms: Claims that donating detoxifies, lowers lipids, or prevents heart attacks are unreliable. People who keep donating are already healthier; after subtracting that layer, the death-rate drop from one extra donation a year shrinks from nearly 20% to 7.5%. In trials that truly split people for comparison, death counts did not differ. On the flip side, donating too often does leave people weak and cold-sensitive — from iron deficiency.
- Benefit: The Finnish Kuopio study followed 2862 men aged 42 to 60 for nearly 9 years on average. Of 153 who had donated in the 24 months before counting started, 1 (0.7%) had an acute heart attack. Of 2529 who had not donated, 316 (12.5%) did (difference between the two rates P<0.0001). After subtracting age and other coronary risk factors, donors’ heart-attack risk was about 88% lower. Relative risk 0.12 (95% CI 0.02–0.86, P=0.035). But that is only from follow-up records, with no randomized comparison. The Nordic SCANDAT database has 1182495 donors followed for 9526627 person-years, with 15401 deaths. Subtracting only age and sex, one extra donation a year linked to 18.6% lower mortality (95% CI 16.8%–20.4%). Subtracting also the layer “only the healthy stay allowed to donate,” the drop is left at 7.5% (5.7%–9.4%). The authors conclude it “cannot be interpreted as solid evidence of a health benefit.” The FeAST randomized controlled trial enrolled 1277 people with symptomatic lower-limb arterial disease, randomized into two groups: one had blood drawn every 6 months to lower iron; one was a control. The main outcome was all-cause death: 125/636 (20%) versus 148/641 (23%). HR 0.85 (HR is a hazard ratio; 1 means the two sides match). 95% CI 0.67–1.08, P=0.17. Death plus non-fatal heart attack and stroke together: 180 (28%) versus 205 (32%), HR 0.88 (0.72–1.07, P=0.20). Neither showed a difference. The INTERVAL randomized trial enrolled 45263 whole-blood donors in three donation intervals: men 12, 10, or 8 weeks; women 16, 14, or 12 weeks. Shorter intervals clearly collected more blood over two years (men’s 8-week group 1.69 units more than the 12-week group, 95% CI 1.59–1.80). Quality of life, physical activity, and cognition showed no between-group differences. But donation-related discomfort rose, including fatigue, shortness of breath, vertigo, dizziness, and restless legs (legs so uncomfortable at night you cannot lie still). Mean hemoglobin and ferritin were also lower, and delays for low hemoglobin were more frequent (all P<0.0001). The anemia group’s rectal temperature was 36.0±0.2 °C versus 36.2±0.1 °C in controls (P=0.001). Oxygen use was 5.28±0.26 versus 5.99±0.29 mL·min⁻¹·kg⁻¹ (P=0.04). ### 19. Don’t start uric-acid–lowering drugs just because a check-up found high uric acid and you have never had pain

Treat 24 people for 3 full years and you prevent only one gout attack. Hoping it protects the kidneys also fails: kidney patients on the drug still lose kidney function as fast as those on placebo. Treat only after a real attack.
- Benefit: The American College of Rheumatology 2020 gout guideline covers asymptomatic hyperuricemia: blood uric acid >6.8 mg/dL, with no prior gout attack and no subcutaneous tophi. The guideline conditionally recommends that these people not start any uric-acid–lowering drug. Drugs include allopurinol, febuxostat, and probenecid. Evidence certainty is rated “high.” A table footnote states that “by attributable risk, 24 patients must be treated for 3 years to prevent one gout attack.” The CKD-FIX randomized controlled trial enrolled 363 people with stage 3 or 4 chronic kidney disease, no gout history, but risk of worsening. One group took 100–300 mg allopurinol daily; one took placebo. The trial ran 104 weeks. eGFR is a kidney-function marker in mL/min/1.73 m². One group changed −3.33 per year (95% CI −4.11 to −2.55). The other −3.23 (−3.98 to −2.47). The difference was −0.10 (−1.18 to 0.97), P=0.85 — kidney function fell at the same speed. Serious adverse events 46% versus 44%. The PERL randomized controlled trial enrolled 530 people with type 1 diabetes and early-to-mid diabetic kidney disease; they took allopurinol for 3 years, then stopped for 2 months to wash out the effect. Allopurinol did lower blood uric acid from 6.1 to 3.9 mg/dL. After washout, the two groups’ GFR differed by only 0.001 mL/min/1.73 m². The credible range for that difference is 95% CI −1.9 to 1.9, P=0.99. Urinary albumin was 40% higher on allopurinol (0 to 80). It looks worthwhile because the intuition “the marker is high so take a drug to push it down” is smooth.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources:FitzGerald JD, Dalbeth N, Mikuls T, et al. (2020). 2020 American College of Rheumatology Guideline for the Management of Gout. Arthritis Care & Research, 72(6), 744-760（表 1 及其脚注）. <https://doi.org/10.1002/acr.24180>；Badve SV, Pascoe EM, Tiku A, et al. (2020). Effects of Allopurinol on the Progression of Chronic Kidney Disease. New England Journal of Medicine, 382(26), 2504-2513. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1915833>；Doria A, Galecki AT, Spino C, et al. (2020). Serum Urate Lowering with Allopurinol and Kidney Function in Type 1 Diabetes. New England Journal of Medicine, 382(26), 2493-2503. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1916624>
- Notes: What to reject is “start lifelong uric-acid–lowering drugs when you have never had an attack,” not “high uric acid does not matter.” The same guideline lists exceptions. The cases below may warrant starting. First: a first attack plus stage 3 or higher chronic kidney disease. Second: blood uric acid >9 mg/dL (about 535 µmol/L). Third: a history of urinary stones. People with tophi, bone erosion on imaging, or 2 or more attacks a year get a strong recommendation to take drugs; those with more than one attack but under 2 a year get a conditional recommendation. Details: Section 16, Item 9 (once gout is confirmed, take uric-acid–lowering drugs long term). About 7.4% of Han Chinese carry the HLA-B*5801 genotype versus 0.7% of White people. Carriers on allopurinol are more likely to get life-threatening severe drug rash; Asian risk of hypersensitivity syndrome is 3 times that of White people. This is a drug you can skip for now; there is no need to take that extra risk. Before starting, test for the genotype — Section 16, Item 9. The two kidney trials were in chronic kidney disease and type 1 diabetes; they cannot be stretched to “high uric acid is wholly harmless to kidneys.” They only reject “lowering uric acid with drugs protects the kidneys.”

### 20. Don’t have the gallbladder removed prophylactically just because a check-up found gallstones and you have never had pain
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: No money, and you also save one operation under general anesthesia and one hospital stay. What you owe in return is remembering which signals mean go to hospital at once.
- In plain terms: People with stones in the gallbladder who have never had pain stay symptom-free for about eight in ten over nearly nine years. Leave them alone and acute problems such as cholecystitis or pancreatitis run about 1 to 3 per thousand per year. Removing the gallbladder is not zero risk: about 1.5% have bile-duct injury. So European and UK guidelines both say: no symptoms, do not cut yet.
- Benefit: Italy’s MICOL population survey ultrasound-scanned 11229 people aged 29 to 69. It found 856 with gallstones, of whom 580 had no symptoms, followed a mean 8.7 years. Of those 580, 453 (78.1%) stayed symptom-free, 61 (10.5%) developed mild symptoms, and 66 (11.4%) severe symptoms. Over follow-up there were 189 cholecystectomies, 41.3% of them in people without symptoms. The authors conclude that “expectant management remains an effective approach for most patients” — watch first, no surgery. A U.S. HMO (a managed-care organization) followed 691 people with confirmed gallstones a mean 78±61.6 months. Of 135 without symptoms followed 58±50.2 months (median 46.3 months), only 10% developed gallstone symptoms and 7% had surgery. Of 50 deaths in the whole group, only 2 were biliary-related, both in the symptomatic group. The authors conclude that “patients with silent stones need not be operated on before symptoms appear”; silent stones are stones that stay quiet and do not flare. UK NICE guideline CG188 item 1.2.1 says: for people with a normal gallbladder and biliary tree who have incidentally found asymptomatic gallstones, “reassure them that they do not need treatment until symptoms develop.” The European Association for the Study of the Liver 2016 gallstone guideline puts both sides’ risks together. Asymptomatic people develop symptoms in about 0.7% to 2.5% per year. Acute cholecystitis, acute pancreatitis, obstructive jaundice (blocked bile duct, person turns yellow), or cholangitis run 0.1% to 0.3% per year. After a first colic, complications rise to 1% to 3% per year, so most people hurt once before a major event. On the surgery side, Sweden’s national registry of 51041 cholecystectomies had bile-duct injury in 1.5%. About one fifth of those (0.3%) were partial or complete duct transection. One-year mortality was 3.9% with duct injury versus 1.1% without. The guideline also notes that after surgery 10% to 40% of patients still have discomfort, or it improves then returns. The guideline’s conclusion: operating on asymptomatic people, open or laparoscopic, does not lengthen life, because the death and complication risk of surgery exceeds the risk of not cutting. Waiting for symptoms also costs less overall. A 1983 estimate gave numbers: prophylactic removal in a 30-year-old man meant living 4 days less on average than watching first; at age 50, 18 days less. No randomized trial has yet compared cutting versus not. Prophylactic removal looks worthwhile because of the intuition “it will hurt eventually, better cut once while young.”

### 21. Don’t give up calcium to prevent kidney stones
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=大 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: No money. It even saves effort: you need not watch every day which foods contain calcium.
- In plain terms: People who have had calcium-oxalate stones are often told to eat less calcium; trial results go the other way. Men with recurrent stones who ate normal calcium while cutting animal protein and salt had, over five years, half the recurrence of those on a low-calcium diet. UK guidelines also say not to restrict calcium.
- Benefit: A 5-year randomized controlled trial enrolled 120 men with recurrent calcium-oxalate stones and high urine calcium (idiopathic hypercalciuria). They were randomized into two groups. One ate normal calcium, 30 mmol a day, while also cutting animal protein (52 g a day) and salt (50 mmol sodium chloride a day). The other ate a traditional low-calcium diet, 10 mmol a day. Over 5 years recurrences were 12/60 versus 23/60. Unadjusted relative risk 0.49 (95% CI 0.24–0.98, the credible range), P=0.04 — recurrence risk on normal calcium was half. During follow-up, urine calcium fell about 170 mg a day in both groups. But urine oxalate differed. The low-calcium group rose a mean 5.4 mg a day; the normal-calcium group fell a mean 7.2 mg a day. UK NICE guideline NG118 item 1.8.1 states not to restrict daily calcium intake. Adults keep 700 to 1200 mg a day. Children and teens 350 to 1000 mg a day by age. Giving up calcium looks worthwhile because of the intuition “stones are made of calcium, so eat less calcium.”
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources:Borghi L, Schianchi T, Meschi T, et al. (2002). Comparison of two diets for the prevention of recurrent stones in idiopathic hypercalciuria. New England Journal of Medicine, 346(2), 77-84. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa010369>；National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (2019). Renal and ureteric stones: assessment and management. NICE guideline NG118，第 1.8.1 条. <https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng118/chapter/Recommendations>
- Notes: Controversy: The trial was only in men, all with recurrent calcium-oxalate stones and high urine calcium; it does not transfer directly to every stone type. Also the trial arm changed three things at once — normal calcium, less animal protein, less salt — so you cannot credit all benefit to “not giving up calcium.” Low salt and less animal protein may also be working. What to reject is “actively cutting calcium-containing foods,” not “you should take more calcium.” What changed here is calcium in daily meals; calcium supplements are separate — whether someone with past stones should take supplements is a question for the treating doctor. Drinking more water: Section 16, Item 8 (after kidney stones, drink 2.5–3 litres a day).

### 22. Don’t buy crystals, bracelets, pixiu, or similar objects for “luck,” “wealth,” or “nourishing people”
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: No money. All you need to do is not count “luck” or “nourishing people” among the reasons to buy. The hard part is not following when everyone else is wearing them and saying they work.
- In plain terms: Claims that wearing them changes luck, attracts wealth, or nourishes people have no checkable evidence. It works like fortune-telling seeming accurate: the words are vague enough that you fit them to yourself. When a seller claims disease-treating effects, that is false advertising; in 2026 livestream rooms were punished for it. Buying them as decoration is fine.
- Benefit: Classic classroom experiments show that everyone gets the same vague “personal analysis” yet most think it is accurate — the Barnum effect. It explains where the experience “things really went smoother after I put it on” comes from. That author’s conclusion is “acceptance by the person does not prove the explanation is correct.” Official case: on 4 June 2026 the State Administration for Market Regulation published typical “fake-fighting, source-clearing” cases in the traditional-craft market. A jewellery shop in Sihui, Guangdong, invented product materials on livestream. It also falsely claimed effects such as “preventing rheumatism, raising immunity, improving sleep, and clearing blood vessels.” The shop used scripted acting to mislead and deceive consumers. That violates Article 9 Paragraph 1 of the Anti-Unfair Competition Law (nationwide).
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources:Forer BR (1949). The fallacy of personal validation: A classroom demonstration of gullibility. Journal of Abnormal and Social Psychology. <https://doi.org/10.1037/h0059240>；国家市场监督管理总局 (2026). 市场监管总局公布一批传统工艺市场「打假清源」典型案例. <https://www.samr.gov.cn/xw/zj/art/2026/art_1529399cfe874b8780c4496eb0158020.html>
- Notes: There is only one conclusion: its effects cannot be checked, and no predictive power can be measured. This does not judge any belief or custom, and it does not target gifts, memorials, or heirlooms. Separate two things. Advertised medical effects have written rules and can be complained about. Claims like “luck” have no research and also cannot be proven false — you can only control them with a budget. Before buying, treat it as ordinary jewellery and ask: “If it changed nothing, would I still pay this price?” How to check materials and certificates, and why treating them as an investment does not pay: Section 5, Items 33 and 34 (count bracelets and watches as money already spent; for jewellery and jade accept only a CMA report). Paying for fortune-telling: Item 15 in this section (fortune-telling, tarot, horoscopes).

### 23. Don’t expect shopping to lift your mood or boost your sense of self-worth
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=是 收益=中 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: No cost at all. The real challenge is resisting the urge to buy things when you’re feeling down.
- In plain terms: People who put more weight on money and things feel less happy themselves, but the link is weak and you cannot tell which causes which. Other research finds that spending on an experience makes people happier than buying stuff. So for the same spend, putting it toward going out once is more likely to make you happier than filling a shopping cart.
- Benefit: One meta-analysis (combining findings from many studies) examined 259 separate samples and 753 effect sizes. The results showed that individuals who prioritized material possessions reported lower happiness levels. On the most commonly used multi-dimensional scale, the correlation coefficient was r = -0.19 (adjusted ρ = -0.24). On scales that measured only “material valuing,” the link was even weaker, with r values ranging from -0.08 to -0.11. The strength of this association varied depending on the outcome measured: it was strongest with risky health and spending behaviors and negative self-evaluations (r = -0.28 to -0.44), and weakest with life satisfaction and negative emotions (r = -0.13 to -0.15). Mediation analyses suggested this negative link may arise because unmet psychological needs drive it. Another study found that across two separate surveys, respondents consistently reported greater happiness from spending on experiences than on material goods. Subsequent lab experiments also showed that recalling a positive mood after spending on an experience led to higher happiness ratings than recalling a similar positive mood after a shopping spree. Both studies used samples from outside China.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources:Dittmar H, Bond R, Hurst M, Kasser T (2014). The relationship between materialism and personal well-being: A meta-analysis. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 107(5), 879-924. <https://doi.org/10.1037/a0037409>；Van Boven L, Gilovich T (2003). To do or to have? That is the question. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 85(6), 1193-1202. <https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.85.6.1193>
- Notes: Caveats: Most of the included studies asked participants at a single point in time whether they valued material goods and how happy they felt, so they cannot determine which factor comes first. The authors themselves note that further experiments and long-term follow-ups are needed to clarify this directionality. Therefore, this finding should not be interpreted as “shopping makes people unhappy”; rather, it means “relying on shopping to improve your mood is unlikely to work.” Moreover, the association is quite weak: an r value of -0.19 explains only a few percentage points of variation, and individual differences are substantial. Both studies used foreign samples, so there are no comparable data available for Chinese populations. This does not mean you shouldn’t spend on things you enjoy; rather, it warns against using shopping as a way to regulate your emotions, as doing so makes impulse buying your first reaction when you’re feeling down. Specific money-saving tips are covered in Section 5. Evidence-based ways to improve your mood when you’re feeling low are outlined in the latter part of Section 22 and in Section 29. Advice on budgeting for the goal of “keeping up with peers” is provided in Subsection 24 of this section.

### 24. Don’t spend extra to move up a tier among people around you — bigger home, car, or circle
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: No money. All you need before raising the budget is one extra question: does this mark-up buy the thing itself, or your place in this group? The hard part is not following when everyone around has upgraded.
- In plain terms: Holding income equal, the more neighbours earn, the lower this person’s reported happiness. It mainly shows up with neighbours of the same education level, and more clearly in people who often deal with neighbours. So money spent to move into a place where everyone is better off than you buys moving yourself down a tier. But when incomes rise together, people still feel better off.
- Benefit: The U.S. National Survey of Families and Households (NSFH) revisited the same people in two waves. The authors matched it to local average income. Local average income (PUMA) was estimated from a 5% sample of the 1990 census. After subtracting own income and a set of personal traits, the result was: higher neighbour income, lower self-reported happiness. The effect is large, stable under other calculations, and highly significant. The authors’ phrasing is that the happiness drop from “neighbour income rising” is on the same order as from “own income falling by the same amount.” Comparing the same person to themselves over time (individual fixed effects), the coefficient’s direction stays and its size is similar; significance only falls to the 10% level. Restricting comparisons to the same state and the same survey wave (state and wave fixed effects), results barely change. So it is not that “people who like moving to poor places are happier anyway,” nor unmeasured state-level factors. The effect is stronger in people who often deal with neighbours and not significant in people who only deal with friends outside the community. Renters and owners did not differ, which does not support “expensive areas have high house prices so spendable money is less.” Happiness falls only with income of same-education neighbours; income of differently educated neighbours barely registers. And it mainly travels through “satisfaction with one’s own material situation,” not through satisfaction with family life or other domains. Finally, if own and neighbour income rise by the same proportion, happiness still rises.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources:Luttmer EFP (2005). Neighbors as Negatives: Relative Earnings and Well-Being. The Quarterly Journal of Economics, 120(3), 963–1002. <https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/120.3.963>；Luttmer EFP (2004). Neighbors as Negatives: Relative Earnings and Well-Being. NBER Working Paper No. 10667（本条引用的表述与数量级按这一版逐字核对）. <https://www.nber.org/papers/w10667>
- Notes: Reasons for grade B: the data are repeated surveys of the same people, there is only this one study, and the sample is abroad. The outcome is self-reported happiness, not money. The authors did use statistics to drop several confounds — same person over time, same state and wave — but it is still not a randomized trial. There is no citeable counterpart for a Chinese population. Benefit magnitude is set to “medium,” following Item 23 in this section (don’t expect shopping to improve mood). The money saved may well reach the 10000-yuan level. But “how much happiness that saved money buys” has no number behind it, so it is not recorded as “large” on a 10000-yuan scale. This does not oppose spending for a larger home, a quieter compound, or a shorter commute — those buy the thing itself. What to stop is the single reason “colleagues all upgraded; I cannot fall behind.” How to rank a place to live: Section 4, Item 18 (put commute time first when choosing where to live). Buying things to regulate mood: Item 23 in this section. Constantly comparing upward online: Section 3, Item 20 (don’t treat “how others are doing” as required daily reading).

### 25. Don’t believe “willpower is like a muscle; use it a while and it’s gone”
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: No money. All that needs changing is one claim.
- In plain terms: The popular claim is: do one self-control task first, then you break more easily afterward, so conserve willpower. Twenty-three labs and 2141 people redid the experiment under the same protocol; the result was almost zero. Stop explaining yourself with “willpower is used up today,” and don’t pay for courses that teach you to stockpile willpower.
- Benefit: A multi-lab preregistered replication had 23 laboratories and 2141 participants. They redid the sequential-task paradigm under Sripada and colleagues’ standardized protocol: first a depleting self-control task, then a second task that needs self-control. Pooled results showed a very small ego-depletion effect, and the 95% credible interval included 0 (d = 0.04, 95% CI −0.07 to 0.15). The authors discuss whether the “self-control is a limited resource” model still stands. Another similarly preregistered redo, 36 labs and 3531 people, found effect size d = 0.06 — again too small to count.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources:Hagger MS, Chatzisarantis NLD, Alberts H, et al. (2016). A Multilab Preregistered Replication of the Ego-Depletion Effect. - Notes: What to reject is the model “self-control is a resource that can be used up,” not “people get tired.” Too little sleep and working too long lower efficiency — that is separate; handle it with the sleep and hours items in Section 3. What has randomized-trial support is changing the environment and the written plan — see Section 4, Item 10 (move what you should not touch farther away) and Section 4, Item 1 (write “at what time, where, when X happens, do Y”). Fixing what you wear and what you eat is still worth doing because it costs almost nothing. ### 26. Don’t count on breakfast or 16:8 intermittent fasting to control weight; pick meal times you can stick with long term
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=小 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: No money. What you save is the daily mental effort of wrestling meal times.
- In plain terms: The popular claim is that skipping breakfast makes people binge at lunch and dinner so the day’s total is higher. Trials are not like that: people who skipped breakfast ate a bit over 200 kcal less in a day and weighed a little less. 16:8 versus simply eating less: body weight after a year also did not differ. People who skip breakfast long term have about 17% more cardiovascular disease, but that is only an association; all-cause death shows no difference.
- Benefit: First the claim “skipping breakfast makes people eat more later.” A meta-analysis pooled 13 randomized trials. People assigned to eat breakfast took in 259.79 kcal more per day than those who skipped (95% CI 78.87–440.71, the credible range). Weight was 0.44 kg higher (0.07–0.82). Skippers did not make it up at lunch and dinner. Follow-up in these trials was short: mean 7 weeks for weight, mean 2 weeks for intake. Next, 16:8 and similar eating only in fixed hours. One trial put 116 overweight adults on it for 12 weeks. Eating only from noon to 8 p.m. versus three regular meals: weight differed by −0.26 kg (−1.30 to 0.78), not significant. Another in Guangzhou, 139 people with obesity, followed 12 months. Time-restricted eating plus calorie control lost 1.8 kg more than calorie control alone (−4.0 to 0.4), also not significant. Finally, whether long-term breakfast skipping harms. In a pool of 2.38 million people, skippers had about 17% higher cardiovascular-disease risk (OR 1.17, 1.09–1.26) and about 49% higher cardiovascular death (OR 1.49, 1.20–1.84). But these are record-only, non-randomized studies. In the original study followed 17 to 23 years, the all-cause-death hazard ratio was 1.19, credible range 0.99 to 1.42, crossing 1 — not a difference.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources:Sievert K, Hussain SM, Page MJ, 等 (2019). Effect of breakfast on weight and energy intake: systematic review and meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials. - Cost: No money. Each check takes a few minutes to half an hour. Have the AI write the statute, guideline, or paper it relies on; open the original yourself and find that sentence. The habit to change is not acting just because it speaks with certainty.

On legal questions, general-purpose AI answers have fabricated content at least 58% of the time, and they will follow wrong premises in your question.
- Benefit: First medicine. A 2026 Nature Medicine randomized trial enrolled 1298 ordinary people in the UK; the protocol was preregistered. Each got two prepared cases and had to judge the disease and whether to go to emergency or watch at home. Some used an AI chatbot — GPT-4o, Llama 3, or Command R+. When the user’s question carries a wrong legal premise, they often accept it wholesale. The same team in 2025 tested professional legal AI tools for lawyers. ### 32. Don’t skip saving because “people can die any time, spending early is happier”; about nine in ten people aged 30 live to 60

- Cost: No money. What to do when the thought “anyway I might be gone any day” appears is first recall your chance of living to 60. The hard part is everyone around saying that, and the thing you want to buy sitting in front of you.
- In plain terms: By WHO figures for China, about 92% of people aged 30 live to 60, and over half live to 80. Young people commonly overestimate how likely they are to die early; living by that feeling, they save about a quarter less than they should. From the numbers above, most people need to save for living into old age.
- Benefit: First how long people live. WHO’s Global Health Observatory computed a 2023 life table for China. A life table uses that year’s death rates by age to project how long a group lives. By that table, probability of a 30-year-old living to 60 is 92.0% (men 89.2%, women 94.9%). To 65: 87.4% (men 83.0%, women 92.0%). To 80: 55.6% (men 45.8%, women 66.1%). A 2019 Journal of Finance study surveyed people on how long they expect to live. Young people underestimate survival; older people overestimate. An earlier working-paper example: a 28-year-old man’s true 5-year survival was 99.4%; he estimated 92.8%. The authors put self-estimated probabilities into a standard life-cycle saving-and-spending model. Versus using actual death rates, young people save 26% less and retirees draw down savings 27% more slowly. People whose life-and-death estimates in the survey were more skewed also saved more skewed, after subtracting risk preference, cognition, and household conditions. The authors think one reason is overweighting rare, memorable ways of dying.

### 33. Don’t have arthroscopic clean-out or meniscus trimming for slowly worsening middle-aged and older knee pain (osteoarthritis, degenerative meniscus tear); lose weight and train thigh muscle first

People who are overweight also need to eat less, move more, and bring weight down. The hard part is that training and weight loss work slowly, while surgery sounds like a one-shot fix.
- In plain terms: For middle-aged and older people with degenerative knee pain, arthroscopic clean-out or cutting a piece of meniscus is no better than sham surgery. Sham surgery only cuts a few skin holes; afterward pain and walking match real surgery. Any benefit lasts a few months at most and may invite leg clots. Sticking with leg training eases knee pain; overweight people who also lose weight hurt even less.

Sham only cut a few skin holes; the scope was not inserted. Patients and assessors did not know who got which. At every follow-up over two years, neither real surgery beat sham on pain or function.
