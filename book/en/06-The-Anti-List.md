# 6. The anti-list

This section collects things that look cheap and effective. Once the original studies are opened, their benefit is close to zero, equal to zero, or in some cases negative.

The items are grouped by subject below. Item numbers are in parentheses.

**Supplements**: multivitamins (item 1), ordinary fish oil (item 2), vitamin D supplements (item 3), antioxidant supplements to prevent cancer (item 4), chondroitin for knee osteoarthritis (item 5), vitamin C to prevent colds (item 6), spending a lot on health products (item 10), taking probiotics long term (item 11), buying detox and colon-cleansing products (item 13), loading calcium after a fracture (item 30), relying on melatonin to cure long-term insomnia (item 34).

**Check-ups and small findings**: a whole-body PET-CT and a tumour-marker panel (item 7), a general check-up package (item 18), do not take medicine for high uric acid if it has never hurt (item 19), do not have a gallbladder stone removed if it has never hurt (item 20), do not give up calcium to prevent kidney stones (item 21), do not have arthroscopic clean-out for degenerative knee pain (item 33).

**Health sayings**: counting on a wristband to help you lose weight (item 8), buying organic food (item 9), cold showers to strengthen immunity (item 12), not forcing yourself to drink water (item 14), blue-light glasses (item 16), treating blood donation as health care (item 17), breakfast or intermittent fasting to control weight (item 26), counting on a correct lifting posture to prevent low-back pain (item 29), demanding an IV drip for a cold or fever (item 35).

**Spending money on luck and on mood**: paying for fortune-telling and horoscopes (item 15), buying crystals, pixiu, and similar objects (item 22), buying things to improve your mood (item 23), spending more in order to move up a tier (item 24), not saving because you think people can die at any time (item 32).

**Willpower and sex**: willpower is like a muscle (item 25), masturbation harms the body (item 27), conversion of sexual orientation (item 28).

**Answers from AI**: judging your own illness with an AI chatbot (item 31).
### 1. Don't take multivitamins just to live longer or prevent cardiovascular disease
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: About 0.5 to 2 yuan a day, or 200 to 700 yuan a year. It takes virtually no time—just swallowing a pill. Taking them long term easily builds psychological dependence, where missing a day makes you feel like you skipped something essential.
- In plain terms: A trial followed 14,641 male doctors for 11.2 years: those taking multivitamins had just as many serious heart problems and deaths as those who took nothing. Looking at 9 trials with 51,550 people altogether, there was also no clear effect on death. Skipping them saves two hundred to seven hundred yuan a year. People diagnosed with a specific nutrient deficiency, or pregnant women taking folic acid, are not covered by this advice.
- Benefit: The PHS II trial enrolled 14,641 male physicians and followed them for a median of 11.2 years (half were followed longer, half shorter). Major cardiovascular events occurred at the same rate whether participants took multivitamins or not (major cardiovascular events HR 1.01, where HR is the hazard ratio and 1 means both groups are identical; 95% CI 0.91–1.10 is the confidence interval). Mortality was also unchanged, with all-cause mortality HR 0.94 (95% CI 0.88–1.02). In 2022, the US Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF) pooled 9 randomized controlled trials covering 51,550 people and found no association between multivitamin use and all-cause mortality, assigning an "I" statement (meaning the evidence is insufficient to assess the balance of benefits and harms). Multivitamins seem appealing because the idea of "covering every nutrient in one pill" sounds convincing and costs only small change each day.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources:Sesso HD et al. (2012). Multivitamins in the prevention of cardiovascular disease in men: the Physicians' Health Study II randomized controlled trial. JAMA. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2012.14805>；US Preventive Services Task Force (2022). Vitamin, Mineral, and Multivitamin Supplementation to Prevent Cardiovascular Disease and Cancer. JAMA. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2022.8970>；Gaziano JM 等 (2012). Multivitamins in the Prevention of Cancer in Men. JAMA. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2012.14641>（备注里那项癌症结果）
- Notes: Controversy: In the same cohort of male physicians, cancer outcomes were also analyzed. Those taking multivitamins had an approximately 8% lower risk of cancer (95% CI 0.86–0.998, P = 0.04, barely crossing the threshold of being unlikely due to chance). The risk of dying from cancer was about 12% lower (95% CI 0.77–1.01); because this interval crosses 1, the finding may simply be a coincidence. Any potential benefit is very modest and has only been observed in male physicians. People with diagnosed nutrient deficiencies, pregnant women (folic acid supplementation), or individuals with severely unbalanced diets are excluded from this advice and should supplement according to medical guidance. 〔14000〕 〔50000〕

### 2. Don’t take regular fish oil just to prevent heart disease  
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: Roughly 1–3 yuan per day, or 400–1,000 yuan per year. It requires no extra time to take.  
- In plain terms: Two large trials enrolled 26,000 and 15,000 people with diabetes respectively. Those who took 1 gram of fish oil daily showed no difference from non-users in terms of major cardiovascular events or death rates. The fact that eating fish is healthy does not mean fish oil capsules are equally beneficial. Skipping them can save you 400–1,000 yuan each year.  
- Benefit: The VITAL trial followed 25,871 participants for 5.3 years; those taking 1 gram of fish oil daily showed no difference in major cardiovascular events or mortality compared to non-users. The hazard ratio for major events was 0.92 (95% CI 0.80–1.06), well below 1, indicating no benefit. All-cause mortality showed a similar pattern (HR 1.02, 95% CI 0.90–1.15). The ASCEND trial, involving 15,480 diabetic patients over 7.4 years, also found no difference in severe vascular events (RR 0.97, 95% CI 0.87–1.08) or mortality (RR 0.95, 95% CI 0.86–1.05). These results appear favorable only because many assume “eating fish is healthy” equates to “fish oil capsules are beneficial.”  
- Evidence grade: A  
- Sources:Manson JE et al. (2019). Marine n-3 Fatty Acids and Prevention of Cardiovascular Disease and Cancer. NEJM. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1811403>；ASCEND Study Collaborative Group (2018). Effects of n-3 Fatty Acid Supplements in Diabetes Mellitus. NEJM. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1804989>；Bhatt DL 等 (2019). Cardiovascular Risk Reduction with Icosapent Ethyl for Hypertriglyceridemia. New England Journal of Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1812792>（备注里那项高纯度处方鱼油试验）
- Notes: Controversy exists: One trial did show benefits. The REDUCE-IT trial included 8,179 high-risk patients with existing cardiovascular disease or diabetes, already on statins and with elevated triglyceride levels (135–499 mg/dL). Those taking 4 grams of prescription-grade pure EPA daily saw a 25% lower risk of events (HR 0.75, 95% CI 0.68–0.83). However, this was a high-dose prescription medication for a specific high-risk group, not ordinary fish oil supplements for the general population. This discussion excludes the health benefits of eating fish itself. 〔17.2〕

### 3. Do not take vitamin D for longevity if you are not deficient
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: About 0.20 to 0.50 RMB a day, or 100 to 200 RMB a year. The money is not much; the real cost is assuming you have already protected your health.
- In plain terms: For people who are not actually deficient, taking vitamin D for 5 years does not lower the risk of cancer, heart disease, or death compared to taking nothing. Adults who rarely go outside and get little sun can take 400 IU a day, staying under 2,000 IU per day. High doses actually cause more bone density loss and more falls. Feeling tired alone does not mean you lack vitamin D; if you have been exhausted for weeks with no clear cause, see a doctor.
- Benefit: The VITAL trial enrolled 25,871 people taking 2,000 IU daily with a median follow-up of 5.3 years. No differences were seen in cancer, cardiovascular problems, or death. For invasive cancer, the hazard ratio (HR) was 0.96 (where 1 means no difference; 95% CI 0.88–1.06). For major cardiovascular events, the HR was 0.97 (0.85–1.12). For all-cause mortality, the HR was 0.99 (0.87–1.12). The D-Health trial enrolled 21,315 Australians aged 60 and older taking 60,000 IU monthly for 5 years, with a median follow-up of 5.7 years. This study also found no difference, with an all-cause mortality HR of 1.04 (0.93–1.18) and death rates of 5.3% versus 5.1%. It often seems beneficial because purely observational studies, which do not intervene, find that people with low vitamin D levels have higher death rates—an association frequently misinterpreted as "supplementation will fix it." Different guidance applies to people who rarely get outdoors. The UK Department of Health recommends that adults who rarely go outside, live in care homes, or wear clothes that cover most of their skin outdoors take 10 micrograms (mcg) of vitamin D daily year-round. The NHS notes that 1 mcg equals 40 IU, making 10 mcg equal to 400 IU. China's national health industry standard recommends 10 mcg daily for adults aged 18 to 64, and 15 mcg for those aged 65 and older. The same standard sets the tolerable upper intake level at 50 mcg (2,000 IU) per day, because excessive intake causes hypercalcemia. The NHS sets a higher upper limit for adults at 100 mcg (4,000 IU). Fatigue has many causes; the NHS lists insufficient sleep, stress and depression, iron deficiency anemia, diabetes, hyperthyroidism, and sleep apnea. It advises seeing a doctor if you feel unexplained fatigue lasting several weeks or severe enough to disrupt daily life. Taking higher doses does not yield better outcomes. In a secondary analysis of VITAL, fracture risk among those taking 2,000 IU daily was identical to the control group (HR 0.98, 0.89–1.08). VITAL also assessed depression among 18,353 participants aged 50 and older who did not have depression at baseline. Incident depression or recurrence was 12.9 cases per 1,000 person-years in the supplement group versus 13.3 in the placebo group, showing no difference (HR 0.97, 0.87–1.09), nor was there any difference in mood scores; the authors concluded the findings do not support taking vitamin D to prevent adult depression. A Canadian trial enrolled 311 healthy adults aged 55 to 70 without osteoporosis, randomly assigning them to 400, 4,000, or 10,000 IU daily for 3 years. Higher doses led to greater bone mineral density loss in the radial bone of the wrist: down 1.2%, 2.4%, and 3.5%, respectively, with no difference in bone compressive strength. The authors concluded this does not support high-dose vitamin D for bone health. A Swiss trial followed 200 seniors aged 70 and older with a history of falls who took 24,000 IU or 60,000 IU monthly for 1 year. The 60,000 IU group saw no improvement in lower-extremity function and actually had more fallers over the year (66.9% vs. 47.9%). An Australian trial evaluated 2,256 women aged 70 and older who took an annual single dose of 500,000 IU in autumn or winter; their fall rate was about 15% higher (relative risk [RR] 1.15, 1.02–1.30) and fracture rate about 26% higher (RR 1.26, 1.00–1.59).
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources:Manson JE et al. (2019). Vitamin D Supplements and Prevention of Cancer and Cardiovascular Disease. NEJM. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1809944>；Neale RE et al. (2022). The D-Health Trial: a randomised controlled trial of the effect of vitamin D on mortality. Lancet Diabetes Endocrinol. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S2213-8587(21)00345-4>；NHS. Vitamin D. <https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/vitamins-and-minerals/vitamin-d/>；NHS. Tiredness and fatigue. <https://www.nhs.uk/symptoms/tiredness-and-fatigue/>；国家卫生健康委员会. WS/T 578.4—2018 中国居民膳食营养素参考摄入量 第4部分：脂溶性维生素，表1. <https://www.nhc.gov.cn/wjw/yingyang/201805/3dcd74635d8e40bcb5d834d3b2f66964/files/1739783549670_23371.pdf>；LeBoff MS et al. (2022). Supplemental Vitamin D and Incident Fractures in Midlife and Older Adults. NEJM. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa2202106>；Burt LA et al. (2019). Effect of High-Dose Vitamin D Supplementation on Volumetric Bone Density and Bone Strength: A Randomized Clinical Trial. JAMA. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2019.11889>；Bischoff-Ferrari HA et al. (2016). Monthly High-Dose Vitamin D Treatment for the Prevention of Functional Decline: A Randomized Clinical Trial. JAMA Intern Med. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2015.7148>；Sanders KM et al. (2010). Annual high-dose oral vitamin D and falls and fractures in older women: a randomized controlled trial. JAMA. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2010.594>；Munns CF et al. (2016). Global Consensus Recommendations on Prevention and Management of Nutritional Rickets. J Clin Endocrinol Metab. <https://doi.org/10.1210/jc.2015-2175>；Mondal K et al. (2014). A Randomized controlled trial on safety and efficacy of single intramuscular versus staggered oral dose of 600 000IU Vitamin D in treatment of nutritional rickets. J Trop Pediatr. <https://doi.org/10.1093/tropej/fmt105>；Okereke OI et al. (2020). Effect of Long-term Vitamin D3 Supplementation vs Placebo on Risk of Depression or Clinically Relevant Depressive Symptoms and on Change in Mood Scores: A Randomized Clinical Trial. JAMA. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2020.10224>；Mikola T et al. (2023). The effect of vitamin D supplementation on depressive symptoms in adults: A systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials. Crit Rev Food Sci Nutr. <https://doi.org/10.1080/10408398.2022.2096560>；Ghaemi S et al. (2024). The effect of vitamin D supplementation on depression: a systematic review and dose-response meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials. Psychol Med. <https://doi.org/10.1017/s0033291724001697>
- Notes: Caveats and nuance: Neither VITAL nor D-Health intentionally recruited people who were truly vitamin D deficient, so their conclusions apply only to those who are not deficient to begin with. People with confirmed deficiency, osteoporosis, or infants and toddlers should follow their doctor's guidance on supplementation. Even severe deficiency does not necessarily require injections. The 2016 Global Consensus on Rickets recommends oral treatment because it restores levels faster than intramuscular injections. A trial of 61 children with rickets compared a single intramuscular injection of 600,000 IU against 60,000 IU taken orally once a week for 10 weeks; at 12 weeks, laboratory and X-ray outcomes showed no difference. Recheck blood levels after completing the prescribed dose; if levels remain low, have a doctor evaluate for malabsorption. For people with limited sun exposure, taking 400 IU daily is strictly to prevent deficiency. National health standards state that vitamin D deficiency causes rickets, osteomalacia, and osteoporosis; this does not mean it prevents cancer or heart disease, nor does it mean it cures fatigue. If a doctor has prescribed a specific dose for your condition, follow their instructions. Evidence on whether vitamin D helps people with existing depression is conflicting. A 2023 meta-analysis of 41 trials found a small reduction in depression scores, but the individual studies varied widely and the authors rated the evidence certainty as very low. A 2024 analysis broken down by duration found no effect in trials lasting a year or longer. If you have depression, see a mental health specialist or psychiatrist first; do not substitute vitamin D for treatment. This review evaluated total mortality, cancer, cardiovascular disease, bone health, falls, and depression, and did not examine other endpoints. For infant supplementation, see Section 20, Item 16 (feed 400 IU of vitamin D daily).

### 4. Do not take antioxidant supplements (beta-carotene, vitamin E, vitamin A) to prevent cancer
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: About 0.5 to 2 yuan a day. For smokers, the real cost is that lung cancer risk climbs alongside it.
- In plain terms: Antioxidant supplements are not just useless—they can be harmful. Combining 78 trials with nearly 300,000 people, the more rigorously designed trials showed slightly higher mortality among those taking them. In two large trials focusing on smokers, beta-carotene raised lung cancer rates by 18% to 28%, and overall death rates also rose by 8% to 17%. Smokers and people exposed to asbestos should stay especially far away from them.
- Benefit: A Cochrane review pooled 78 randomized controlled trials with 296,707 participants. Across all participants taking antioxidant supplements, overall mortality showed an RR of 1.02 (RR is relative risk, where 1 means no difference between groups; 95% CI 0.98–1.05 is the confidence interval). Looking only at the more rigorously designed trials with low risk of bias, RR was slightly higher at 1.04 (1.01–1.07). Looking at beta-carotene alone, RR was 1.05 (1.01–1.09); for vitamin E alone, RR was 1.03 (1.00–1.05). In the ATBC trial of 29,133 male smokers taking 20 mg daily, the lung cancer rate was 18% higher (3%–36%) and overall mortality was 8% higher (1%–16%). The CARET trial followed 18,314 smokers or workers exposed to asbestos; their risk of lung cancer was about 28% higher (RR 1.28, 1.04–1.57), and their risk of death was about 17% higher (all-cause mortality RR 1.17, 1.03–1.33). These supplements seem like a good deal only because every step in the chain of reasoning—"antioxidants prevent aging, and anti-aging prevents cancer"—sounds like common sense.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources:Bjelakovic G et al. (2012). Antioxidant supplements for prevention of mortality in healthy participants and patients with various diseases. Cochrane Database Syst Rev. <https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD007176.pub2>；The Alpha-Tocopherol, Beta Carotene Cancer Prevention Study Group (1994). The effect of vitamin E and beta carotene on the incidence of lung cancer and other cancers in male smokers. NEJM. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJM199404143301501>；Omenn GS et al. (1996). Effects of a combination of beta carotene and vitamin A on lung cancer and cardiovascular disease. NEJM. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJM199605023341802>
- Notes: In 2022, the U.S. Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF) assigned a D grade to beta-carotene and vitamin E, meaning they recommend against their use. Smokers and people exposed to asbestos have already suffered harm from them. Antioxidants naturally found in vegetables and fruits do not count toward this warning—keep eating them.

### 5. Don’t expect glucosamine/chondroitin to treat knee osteoarthritis
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: About 2 to 5 yuan a day, or 700 to 1800 yuan a year.
- In plain terms: People with knee osteoarthritis who took a placebo for 24 weeks also had about six in ten say the pain eased. Those on glucosamine or chondroitin were only four or five percentage points above that — small enough that it may be chance. Spending 700 to 1800 a year buys an effect that is basically the same as placebo.
- Benefit: The GAIT trial enrolled 1583 people with knee osteoarthritis for 24 weeks. Among those on placebo (fake medicine), 60.1% also said pain eased. Glucosamine was only 3.9 percentage points higher (P=0.30). Chondroitin was 5.3 percentage points higher (P=0.17). Both together were 6.5 percentage points higher (P=0.09). These gaps are too small to rule out chance. In the same trial the real control drug celecoxib was 10.0 percentage points higher (P=0.008). It looks worthwhile because “cartilage building blocks” sound on-target, and placebo alone already helped six in ten people say it worked.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources:Clegg DO et al. (2006). Glucosamine, chondroitin sulfate, and the two in combination for painful knee osteoarthritis. NEJM. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa052771>
- Notes: Controversy: In the same trial, among those who already hurt badly at the start, both together had a response rate of 79.2% versus 54.3% in the control group (P=0.002). The authors say this was dug out of the data afterward and is only a lead, not a conclusion. What actually has evidence for knee osteoarthritis is losing weight and building muscle; see Item 33 in this section (arthroscopic clean-out).

### 6. Do not rely on vitamin C to prevent colds
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=小 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: Effervescent tablets cost about 1 to 2 yuan each, or 300 to 700 yuan a year.
- In plain terms: Taking vitamin C every day will barely reduce how often you catch a cold, and starting it only after symptoms begin yields inconsistent results at best. Daily supplementation can shorten a cold by about 8%, so decide for yourself whether that is worth spending several hundred yuan a year. Marathon runners and soldiers training in extreme cold are the exceptions: among them, it cut the number of people getting sick by about half.
- Benefit: A Cochrane review pooled 29 controlled trials involving 11,306 participants. For the general public, daily vitamin C supplementation does not meaningfully reduce the number of colds caught, with a relative risk (RR) of 0.97 (95% CI 0.94–1.00; an RR of 1 means no difference between groups). In trials where participants only started taking it after catching a cold, the review noted "no consistent effect": some trials showed a benefit, while others showed none. Within the same review, regular daily supplementation shortened the duration of cold symptoms by about 8% in adults (95% CI 3%–12%) and 14% in children (7%–21%). Marathon runners, skiers, and soldiers on subarctic exercises were exceptions: across 5 trials with 598 participants, the incidence of colds was roughly halved (RR 0.48, 95% CI 0.35–0.64). It often feels worthwhile because it is inexpensive, tastes pleasant, and the claim that "vitamin C prevents colds" has circulated for half a century.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources:Hemilä H, Chalker E (2013). Vitamin C for preventing and treating the common cold. Cochrane Database Syst Rev. <https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD000980.pub4>
- Notes: Caveat: In the same review, daily intake did shorten the duration of cold symptoms by about 8% in adults (3%–12%) and 14% in children (7%–21%), which works out to being sick for roughly half a day less. Marathon runners, skiers, and soldiers marching in subarctic conditions were exceptions: across 5 trials with 598 participants, the number of people catching colds was cut by about half (RR 0.48, 0.35–0.64). The vast majority of people do not belong to either category. 〔500〕

### 7. Don’t get a whole-body PET-CT or tumor marker panel just because you have no symptoms  
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: A whole-body PET-CT costs roughly 7,000–10,000 RMB per scan and exposes you to ionizing radiation. Tumor marker panels cost a few hundred RMB. The real cost comes later: if a test flags an “abnormality” that turns out to be a false positive, you’ll need follow-up tests, biopsies, and possibly surgery — all of which add up financially and physically.  
- In plain terms: When people without symptoms get screened, what turns up most often is a false alarm. Over eight in ten who get a whole-body CT have at least one “abnormality” noted, and nearly four in ten are called back for more tests, yet most findings are benign. Women screened with ovarian-cancer markers do not die less of ovarian cancer, and some have had surgery for nothing after a false alarm.  
- Benefit: The USPSTF, which evaluates preventive measures in the U.S., gave a D rating to using CA-125 and ultrasound to screen asymptomatic women for ovarian cancer — meaning it does not recommend this screening. Their basis is the PLCO trial, which showed a 0.34% death rate from ovarian cancer in the screened group versus 0.29% in the control group (RR 1.18, 95% CI 0.82–1.71). Screening offers no real benefit. The rate of unnecessary surgery due to false positives ranges from 0.2% to 3.25% across studies, with up to 15% of those patients experiencing serious complications. Another study screened 1,192 symptom-free individuals with whole-body CT; 86% had at least one “abnormality” noted, and 37% were advised to undergo further tests. Most of these findings were benign. The idea that screening is “worth it” because it enables early detection stems largely from intuition rather than solid evidence.  
- Evidence grade: A  
- Sources:US Preventive Services Task Force (2018). Screening for Ovarian Cancer: US Preventive Services Task Force Recommendation Statement. JAMA（同一份建议另有 USPSTF 官方页）. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2017.21926>、<https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/recommendation/ovarian-cancer-screening>；Furtado CD et al. (2005). Whole-body CT screening: spectrum of findings and recommendations in 1192 patients. Radiology. <https://doi.org/10.1148/radiol.2372041741>
- Notes: The tumor marker portion of this recommendation is supported by randomized controlled trials. In contrast, the evidence for whole-body CT and PET-CT relies mainly on observational follow-ups without proper control groups, so it falls to a B rating. Other proven screening methods — such as tests for colorectal and cervical cancer, breast cancer screening, or low-dose chest CT for high-risk individuals — are discussed in Section 1. For people already showing symptoms or diagnosed with cancer, PET-CT is a diagnostic tool, not a screening measure, and is handled separately.

### 8. Don’t expect fitness trackers to help you lose weight
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: Devices cost between 200 and 2,000 RMB. Plus, you have to constantly check those numbers every day.
- In plain terms: Overweight people on the same diet and exercise plan lost 3.5 kg in two years if they wore a fitness band, while those without one lost 5.9 kg. To lose weight, the device is not money you have to spend.
- Benefit: The IDEA trial involved 471 overweight or obese adults and followed them for 24 months. Everyone followed identical diet and exercise routines; the only difference was whether they wore wearable devices. The group wearing trackers lost 3.5 kg (95% CI 2.6–4.5). The other group lost 5.9 kg (95% CI 5.0–6.8), a difference of 2.4 kg (95% CI 1.0–3.7, P=0.002). It seems cost-effective only because the idea “you must measure to manage” sounds logical, and the devices are a one-time purchase.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources:Jakicic JM et al. (2016). Effect of Wearable Technology Combined With a Lifestyle Intervention on Long-term Weight Loss: The IDEA Randomized Clinical Trial. JAMA. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2016.12858>
- Notes: This is just one trial, using devices from the early 2010s worn on the arm; we can’t conclude that all wearable devices are useless. Exercise itself remains effective, as discussed in Section 2. The bottom line: buying fitness trackers isn’t necessary if you want to lose weight.

### 9. Don’t pay a premium for organic food just to be “healthier”
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: Typically 1 to 3 times the price of regular versions of the same foods.
- In plain terms: There is no strong evidence that organic food is more nutritious. Only 3 studies looked at how healthy people were after eating it, and none found a difference. Organic produce does have pesticide residues detected 30 percentage points less often, but a detection is not the same as exceeding the limit.
- Benefit: One systematic review combined findings from 17 human studies and 223 studies analyzing food composition. Its conclusion was that “there is insufficient evidence to show organic food is significantly more nutritious than conventional food.” Of those human studies, just three looked at actual health outcomes such as allergies and symptomatic infections; no differences were observed between organic and conventional diets. The perception that organic food offers better value stems from the common-sense chain of reasoning: “no pesticides means greater safety, and greater safety means better health.”
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources:Smith-Spangler C et al. (2012). Are organic foods safer or healthier than conventional alternatives?: a systematic review. Ann Intern Med. <https://doi.org/10.7326/0003-4819-157-5-201209040-00007>
- Notes: Very few studies have directly examined how organic food impacts human health. Therefore, the conclusion is “there is no evidence that organic food is superior,” rather than “it has been proven to offer no benefits.” Environmental impact, animal welfare, and taste are not addressed here. Eating more fruits and vegetables remains beneficial regardless of whether they are organic or not.

### 10. Don’t spend a lot on health products, herbal pastes, or tonics to “tone the body”
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=大 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: From a few hundred yuan to over 10000 yuan. Packaged as gifts, the price often multiplies several times.
- In plain terms: Multivitamins, fish oil, vitamin D, and other common ingredients have already been checked one by one in the first six items of this section; none showed a benefit. The line on the pack — “Health food is not a drug and cannot replace drug treatment of disease” — is required by the agency that regulates health foods. If the money is meant as a gift for parents brought to the door, the intention is fine; only the product is the wrong choice.
- Benefit: What you get back is the money saved. The reasons are in the first 6 items of this section. The most common ingredients in health products — multivitamins, fish oil, vitamin D, antioxidants, glucosamine, vitamin C — showed no expected benefit in randomized controlled trials. The numbers for each are in those 6 items’ Benefit lines. Regulatory rules only say what may be claimed; they are not evidence that a product is useless. - Evidence grade: C
- Sources:国家市场监督管理总局 (2019). 市场监管总局就《保健食品标注警示用语指南》和《保健食品原料目录与保健功能目录管理办法》有关情况举办专题新闻发布会. <https://www.samr.gov.cn/tssps/sjdt/tpxw/art/2023/art_4b658b824b1b4b0ba57c09a56cc93aad.html>
- Notes: The conclusion is “no evidence in support,” not “already proven useless,” so the grade is only C. For products with clear ingredients, check the matching studies in the earlier items. People confirmed nutrient-deficient or under treatment should follow the doctor. What to reject is the product, not the goodwill. If an older person already bought and is taking them, and is not using them to replace prescribed medicine and has no problems from them, there is no need to start an argument at the door. Arranging the vaccines and screenings that are due matters more. The same money spent on the items below each has a source elsewhere in the book. Go with an older person for the yearly flu shot — Section 1, Item 20. Shingles vaccine after 50 — Section 1, Item 21. Pneumococcal vaccine at 65 and above — Section 1, Item 22. Buy a blood-pressure monitor and see that they take blood-pressure medicine as prescribed until the reading is on target — Section 1, Item 7. Adapt the bathroom and stairs at home and practice balance and leg strength with them — Section 1, Item 13. Age-appropriate cancer screening, go with them once — Section 1, Items 17 to 19. For someone already long-term bedridden, pressure-ulcer prevention and long-term care insurance are in Section 17, Items 7 and 8. If you want to bring something to the door, swap in fruit, rice and noodles, or foods they like — more solid than a herbal paste. What really needs stopping are two kinds: prepaid “invest for retirement” schemes, and products that would replace medicine — see Section 17, Item 5.

### 11. Don’t take probiotics long-term if you have no gut problems
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=小 口径=金钱 -->

- Cost: Approximately 2–5 RMB per day, or 700–1800 RMB per year.
- In plain terms: Pooling 45 trials in healthy adults, taking probiotics does not lastingly change the gut bacteria; stop and they go back. Blood lipids also do not improve. Antibiotic-related diarrhea and similar specific cases have other evidence; they are not in this item.
- Benefit: The same review concluded that there is no reliable evidence that probiotics can produce lasting alterations in gut flora or improve blood lipid profiles in healthy adults. While gut bacteria do shift temporarily while taking probiotics, these changes reverse once use stops. The practice may seem cost-effective simply because “gut health” is a broad concept that can be loosely applied, and the daily cost of a single probiotic dose is quite low.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources:Khalesi S et al. (2019). A review of probiotic supplementation in healthy adults: helpful or hype? Eur J Clin Nutr. <https://doi.org/10.1038/s41430-018-0135-9>
- Notes: The review also notes a few minor benefits in healthy individuals, such as firmer, better-textured stools and increased Lactobacillus levels in women’s vaginas. Separate evidence exists for probiotics used to treat antibiotic-induced diarrhea or for specific strains targeting particular medical conditions; those cases are not covered here. This review pertains solely to healthy adults using probiotics as a daily supplement.

### 12. Don't force yourself into cold showers just to "boost immunity"
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=小 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: Free. It means enduring 30 to 90 seconds of discomfort every day, and sticking with it through winter takes real willpower. People with cardiovascular disease should be careful, as sudden cold exposure carries risks for them.
- In plain terms: In one trial, people who took cold showers reported 29% fewer days off work for illness. But they felt sick for just as many days as anyone else, so it didn't actually keep them from getting sick. A 2025 review pooling 11 trials found that markers of inflammation actually rose briefly right after cold exposure, while immune markers showed no change. Take them if you like them, but don't treat them as an investment in your health.
- Benefit: A randomized controlled trial in the Netherlands with 3,018 participants tested ending a warm shower with 30, 60, or 90 seconds of cold water. Participants reported a 29% reduction in days of sickness absence (incidence rate ratio, IRR 0.71). However, there was no significant difference between groups in the total number of days participants reported feeling sick. The study did not track the total number of sick leave episodes. Quality of life, work productivity, and anxiety showed no meaningful differences either. A 2025 systematic review pooled 11 randomized trials with 3,177 participants: markers of inflammation actually rose immediately and 1 hour after cold-water exposure. Immune markers showed no change. Stress markers dropped only at the 12-hour mark. The authors noted the evidence was "limited by few RCTs and small sample sizes." Cold showers seem appealing because they cost nothing and align neatly with the intuition that "suffering must be effective."
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources:Buijze GA et al. (2016). The Effect of Cold Showering on Health and Work: A Randomized Controlled Trial. PLOS ONE. <https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0161749>；Cain T et al. (2025). Effects of cold-water immersion on health and wellbeing: A systematic review and meta-analysis. PLOS ONE. <https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0317615>
- Notes: Controversy: Sickness absence days did drop by 29%, a difference unlikely to be pure chance. But total days spent feeling ill did not decrease, so this cannot be described as genuinely falling ill less often. Athletes using cold water for post-exercise recovery is a separate topic outside the scope of this entry. Cold showers show no obvious harm in healthy individuals; just don't treat them as an investment in your health.

### 13. Don’t buy detox, colon-cleansing, enzyme, or alkaline water products
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: Several hundred to several thousand yuan. Diets meant to “detox” by starving oneself can even lower blood sugar and cause nutritional deficiencies.
- In plain terms: The detox industry is massive, yet there is very little clinical evidence to back it up, and no proper trials have been conducted on commercially available detox diets. When researchers looked into whether diet pH, alkaline water, or cancer are linked, only one study met the criteria — and it found no connection.
- Benefit: One review concluded that “despite the booming detox industry, there is very little clinical evidence supporting these diets,” and that “no randomized controlled trials have been done on commercial detox diets.” Another systematic review examined diet pH, alkaline water, and cancer: after screening 8,278 studies, just one qualified for inclusion, and it showed no link either. The authors wrote, “It is not reasonable to promote alkaline diets and alkaline water to the public for cancer prevention or treatment.” These products seem cost-effective only because the assumptions “the body contains toxins needing removal” and “an acidic body state causes cancer” sound plausible from a physiological standpoint.

- Notes: There is simply no evidence to support these products, which is why they receive a C rating. Removing metabolic waste is naturally the job of the liver and kidneys; as long as these organs are healthy, there is no need for any extra “detox” measures.

### 14. Don’t force yourself to drink water just to hit “8 glasses a day”
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=小 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: No money. It takes a little time and attention to remember to drink, and night trips to the toilet also increase.
- In plain terms: Someone chased down the source of “8 glasses a day” and found not a single study that supports it. Food and other drinks already carry a lot of water, so you need not drink that much. This conclusion applies only to healthy adults who live in a mild climate and are not very active. Hot outdoor work, hard exercise, and people who must drink more for treatment or prevention should drink more on purpose.
- Benefit: One literature review specifically checked the “8×8” claim — 8 glasses a day, 8 ounces each. Its conclusion was “no scientific study supporting 8×8 was found.” It also wrote that “surveys of food and fluid intake in thousands of adults strongly suggest such a large amount is not needed,” because food and other drinks already supply a lot of water. The author states clearly that this conclusion applies only to “healthy adults in a temperate climate who sit most of the time.” He also wrote that some treatments or prevention need 8×8 or more, and that “hard labor and exercise, especially in hot climates,” of course need more. The claim looks worthwhile because water is free, 8 is a neat number, and “drink more water” sounds harmless in any setting.
- Evidence grade: C
- Sources:Valtin H (2002). "Drink at least eight glasses of water a day." Really? Is there scientific evidence for "8 x 8"? Am J Physiol Regul Integr Comp Physiol. <https://doi.org/10.1152/ajpregu.00365.2002>
- Notes: What to reject is the number “you must drink so many glasses a day,” not the idea that drinking more water is harmful. People working in heat and people doing hard exercise are a separate case and should drink on purpose. People who have had kidney stones should drink more — see Section 16, Item 8 (after kidney stones, get the water up). The next two lines are common-sense reminders; the review in the sources line does not state them: drink when thirsty and check urine color; older people who feel thirst less should drink on purpose.

### 15. Do not pay for fortune-telling, tarot, or horoscopes to make decisions
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: Zero cost. The hard part is giving up a convenient shortcut for decision-making and having to think through choices yourself.
- In plain terms: In a classic classroom experiment, every student received the exact same "personal personality profile," yet almost everyone felt it was remarkably accurate. That is the secret behind why fortune-telling feels so true. In controlled tests, astrologers picked the correct match no better than random guessing. The financial damage goes far beyond pocket change: an online fortune-telling fraud ring in Xixian, Henan, lured people in and charged incense fees and "offering packages" costing up to 3,888 yuan; the ringleader was sentenced to 11 years in prison.
- Benefit: A classic classroom experiment demonstrated this effect: after completing a personality test, every student received the exact same "individual profile," yet almost all rated it as highly accurate. The author concluded that "acceptance of a description by the individual or the analyst does not prove the validity of the description." When 39 students rated the profile's accuracy on a scale of 0 to 5, 34 rated it a 4 or 5. Based on the distribution in the original table, the average score was about 4.26 points. This explains why fortune-telling always feels spot-on, a phenomenon known as the Barnum effect. Astrology was also evaluated in a double-blind trial published in *Nature*. Astrologers were asked to identify which of three personality test results matched a given natal chart. Out of 116 trials, they chose correctly 40 times. Pure random guessing would yield 38.5 correct choices, whereas the astrologers had predicted they would get 58.5 right. Official legal case: The procuratorate of Xixian County, Henan Province, reported an online fortune-telling fraud ring that drew victims in with free palm readings before selling "incense fees" and "worship packages" priced up to 3,888 yuan. With over 50 victims, authorities recovered more than 2 million yuan in illicit funds; the ringleader was sentenced to 11 years in prison and fined 100,000 yuan.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources:Forer BR (1949). The fallacy of personal validation: A classroom demonstration of gullibility. Journal of Abnormal and Social Psychology 44(1):118-123（表 1 评分分布：2 分 1 人、3 分 4 人、4 分 18 人、5 分 16 人，N=39）. <https://doi.org/10.1037/h0059240>；Carlson S (1985). A double-blind test of astrology. Nature 318:419-425（表 2：首选 116 份，挑中 40 份，随机期望 38.5，占星师预计 58.5；「The data are consistent with chance, inconsistent with astrological hypothesis」）. <https://doi.org/10.1038/318419a0>，Richard Muller 网站收录的全文副本 <https://muller.lbl.gov/papers/Astrology-Carlson.pdf>；最高人民检察院 (2026). 河南息县：历经三年持续追踪打掉一批网络算命诈骗团伙. <https://www.spp.gov.cn/zdgz/202607/t20260728_732935.shtml>
- Notes: Both citations are single individual studies. Forer's original paper did not directly state the average score; 4.26 was calculated from the distribution in Table 1 of the original text. This item solely addresses predictive inaccuracy and does not judge any personal faith or spiritual beliefs. Spending a few dollars purely for casual fun does not count; using it to decide whether to resign, marry, or invest is where people lose serious money.

### 16. Do not buy blue-light glasses to "protect eyesight" or believe screens cause permanent damage, but treat severe eye pain and redness as an emergency
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: Free, and it saves hundreds to thousands in lens markups.
- In plain terms: Blue-light filtering lenses show no clear benefits, and skipping them saves hundreds to thousands in lens markups. Compared with regular lenses, studies found no difference in eye strain or how sharply people see, and no evidence that they protect the back of the eye. Sore or dry eyes from screen time recover with rest and do not cause permanent eye damage. What can truly damage your eyes are welding arcs and lasers.
- Benefit: A 2023 Cochrane systematic review included 17 randomized controlled trials, with sample sizes ranging from 5 to 156 participants per study and follow-up periods from less than 1 day to 5 weeks. Because the data were limited, trial populations varied widely, and follow-up times were inconsistent, the authors did not pool the results into a meta-analysis. All comparisons below are relative to non-blue-light-filtering lenses. At less than one week of follow-up, self-reported eye strain "probably shows no difference" (low certainty of evidence). In one 120-person trial, the difference between the two groups was 9.76 units (95% CI −33.95 to 53.47); crossing zero indicates no discernible difference. Critical flicker fusion frequency, an objective measure of eye fatigue, showed "probably little to no difference" (low certainty). Best spectacle-corrected visual acuity "probably showed no effect", with a difference of 0.00 logMAR (95% CI −0.02 to 0.02; 1 study, 156 participants, moderate certainty). Whether it affects daytime alertness remains unclear (2 trials, 42 participants, very low certainty). The review also found no evidence to support claims of protecting the macular retina. The American Academy of Ophthalmology patient education page explicitly states that the claim "prolonged screen use permanently damages eyes" is "simply not true"; extended use only causes temporary discomfort. China's Classification and Catalogue of Occupational Diseases (2024 edition) lists electro-ophthalmia caused by welding arcs and eye injuries caused by lasers as statutory occupational diseases.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources:Singh S, Downie LE, Anderson AJ, Keller PR, White SJ, Ang M, Wolffsohn JS (2023). Blue-light filtering spectacle lenses for visual performance, sleep, and macular health in adults. Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, 8, CD013244. <https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD013244.pub2>；American Academy of Ophthalmology (2024). Computers, Digital Devices, and Eye Strain（「You may have heard that looking at a computer, smartphone, or other digital screen for long periods of time will permanently damage your eyes. Thankfully, this is not true.」）. <https://www.aao.org/eye-health/tips-prevention/computer-usage>；国家卫生健康委等四部门 (2024). 关于印发《职业病分类和目录》的通知（国卫职健发〔2024〕39 号，「三、职业性眼病」第 2 项电光性眼炎，「六、物理因素所致职业病」第 6 项激光所致眼（角膜、晶状体、视网膜）损伤）. <https://www.gov.cn/gongbao/2025/issue_11886/202502/content_7007111.html>
- Notes: Screen use causes visual fatigue and dry eyes, typically felt as soreness, dryness, and temporary blurring after prolonged viewing. Taking a break and blinking more often provides relief. These symptoms are reversible and do not mean your eyes have suffered permanent damage. This statement comes from the American Academy of Ophthalmology educational resources rather than the Cochrane review in the Sources section, so it is graded C. Strong light sources that can cause irreversible eye damage in a short time include welding arcs, ultraviolet radiation, and lasers. Eye injury from welding arcs is known as electro-ophthalmia, a statutory occupational disease. Protection requires proper safety goggles and welding masks, not blue-light glasses; see Section 19, Item 10 (protection against dust, noise, and chemical hazards). When staying up all night staring at a screen, the main danger is not the screen itself, but an urgent exception. Severe eye pain, redness, rainbow halos around lights, accompanied by headache, nausea, and vomiting, indicates an acute angle-closure glaucoma attack. It can permanently damage the optic nerve within days and requires same-day emergency ophthalmic care; see Section 13, Item 6 (ophthalmic emergencies). Triggers include pupil dilation in the dark and prolonged head-down posture that pushes the lens forward; high-risk groups include people over 50, those with hyperopia (farsightedness), and those with shallow anterior chambers. Screen-induced dry eye left unaddressed over time can also turn into a chronic condition requiring medical attention rather than something that disappears after a night's sleep. If eye discomfort persists or vision genuinely declines, consult an ophthalmologist to find the root cause rather than simply swapping lenses on your own. For myopia prevention in children and teenagers, see Section 30, Items 4, 12, and 9 (2 hours of daily outdoor time, cycloplegic refraction, avoiding "myopia cure" products).

### 17. Do not treat blood donation as personal healthcare: claims of "detox, lowering blood lipids, and preventing heart attacks" lack support from randomized trials, while frequent donation genuinely causes iron deficiency
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=中 毅力=些 收益=小 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: Free. One blood donation takes about an hour, including paperwork, blood collection, and resting under observation. Over the next few weeks, the body works to restore the lost iron.
- In plain terms: Claims that donating blood prevents heart attacks or lowers your risk of dying do not hold up. People who can donate repeatedly are simply healthier to begin with; once you account for this healthy-donor bias, the drop in mortality for each extra donation per year shrinks from nearly 20% down to 7.5%. In trials that actually assigned participants into comparison groups, there was no difference in deaths. On the other hand, donating too often genuinely leaves you fatigued and sensitive to cold, and the reason is iron deficiency.
- Benefit: The Finnish Kuopio study followed 2,862 men aged 42 to 60 for an average of nearly 9 years. Among the 153 men who had donated blood in the 24 months before baseline, 1 (0.7%) experienced an acute myocardial infarction. Among the 2,529 non-donors, 316 (12.5%) experienced one (difference between proportions P < 0.0001). After adjusting for age and other coronary risk factors, donors had an estimated 88% lower risk of heart attack, with a relative risk of 0.12 (95% CI 0.02–0.86, P = 0.035). However, this was merely an observational cohort without randomized controls. The Nordic SCANDAT database tracked 1,182,495 blood donors over 9,526,627 person-years, recording 15,401 deaths. Adjusting only for baseline demographic factors such as age and sex showed an 18.6% lower mortality per additional donation per year (95% CI 16.8%–20.4%). But after further adjusting for the healthy-donor effect—that people are only allowed to keep donating if they remain healthy—the reduction shrank to just 7.5% (5.7%–9.4%). The authors concluded this "cannot be interpreted as sound evidence of a health benefit." The FeAST randomized controlled trial enrolled 1,277 patients with symptomatic peripheral artery disease, randomly assigned to iron reduction via phlebotomy every 6 months or to a control group. The primary endpoint was all-cause mortality: 125/636 (20%) versus 148/641 (23%), HR 0.85 (95% CI 0.67–1.08, P = 0.17). Combining death with nonfatal myocardial infarction and stroke yielded 180 (28%) versus 205 (32%), HR 0.88 (0.72–1.07, P = 0.20)—neither outcome showed a significant difference. The INTERVAL randomized trial assigned 45,263 whole-blood donors to varying inter-donation intervals: 12, 10, or 8 weeks for men, and 16, 14, or 12 weeks for women. Shorter intervals yielded significantly more blood over 2 years (men in the 8-week group donated 1.69 more units than the 12-week group, 95% CI 1.59–1.80). Quality of life, physical activity, and cognitive function showed no differences between groups. However, shorter intervals led to more donation-related symptoms, including fatigue, shortness of breath, dizziness, lightheadedness, and restless legs (an uncomfortable urge to move the legs at night). In addition, mean hemoglobin and ferritin levels were lower, and deferrals due to low hemoglobin were significantly more frequent (all P < 0.0001). Iron deficiency impairs thermogenesis: 10 women with iron deficiency anemia, 8 iron-depleted but non-anemic women, and 12 control women immersed themselves in 28 °C water for 100 minutes. The anemic group had a rectal temperature of 36.0 ± 0.2 °C compared to 36.2 ± 0.1 °C in controls (P = 0.001). Oxygen consumption was 5.28 ± 0.26 versus 5.99 ± 0.29 mL·min⁻¹·kg⁻¹ (P = 0.04). Levels of the thyroid hormones thyroxine and triiodothyronine were also lower (P < 0.002). The myth appears plausible because donors are pre-screened, healthy individuals, whereas the hypothesis that "shedding iron protects the heart" has never been demonstrated in randomized trials.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources:Salonen JT, Tuomainen TP, Salonen R, Lakka TA, Nyyssönen K (1998). Donation of blood is associated with reduced risk of myocardial infarction. The Kuopio Ischaemic Heart Disease Risk Factor Study. American Journal of Epidemiology, 148(5), 445-451. <https://doi.org/10.1093/oxfordjournals.aje.a009669>；Ullum H, Rostgaard K, Kamper-Jørgensen M, et al. (2015). Blood donation and blood donor mortality after adjustment for a healthy donor effect. Transfusion, 55(10), 2479-2485. <https://doi.org/10.1111/trf.13205>；Zacharski LR, Chow BK, Howes PS, et al. (2007). Reduction of iron stores and cardiovascular outcomes in patients with peripheral arterial disease: a randomized controlled trial. JAMA, 297(6), 603-610. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.297.6.603>；Di Angelantonio E, Thompson SG, Kaptoge S, et al. (2017). Efficiency and safety of varying the frequency of whole blood donation (INTERVAL): a randomised trial of 45 000 donors. Lancet, 390(10110), 2360-2371. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(17)31928-1>；Beard JL, Borel MJ, Derr J (1990). Impaired thermoregulation and thyroid function in iron-deficiency anemia. American Journal of Clinical Nutrition, 52(5), 813-819. <https://doi.org/10.1093/ajcn/52.5.813>；全国人大常委会 (1997). 中华人民共和国献血法（第二、九、十四条）. <https://flk.npc.gov.cn/detail?id=2c909fdd678bf17901678bf5e71801f9>；国家卫生健康委等 (2024-01-08). 关于进一步做好无偿献血者激励奖励工作的通知. <https://www.gov.cn/zhengce/zhengceku/202401/content_6924875.htm>
- Notes: What should be rejected is donating blood "for your own health," not blood donation itself. Donated blood benefits other people, falling under Tier 4 (strangers) in this book's framework. The tangible return comes from institutional policies. Article 14 of the Blood Donation Law provides that voluntary blood donors are exempt from hospital fees for blood collection, storage, separation, and testing when they require blood. Spouses and immediate family members may also receive exemptions or fee reductions under provincial regulations. A January 2024 notice from the National Health Commission confirmed that 28 provinces have implemented direct reimbursement at hospital discharge for blood-use costs incurred by donors and their family members. Winners of the National Voluntary Blood Donation Contribution Award can also take advantage of local "three-free" policies, receiving free public transit, free admission to government-run parks, and exemption from standard outpatient consultation fees at public hospitals. When you donate, stick to the statutory intervals. Article 9 of the Blood Donation Law sets donations at typically 200 mL, up to a maximum of 400 mL, with at least 6 months between donations. The recommended age range is 18 to 55 years. Do not shorten intervals just to hit an honorary milestone or meet an organizational quota. Women, low-body-weight individuals, and those with heavy menstrual bleeding already have low iron reserves; frequent donors can pay for a serum ferritin test out of pocket. If you experience persistent fatigue, cold sensitivity, or an uncomfortable, uncontrollable urge to move your legs when lying down at night (restless legs) after donating, do not write it off as "drained vitality"—get a complete blood count and a ferritin check. If iron deficiency is found, supplement iron under medical guidance and lengthen your donation interval. The human evidence on cold sensitivity involved only 10 individuals to illustrate the underlying mechanism, not to quantify the exact population prevalence.

### 18. Do not rely on generic health check packages to prevent disease; stick to the few evidence-based screenings matched to age and sex
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: Free, and it saves several hundred to several thousand yuan each year on commercial package fees. The trade-off is remembering on your own which tests are needed and how often to get them.
- In plain terms: People who get regular generic health checks have the same mortality as those who do not, dying from cardiovascular disease and cancer at the exact same rates. Sticking to the few evidence-based screenings matched to your age and sex is enough. Residents aged 35 and older can get their blood pressure checked for free once a year, and those aged 65 and older receive a free annual health checkup.
- Benefit: A 2019 updated Cochrane systematic review included 17 randomized controlled trials. For total mortality, it pooled 11 trials with 233,298 participants and 21,535 deaths: the general health check group showed no difference compared with controls, with a relative risk (RR) of 1.00 (where 1 means both arms are identical; 95% CI 0.97–1.03). For cardiovascular mortality, pooling 9 trials with 170,227 participants and 6,237 deaths yielded an RR of 1.05 (95% CI 0.94–1.16). For cancer mortality, pooling 8 trials with 139,290 participants and 3,663 deaths yielded an RR of 1.01 (95% CI 0.92–1.12). Neither outcome showed any difference. The authors concluded that "general health checks are unlikely to be beneficial." Under the National Basic Public Health Service Program, permanent residents aged 35 and older receive one free blood pressure measurement each year. It also provides seniors aged 65 and older with one free comprehensive health checkup annually. This free checkup includes a physical examination, complete blood count (CBC), routine urinalysis, fasting blood glucose, and an electrocardiogram (ECG). Liver function testing covers aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and total bilirubin. Kidney function testing covers serum creatinine and blood urea. Blood lipid testing covers total cholesterol, triglycerides, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C). Finally, it includes an abdominal B-mode ultrasound covering the liver, gallbladder, pancreas, and spleen (nationwide).
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources:Krogsbøll LT, Jørgensen KJ, Gøtzsche PC (2019). General health checks in adults for reducing morbidity and mortality from disease. Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, 1, CD009009. <https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD009009.pub3>；国家卫生计生委 (2017). 国家基本公共卫生服务规范（第三版）（老年人健康管理服务、高血压患者健康管理服务）. <https://www.nhc.gov.cn/ewebeditor/uploadfile/2017/04/20170417104506514.pdf>
- Notes: What is being rejected here is "using annual checkup packages as a disease prevention strategy," not avoiding all medical tests. Furthermore, annual free blood pressure checks for those aged 35 and older and annual free checkups for those aged 65 and older are already provided under the National Basic Public Health Service Program, so there is no need to pay out of pocket. Tests that have clear evidence should still be done. For blood pressure, blood glucose, and hepatitis B, see Section 1, Items 7, 8, and 14. For breast, cervical, and colorectal screening, see Section 1, Items 17, 18, and 19. For Helicobacter pylori and low-dose CT, see Section 1, Items 23 and 24. ### 19. Do not start uric acid-lowering drugs just because a routine checkup shows high uric acid if you have never had gout pain
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=小 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: Free. It also saves ongoing prescription costs and routine monitoring expenses, while avoiding unnecessary medication risks.
- In plain terms: The American College of Rheumatology conditionally advises people with high uric acid who have never had a gout flare or gout nodules not to start uric acid-lowering drugs. It takes 24 people taking them for 3 years just to prevent 1 flare. Taking them to protect the kidneys also falls flat: kidney function declines just as fast as on a dummy pill. Wait to treat until an actual flare occurs.
- Benefit: The 2020 American College of Rheumatology guideline addresses asymptomatic hyperuricemia, defined as serum urate >6.8 mg/dL with no prior gout flares or subcutaneous tophi. The guideline conditionally recommends against initiating any urate-lowering therapy (ULT), including allopurinol, febuxostat, and probenecid, with a "high" certainty of evidence. A footnote under the recommendation table notes that "based on attributable risk calculations, 24 patients need to be treated for 3 years to prevent 1 gout flare." The CKD-FIX randomized controlled trial enrolled 363 patients with stage 3 or 4 chronic kidney disease who had no history of gout but were at risk of progression. One group received 100–300 mg of allopurinol daily, and the other received a placebo over 104 weeks. The annual change in estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR, in mL/min/1.73 m²) was -3.33 (95% CI -4.11 to -2.55) in the allopurinol group and -3.23 (-3.98 to -2.47) in the placebo group, a difference of -0.10 (-1.18 to 0.97; P=0.85), showing that kidney function declined at the same rate. Serious adverse events occurred in 46% versus 44%. The PERL randomized controlled trial enrolled 530 patients with type 1 diabetes and early-to-moderate diabetic kidney disease who took allopurinol for 3 years, followed by a 2-month washout period. Allopurinol lowered serum urate from 6.1 to 3.9 mg/dL. However, after the washout period, the between-group difference in GFR was only 0.001 mL/min/1.73 m² (95% CI -1.9 to 1.9; P=0.99). Urinary albumin excretion was actually 40% higher (0 to 80) in the allopurinol group. This intervention only seems appealing because the intuition that "an abnormal lab number must be forced down with drugs" feels so convincing.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources:FitzGerald JD, Dalbeth N, Mikuls T, et al. (2020). 2020 American College of Rheumatology Guideline for the Management of Gout. - Notes: What is being rejected is lifelong urate-lowering medication before ever experiencing a flare, not that high uric acid is completely harmless. Furthermore, the two kidney trials evaluated patients with chronic kidney disease and type 1 diabetes; their results cannot be generalized to mean "high uric acid is completely harmless to kidneys." They simply disprove the assumption that lowering uric acid with drugs protects kidney function.

### 20. Do not get a preventive gallbladder removal just because an ultrasound found stones that have never hurt
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: Free. It also spares you a surgery under general anesthesia and a hospital stay. In return, you must learn which warning signs mean you have to go to the hospital right away.
- In plain terms: Among people who have gallstones that have never caused pain, roughly 80% remain completely symptom-free over nearly nine years. If you leave them alone, emergencies like gallbladder inflammation or pancreatitis occur in only about 1 to 3 out of every 1,000 people each year. Surgery is not zero-risk either: roughly 1.5% suffer bile duct injury. Both European and British guidelines advise against operating when there are no symptoms.
- Benefit: The Italian MICOL population screening performed ultrasound scans on 11,229 people aged 29 to 69. They identified 856 people with gallstones, 580 of whom had no symptoms, followed for an average of 8.7 years. Among these 580 people, 453 (78.1%) stayed entirely symptom-free, 61 (10.5%) developed mild symptoms, and 66 (11.4%) developed severe symptoms. During the follow-up period, 189 cholecystectomies were performed in total, 41.3% of them in people who had never developed symptoms. The authors concluded that "expectant management remains an effective approach for most patients"—expectant management means watchful waiting rather than rushing into surgery. An American HMO (a type of managed care organization) tracked 691 people with confirmed gallstones for an average of 78±61.6 months. Among them, 135 people without symptoms were followed for 58±50.2 months (median 46.3 months); only 10% developed biliary symptoms, and 7% underwent surgery. Out of 50 deaths in the entire cohort, only 2 were biliary-related, both in the symptomatic group. The authors concluded that "patients with silent gallstones do not require prophylactic surgery"—silent stones are stones that cause no pain and provoke no attacks. Section 1.2.1 of the UK NICE guideline CG188 states: "Reassure people with asymptomatic gallbladder stones found incidentally and a normal gallbladder and biliary tree that they do not need treatment unless they develop symptoms." The European Association for the Study of the Liver (EASL) 2016 guideline weighs the risks on both sides. In asymptomatic individuals, about 0.7% to 2.5% develop symptoms each year. Acute complications—such as acute cholecystitis, acute pancreatitis, obstructive jaundice (bile duct blockage causing yellow skin/eyes), or cholangitis—occur in 0.1% to 0.3% per year. Only after a first attack of biliary colic do complications rise to 1% to 3% per year, meaning most people experience pain before anything catastrophic happens. On the surgical side, among 51,041 cholecystectomies in the Swedish national registry, 1.5% sustained a bile duct injury. Roughly one-fifth of those (0.3%) were partial or complete transections. One-year mortality was 3.9% in those with a bile duct injury versus 1.1% in those without. The guideline also notes that 10% to 40% of patients still experience discomfort after surgery, or symptoms return after temporary relief. The guideline concludes: preventive surgery in asymptomatic individuals, whether open or laparoscopic, does not prolong life, because the mortality and complication risks of the operation itself outweigh the risks of leaving stones alone. Waiting until symptoms appear also results in lower overall costs. A 1983 decision analysis provided specific estimates: a 30-year-old man having prophylactic surgery lost an average of 4 days of life compared to watchful waiting, and a 50-year-old lost 18 days. To date, no randomized controlled trial has directly compared surgery versus observation. Prophylactic removal sounds appealing largely due to the intuition that "it will hurt sooner or later, so you might as well take it out while you are young."

A commentary in the same journal highlighted this very issue as one of the central debates. The full consensus requires a subscription, so this book could not verify the exact conditions it lists. Another counterargument involves small stones: stones smaller than 5 mm are more strongly associated with pancreatitis. ### 21. Do not cut out calcium to prevent kidney stones
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=大 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: Free. It actually saves effort, since you do not have to constantly watch out for calcium-rich foods every day.
- In plain terms: People who have had calcium oxalate kidney stones are often told to cut back on calcium, but clinical trial results show the exact opposite. Men with recurring stones and high urinary calcium who consumed normal amounts of calcium—while also cutting down on animal protein and salt—had only about half the recurrence rate over five years compared with those on a low-calcium diet. Guidelines in the UK also explicitly advise against restricting calcium.
- Benefit: A 5-year randomized controlled trial evaluated 120 men with recurrent calcium oxalate stones and elevated urinary calcium (idiopathic hypercalciuria). They were randomly assigned to two groups. One group consumed a normal-calcium diet (30 mmol/day) combined with reduced animal protein (52 g/day) and low salt (50 mmol/day sodium chloride). The other group followed a traditional low-calcium diet (10 mmol/day). Over 5 years, recurrence occurred in 12/60 versus 23/60 men. The unadjusted relative risk was 0.49 (95% CI 0.24–0.98), P = 0.04, meaning the normal-calcium group had about half the risk of recurrence. During follow-up, urinary calcium fell by approximately 170 mg/day in both groups. However, urinary oxalate excretion differed: it increased by an average of 5.4 mg/day in the low-calcium group, while decreasing by an average of 7.2 mg/day in the normal-calcium group. The UK NICE guideline NG118 (section 1.8.1) explicitly states not to restrict dietary calcium intake: maintaining 700 to 1,200 mg/day for adults, and 350 to 1,000 mg/day for children and young people depending on age. Cutting out calcium seems intuitive because "stones are made of calcium, so eat less calcium," but that intuition is misleading.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources:Borghi L, Schianchi T, Meschi T, et al. (2002). Comparison of two diets for the prevention of recurrent stones in idiopathic hypercalciuria. New England Journal of Medicine, 346(2), 77-84. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa010369>；National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (2019). Renal and ureteric stones: assessment and management. NICE guideline NG118，第 1.8.1 条. <https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng118/chapter/Recommendations>
- Notes: Controversies and caveats: This trial was conducted only in men with recurrent calcium oxalate stones and high urinary calcium, so findings cannot be directly generalized to all stone types. In addition, the intervention group modified three factors simultaneously—normal calcium, lower animal protein, and lower salt—so the entire benefit cannot be attributed solely to not cutting out calcium; lower salt and reduced animal protein likely played a role as well. What is being refuted is "actively eliminating calcium-rich foods," not suggesting that "one should take heavy calcium supplements." This refers to calcium in daily meals; calcium supplements are a separate matter, and people with a history of kidney stones should consult their treating physician before taking them. For increasing fluid intake, see Section 16, Item 8 (Drink 2.5–3 liters of water daily after kidney stones).

### 22. Do not buy crystals, bead bracelets, or pixiu charms to "change your luck," "attract wealth," or "nourish the body"
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: Costs nothing. All you need to do is stop counting "changing luck" or "nourishing the body" among your reasons to buy. The hard part is holding back when everyone around you is wearing them and swearing they work.
- In plain terms: Claims that wearing these items can change your luck, attract wealth, or nourish your body have no verifiable evidence. It works on the same principle that makes fortune-telling seem accurate: keep statements vague enough, and you will naturally see yourself in them. In 2026, a live-streaming seller was penalized for false advertising after fabricating materials and falsely claiming items could prevent and treat rheumatism. Buying them simply as decoration is completely fine.
- Benefit: Classic classroom experiments show that when everyone receives the exact same generic "personality analysis," they almost universally rate it as highly accurate—a phenomenon known as the Barnum effect. It explains where the feeling of "things really went smoother after I put this on" comes from. The author's conclusion was that a subject's acceptance does not prove an interpretation correct. Official case: on June 4, 2026, the State Administration for Market Regulation published a batch of typical enforcement cases targeting fraud in traditional craft markets. Among them, a jewelry store in Sihui, Guangdong, fabricated product materials during live streams. It also falsely claimed its items could "prevent and treat rheumatism, boost immunity, improve sleep quality, and clear blood vessels." The shop used scripted acting to mislead and deceive consumers, violating Paragraph 1 of Article 9 of the Anti-Unfair Competition Law. The case was titled "False Commercial Promotion Case of Langzhen Jewelry Store in Sihui City" (nationwide).
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources:Forer BR (1949). The fallacy of personal validation: A classroom demonstration of gullibility. Journal of Abnormal and Social Psychology. <https://doi.org/10.1037/h0059240>；国家市场监督管理总局 (2026). 市场监管总局公布一批传统工艺市场「打假清源」典型案例. <https://www.samr.gov.cn/xw/zj/art/2026/art_1529399cfe874b8780c4496eb0158020.html>
- Notes: There is only one conclusion: its purported effects cannot be verified, and it displays no predictive ability whatsoever. This is not an evaluation of any faith or custom, nor does it target gifts, souvenirs, or family heirlooms. Distinguish between two separate issues: health claims made by merchants violate clear statutory rules and can be reported. In contrast, claims like "turning your luck around" have no research behind them and cannot be disproven, so you can only protect yourself by setting a strict spending budget. Before buying, treat it as an ordinary accessory and ask yourself: "If it changes nothing about my life, would I still pay this price?" For how to verify materials and certificates, and why treating these items as investments does not pay off, see Section 5, Items 33 and 34 (treating bead bracelets and luxury watches as money spent; looking for CMA reports on gems and jade). ### 23. Don’t expect shopping to lift your mood or boost your sense of self-worth
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=是 收益=中 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: No cost at all. The real challenge is resisting the urge to buy things when you’re feeling down.
- In plain terms: People who put more weight on money and things feel less happy themselves, but the link is weak and you cannot tell which causes which. Other research finds that spending on an experience makes people happier than buying stuff. So for the same spend, putting it toward going out once is more likely to make you happier than filling a shopping cart.
- Benefit: One meta-analysis (combining findings from many studies) examined 259 separate samples and 753 effect sizes. The results showed that individuals who prioritized material possessions reported lower happiness levels. On the most commonly used multi-dimensional scale, the correlation coefficient was r = -0.19 (adjusted ρ = -0.24). On scales that measured only “material valuing,” the link was even weaker, with r values ranging from -0.08 to -0.11. The strength of this association varied depending on the outcome measured: it was strongest with risky health and spending behaviors and negative self-evaluations (r = -0.28 to -0.44), and weakest with life satisfaction and negative emotions (r = -0.13 to -0.15). Mediation analyses suggested this negative link may arise because unmet psychological needs drive it. Another study found that across two separate surveys, respondents consistently reported greater happiness from spending on experiences than on material goods. Subsequent lab experiments also showed that recalling a positive mood after spending on an experience led to higher happiness ratings than recalling a similar positive mood after a shopping spree. Both studies used samples from outside China.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources:Dittmar H, Bond R, Hurst M, Kasser T (2014). The relationship between materialism and personal well-being: A meta-analysis. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 107(5), 879-924. <https://doi.org/10.1037/a0037409>；Van Boven L, Gilovich T (2003). To do or to have? That is the question. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 85(6), 1193-1202. <https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.85.6.1193>
- Notes: Caveats: Most of the included studies asked participants at a single point in time whether they valued material goods and how happy they felt, so they cannot determine which factor comes first. The authors themselves note that further experiments and long-term follow-ups are needed to clarify this directionality. Therefore, this finding should not be interpreted as “shopping makes people unhappy”; rather, it means “relying on shopping to improve your mood is unlikely to work.” Moreover, the association is quite weak: an r value of -0.19 explains only a few percentage points of variation, and individual differences are substantial. Both studies used foreign samples, so there are no comparable data available for Chinese populations. This does not mean you shouldn’t spend on things you enjoy; rather, it warns against using shopping as a way to regulate your emotions, as doing so makes impulse buying your first reaction when you’re feeling down. Specific money-saving tips are covered in Section 5. Evidence-based ways to improve your mood when you’re feeling low are outlined in the latter part of Section 22 and in Section 29. Advice on budgeting for the goal of “keeping up with peers” is provided in Subsection 24 of this section.

### 24. Don’t spend extra to move up a tier among people around you — bigger home, car, or circle
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: No money. All you need before raising the budget is one extra question: does this mark-up buy the thing itself, or your place in this group? The hard part is not following when everyone around has upgraded.
- In plain terms: Holding income equal, the more neighbours earn, the lower this person’s reported happiness. It mainly shows up with neighbours of the same education level, and more clearly in people who often deal with neighbours. So money spent to move into a place where everyone is better off than you buys moving yourself down a tier. But when incomes rise together, people still feel better off.
- Benefit: The U.S. National Survey of Families and Households (NSFH) revisited the same people in two waves. The authors matched it to local average income. Local average income (PUMA) was estimated from a 5% sample of the 1990 census. After subtracting own income and a set of personal traits, the result was: higher neighbour income, lower self-reported happiness. The effect is large, stable under other calculations, and highly significant. The authors’ phrasing is that the happiness drop from “neighbour income rising” is on the same order as from “own income falling by the same amount.” Comparing the same person to themselves over time (individual fixed effects), the coefficient’s direction stays and its size is similar; significance only falls to the 10% level. Restricting comparisons to the same state and the same survey wave (state and wave fixed effects), results barely change. So it is not that “people who like moving to poor places are happier anyway,” nor unmeasured state-level factors. The effect is stronger in people who often deal with neighbours and not significant in people who only deal with friends outside the community. Renters and owners did not differ, which does not support “expensive areas have high house prices so spendable money is less.” Happiness falls only with income of same-education neighbours; income of differently educated neighbours barely registers. And it mainly travels through “satisfaction with one’s own material situation,” not through satisfaction with family life or other domains. Finally, if own and neighbour income rise by the same proportion, happiness still rises.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources:Luttmer EFP (2005). Neighbors as Negatives: Relative Earnings and Well-Being. The Quarterly Journal of Economics, 120(3), 963–1002. <https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/120.3.963>；Luttmer EFP (2004). Neighbors as Negatives: Relative Earnings and Well-Being. NBER Working Paper No. 10667（本条引用的表述与数量级按这一版逐字核对）. <https://www.nber.org/papers/w10667>
- Notes: Reasons for grade B: the data are repeated surveys of the same people, there is only this one study, and the sample is abroad. The outcome is self-reported happiness, not money. The authors did use statistics to drop several confounds — same person over time, same state and wave — but it is still not a randomized trial. There is no citeable counterpart for a Chinese population. Benefit magnitude is set to “medium,” following Item 23 in this section (don’t expect shopping to improve mood). The money saved may well reach the 10000-yuan level. But “how much happiness that saved money buys” has no number behind it, so it is not recorded as “large” on a 10000-yuan scale. This does not oppose spending for a larger home, a quieter compound, or a shorter commute — those buy the thing itself. What to stop is the single reason “colleagues all upgraded; I cannot fall behind.” How to rank a place to live: Section 4, Item 18 (put commute time first when choosing where to live). Buying things to regulate mood: Item 23 in this section. Constantly comparing upward online: Section 3, Item 20 (don’t treat “how others are doing” as required daily reading).

### 25. Don’t believe “willpower is like a muscle; use it a while and it’s gone”
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: No money. All that needs changing is one claim.
- In plain terms: The popular claim is: do one self-control task first, then you break more easily afterward, so conserve willpower. Twenty-three labs and 2141 people redid the experiment under the same protocol; the result was almost zero. Stop explaining yourself with “willpower is used up today,” and don’t pay for courses that teach you to stockpile willpower.
- Benefit: A multi-lab preregistered replication had 23 laboratories and 2141 participants. They redid the sequential-task paradigm under Sripada and colleagues’ standardized protocol: first a depleting self-control task, then a second task that needs self-control. Pooled results showed a very small ego-depletion effect, and the 95% credible interval included 0 (d = 0.04, 95% CI −0.07 to 0.15). The authors discuss whether the “self-control is a limited resource” model still stands. Another similarly preregistered redo, 36 labs and 3531 people, found effect size d = 0.06 — again too small to count.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources:Hagger MS, Chatzisarantis NLD, Alberts H, et al. (2016). A Multilab Preregistered Replication of the Ego-Depletion Effect. Perspectives on Psychological Science, 11(4), 546–573. <https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691616652873>；Vohs et al. (2021). A Multisite Preregistered Paradigmatic Test of the Ego-Depletion Effect. Psychological Science. <https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797621989733>
- Notes: What to reject is the model “self-control is a resource that can be used up,” not “people get tired.” Too little sleep and working too long lower efficiency — that is separate; handle it with the sleep and hours items in Section 3. What has randomized-trial support is changing the environment and the written plan — see Section 4, Item 10 (move what you should not touch farther away) and Section 4, Item 1 (write “at what time, where, when X happens, do Y”). Fixing what you wear and what you eat is still worth doing because it costs almost nothing. What it saves is those few minutes and a few rounds of indecision, not willpower; don’t expect it to make afternoon judgment better. Other items in this section count money; this one counts time and energy — the two are not converted.

### 26. Do not expect eating breakfast or 16:8 fasting to control your weight; pick a meal schedule you can stick to long term
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=小 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: Free. What you save is the mental energy spent obsessing over meal timing every day.
- In plain terms: The popular belief is that skipping breakfast leads to overeating at lunch and dinner, making you eat more overall. In trials lasting several weeks, that did not happen: people who skipped breakfast ate over two hundred fewer calories a day and weighed slightly less. Comparing 16:8 time-restricted eating to simply cutting calories, weight was no different after a full year. People who skip breakfast long-term have more cardiovascular disease, but that is only an observational link; overall mortality shows no difference.
- Benefit: First, consider the claim that "skipping breakfast makes you eat more later." A meta-analysis pooled 13 randomized trials. Participants assigned to eat breakfast consumed 259.79 kcal more per day than those who skipped breakfast (95% CI 78.87–440.71, the confidence interval). They also weighed 0.44 kg more (0.07–0.82). People who skipped breakfast did not compensate at lunch or dinner. These trials had very short follow-up periods, averaging 7 weeks for weight and 2 weeks for intake. Next, look at 16:8 and similar practices that restrict eating to a set window of hours. One trial enrolled 116 overweight adults for 12 weeks. The group eating only between 12:00 PM and 8:00 PM, compared to the group eating three regular meals a day, had a weight difference of −0.26 kg (−1.30 to 0.78), which was not statistically significant. Another trial conducted in Guangzhou followed 139 patients with obesity for 12 months. Time-restricted eating combined with calorie restriction reduced weight by an additional 1.8 kg (−4.0 to 0.4) compared to calorie restriction alone, which was also not statistically significant. Finally, look at whether skipping breakfast long term is harmful. In a pooled analysis of 2.38 million people, those who skipped breakfast had higher rates of cardiovascular disease, with an odds ratio (OR) of 1.17 (1.09–1.26). Cardiovascular mortality was also higher, with an odds ratio (OR) of 1.49 (1.20–1.84). However, these were observational studies without randomization. In the original study following participants for 17 to 23 years, the hazard ratio for all-cause mortality was 1.19, with a confidence interval of 0.99 to 1.42, which crosses 1 and shows no statistically significant difference.
- Evidence grade: A

People who skip breakfast are already more likely to smoke, drink alcohol, be sedentary, and have lower incomes, and these confounding factors cannot be completely adjusted for. It is therefore unclear how much of this association is caused by breakfast itself. The all-cause mortality estimate already crossed 1. The 16:8 schedule is also controversial. A review pooling 30 trials with 1,341 people found that, when both groups consumed the same number of calories, the time-restricted eating group lost an additional 1.46 kg (−2.65 to −0.26). The trade-off was losing an extra 0.41 kg of lean mass. However, most of these trials were very short, and the longest, most rigorous 12-month trial found no difference. Prolonged fasting is also linked to the gallbladder. Taken together, meal timing has very little impact on weight control, so do not waste energy on it. If you want to play it safe, eat regular meals, but do not expect them to help you lose weight. This advice does not apply to people with diabetes, those taking glucose-lowering medications, or during pregnancy, where skipping a meal may trigger hypoglycemia; follow medical guidance. Extreme fasting and self-induced vomiting are a different matter; see Section 28, Item 1 (Do not use extreme dieting, fasting, or self-induced vomiting to control weight).

### 29. Do not rely on "proper lifting posture" or manual handling training to prevent back pain—reduce lifted weight and frequency, and exercise regularly
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=中 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: Free. Stop obsessing over whether your back curves while lifting, and stop spending money on back-care classes or back support belts. Do two things instead. Split heavy loads, use a cart, or ask for help, which takes only a few extra minutes. Make time to exercise a few times a week, which requires long-term consistency.
- In plain terms: Workers who attend manual handling training have just as much back pain later as those who do not, and people who bend their backs more while lifting are not more likely to develop back pain. What does track with back pain is lifting heavier loads and lifting more often: the heavier each lift and the more times per day, the more common back pain becomes, though this is only an association and some studies found no link. People who exercise regularly have back pain episodes about 35% to 45% less often.
- Benefit: First, training. A 2011 Cochrane systematic review pooled 9 randomized trials with 20,101 employees. Across 7 trials and 19,317 workers, those who received manual handling training reported about the same back pain as those who received nothing (OR 1.17, 95% CI 0.68–2.02, crossing 1). Compared with watching a video alone, results were also similar (RR 0.93, 0.69–1.25), with moderate-quality evidence. The authors concluded that manual handling advice and training, with or without assistive equipment, do not prevent back pain or related disability. Second, lumbar flexion. A 2020 systematic review pooled studies on how much the back bends during lifting, with low-quality evidence. In the only prospective study, the difference in maximum lumbar flexion between those who later developed back pain and those who did not was only 1.5 degrees (−0.7 to 3.7), which was not statistically significant. Nine of 11 cross-sectional studies found no difference between groups. Another 7 studies using a different measurement found that workers with back pain actually bent their backs 6.0 degrees less (−11.2 to −0.9). The authors concluded there is no evidence that greater lumbar flexion causes back pain. Finally, what actually helps. A meta-analysis pooled 8 prospective studies: for every 10 kg increase in load weight per lift, the odds ratio for back pain was OR 1.11 (1.05–1.18); for every 10 additional lifts per day, the odds ratio was OR 1.09 (1.03–1.15). Both numbers reflect associations only. A 2010 systematic review identified 9 high-quality studies: 4 found an association between manual handling and back pain, while 5 did not. Another review pooled 21 randomized trials with 30,850 participants. Exercise combined with education reduced the risk of back pain episodes (RR 0.55, 0.41–0.74, about 45% lower), with moderate-quality evidence. Exercise alone reduced risk (RR 0.65, 0.50–0.86, about 35% lower), with low to very low-quality evidence. Education alone showed no clear effect (RR 1.03, 0.83–1.27), nor did back support belts (RR 1.01, 0.71–1.44). Official guidance from the UK Health and Safety Executive (HSE) also explicitly states that training alone cannot ensure manual handling safety, and good technique cannot substitute for providing handling aids or redesigning tasks and loads.

### 30. Don’t count on loading calcium or drinking bone broth after a fracture to make bone heal faster

- Cost: No money, and you also save the cost and effort of calcium tablets, vitamin D, and simmering broth every day. All you need is to test for deficiency and supplement only if deficient; if not deficient, do not add extra.
- In plain terms: Trials of vitamin D after fracture show no case where bone healed better. Studies of calcium plus vitamin D together are few; whether they help is unclear. Calcium in a large bowl of bone broth is only a few tenths of a percent of the daily need. People already vitamin D deficient or with osteoporosis are a separate case.
- Benefit: First vitamin D. A 2023 systematic review included 14 studies and 2734 fracture patients. The authors conclude that vitamin D alone has little effect on bone healing, union, or function after recovery. Studies that seemed to show benefit were generally lower quality. They state that counting only randomized trials, none showed a benefit from supplementing. One enrolled 100 long-bone fracture patients with low vitamin D: half took 100000 IU once within two weeks of injury; half took placebo. Two in each arm failed to unite — 4% together. Another enrolled 102 people aged 18 to 50 with thigh or lower-leg fractures in four dose or placebo groups. At 3 and 12 months, healing scores showed no difference. Another enrolled 32 postmenopausal women with wrist fractures. Versus no supplement, a high-dose group equivalent to about 1800 IU a day had lower compressive stiffness of bone. Next calcium. A 2014 systematic review says clinical studies of how vitamin D insufficiency or supplementing affects fracture healing are few and conclusions unclear. The two studies it found used calcium plus vitamin D together. They saw higher bone density or callus (new bone) at the fracture site; they did not look at healing speed. Finally bone broth. A Kaohsiung, Taiwan assay bought three bone-broth noodle soups, 450 to 550 g of broth per serving. Mean calcium per serving was 14 to 39 mg — 1.4% to 3.9% of a 1000 mg daily recommendation. The authors conclude one serving supplies under 5% of the daily recommendation. China’s recommendation is 800 mg a day at ages 18 to 49 and 1000 mg from 50 up; daily upper limit 2000 mg. The health industry standard that sets these figures also states that long-term excess calcium raises kidney-stone risk. A U.S. trial of 36282 postmenopausal women taking 1000 mg calcium plus 400 IU vitamin D daily, followed a mean 7 years: kidney-stone risk about 17% higher (HR 1.17, 95% CI 1.02–1.34, credible range). Hip fracture about 12% lower, not significant (HR 0.88, 0.72–1.08). It looks worthwhile because of the intuition “bone is made of calcium, so more calcium grows faster.”

### 31. Do not rely on AI chatbots to self-diagnose illness or handle legal issues; verify claims against original sources before seeking treatment, suing, or spending serious money

- Cost: Free. Each verification takes an extra few minutes to half an hour. The habit to break is taking action simply because the AI sounds confident.

On legal questions, at least 58% of answers from general-purpose AI contain fabricated material, and it will play along with mistaken premises built into your questions.
- Benefit: First, health. A pre-registered randomized controlled trial published in *Nature Medicine* in 2026 enrolled 1,298 members of the UK public. One group used AI chatbots for assistance (GPT-4o, Llama 3, Command R+). The control group used their usual methods, mostly web searches or the UK National Health Service (NHS) website. When complete vignettes were fed directly to the AI, it recognized the relevant disease in 94.9% of cases and correctly judged where to seek care in 56.3%. The control group identified the relevant disease more often than AI users, with an odds ratio of 1.76 (95% CI 1.45–2.13). When a user's prompt contained a false legal premise, the AI often accepted it without question. In 2025, the same team tested commercial legal AI tools designed for lawyers. Despite marketing claims that they do not hallucinate, 17% to 33% of their answers contained fabrications. AI feels deceptively useful because it is available on demand, free of charge, and speaks with the confidence of a doctor or lawyer.

### 35. Do not demand an IV drip for a common cold or routine fever, and skip seasonal drips to "flush blood vessels"
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=小 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: Costs nothing, and actually saves the money spent on an IV drip plus several hours sitting in a clinic. The hard part is psychological: many people believe an IV bag makes them recover faster, and some older adults have made seasonal "vessel flushing" a habit, feeling a doctor who refuses them is neglecting their care.
- In plain terms: Health authorities in Guangdong Province list common colds and viral sore throats among conditions that generally do not require an IV drip. At a National Health Commission press briefing, medical experts clarified that seasonal IV drips cannot flush away plaques in blood vessels and can actually overburden the heart and kidneys. Nationwide, more than half of reported adverse drug reactions involve injections, and nine out of ten of those are from intravenous drips.
- Benefit: No clinical trials have directly compared giving an IV drip versus not giving one for a common cold, so this recommendation relies on official regulatory documents and surveillance data. First, regarding colds: in 2017, the Guangdong Provincial Health and Family Planning Commission issued a directive urging primary healthcare facilities to reduce unnecessary IV infusions. The document explicitly reiterated the World Health Organization (WHO) principle for rational drug use: "Do not inject if oral medication is an option; do not administer intravenously if intramuscular injection is an option." Intravenous infusion should only be used in three scenarios: difficulty swallowing; severe absorption issues (such as vomiting or severe diarrhea); or critical, rapidly progressing illness where medication must quickly reach high concentrations in the bloodstream. The directive included a list of common outpatient and emergency conditions that generally do not require IV infusions, with the very first internal medicine item being "upper respiratory tract infection: common cold, viral pharyngitis." Second, regarding "flushing blood vessels": at an August 6, 2025 press conference held by the National Health Commission, a chief physician from Dongfang Hospital of Beijing University of Chinese Medicine debunked this myth, stating: "The claim that seasonal IV infusions can clear blood vessels and prevent cerebral infarction is false." An IV drip cannot "wash away" or "unclog" atherosclerotic plaques. Blindly receiving infusions can increase strain on the heart or kidneys and may even trigger heart failure. Finally, regarding risks: the Annual National Report on Adverse Drug Reaction Monitoring (2024) analyzed cases by route of administration. Among reported adverse drug reactions, injections accounted for 57.2%, while oral administration accounted for 33.1%. Among injections, intravenous administration accounted for 91.0%. This breakdown reflects the proportion of reported adverse events, not the baseline risk per infusion. - Notes: Graded C because evidence is limited to official guidelines and expert statements, with no controlled trials quantifying adverse events avoided by withholding drips. Those who genuinely require an IV should still receive one—such as for severe dehydration, inability to take oral medicine due to vomiting, or critical illness—following a doctor's advice. Asking a doctor for antibiotics for a cold is equally useless; see Section 34, Item 7 (Do not take antibiotics for a cold). For diarrhea, start with oral rehydration salts; see Section 34, Item 8 (Oral rehydration salts). What genuinely works to prevent strokes is controlling blood pressure and blood lipids and taking prescribed medication long-term; see Section 1, Item 7 (Blood pressure measurement) and Section 2, Item 12 (Regular blood lipid screening). Lists of conditions that do not require IV therapy are determined by each province; Guangdong's list dates from 2017, and subsequent updates were not verified. The modest benefit rating is a reasoned judgment: it saves the cost and several hours of a single IV session, while the per-infusion probability of an adverse reaction cannot be calculated from total reports. Beneficiaries are you and the older adults in your family.
