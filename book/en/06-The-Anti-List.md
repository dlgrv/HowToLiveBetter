# 6. The Negative List

This section collects things that "look cheap and effective" but whose original evidence shows a benefit that is close to zero, zero, or negative. Items are ordered by a combination of how much people spend on them and how hard the evidence is: the higher an item sits on the list, the more worth stopping first.
### 1. Do not take a multivitamin to live longer or prevent cardiovascular disease
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: about 0.5–2 yuan/day, 200–700 yuan/year; plus the psychological dependence of long-term pill-taking
- In plain terms: 14,000-odd male physicians took it for eleven years and showed no difference in cardiovascular events or deaths; pooled across 9 trials and over 50,000 people, no effect on mortality shows up either. What you save is 200 to 700 yuan a year. People with a diagnosed nutrient deficiency and pregnant women taking folic acid are not covered by this.
- Benefit: PHS II (14,641 male physicians, median follow-up 11.2 years) major cardiovascular events HR 1.01 (95% CI 0.91–1.10), all-cause mortality HR 0.94 (0.88–1.02); USPSTF 2022 pooled 9 RCTs, 51,550 people — multivitamins showed no association with all-cause mortality, graded I (insufficient evidence); it looks like a good deal because of the "one pill covers all nutrients" intuition and the low unit price
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Sesso HD et al. (2012). Multivitamins in the prevention of cardiovascular disease in men: the Physicians' Health Study II randomized controlled trial. JAMA. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2012.14805>；US Preventive Services Task Force (2022). Vitamin, Mineral, and Multivitamin Supplementation to Prevent Cardiovascular Disease and Cancer. JAMA. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2022.8970>；Gaziano JM 等 (2012). Multivitamins in the Prevention of Cancer in Men. JAMA. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2012.14641>（备注里那项癌症结果）
- Notes: Controversy: the cancer analysis in the same PHS II cohort showed total cancer incidence HR 0.92 (0.86–0.998, P=0.04), cancer death HR 0.88 (0.77–1.01) not reaching significance (Gaziano JM et al. 2012, JAMA, <https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2012.14641>); the effect is tiny and seen only in a male-physician population. People with diagnosed nutrient deficiency, pregnant women (folic acid), and those with highly unbalanced diets are not covered — supplement as medically advised.

### 2. Do not take ordinary fish oil to prevent cardiovascular disease
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: about 1–3 yuan/day, 400–1,000 yuan/year
- In plain terms: in two trials (26,000-odd people and 15,000-odd people with diabetes), 1 gram of fish oil a day made no difference to cardiovascular events or deaths. "Eating fish is good for you" does not equal "swallowing fish oil capsules is good for you"; what you save is 400 to 1,000 yuan a year.
- Benefit: VITAL (25,871 people, 1 g/day, median follow-up 5.3 years) major cardiovascular events HR 0.92 (95% CI 0.80–1.06), all-cause mortality HR 1.02 (0.90–1.15); ASCEND (15,480 people with diabetes, 1 g/day, mean follow-up 7.4 years) serious vascular events RR 0.97 (0.87–1.08), all-cause mortality RR 0.95 (0.86–1.05); it looks like a good deal because "eating fish is beneficial" gets directly equated with "fish oil capsules are beneficial"
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Manson JE et al. (2019). Marine n-3 Fatty Acids and Prevention of Cardiovascular Disease and Cancer. NEJM. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1811403>；ASCEND Study Collaborative Group (2018). Effects of n-3 Fatty Acid Supplements in Diabetes Mellitus. NEJM. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1804989>；Bhatt DL 等 (2019). Cardiovascular Risk Reduction with Icosapent Ethyl for Hypertriglyceridemia. New England Journal of Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1812792>（备注里那项高纯度处方鱼油试验）
- Notes: Controversy: REDUCE-IT (8,179 people with existing cardiovascular disease or diabetes, on statins, triglycerides 135–499 mg/dL) used 4 g/day of pure EPA as a prescription drug: primary endpoint 17.2% vs 22.0%, HR 0.75 (0.68–0.83) (Bhatt DL et al. 2019, NEJM, <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1812792>). That is high-dose prescription EPA in a high-risk population, not supplement-grade fish oil in ordinary people; eating fish itself is outside the scope of this item.

### 3. Do not give vitamin D supplements to people who are not deficient, for the sake of longevity
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: about 0.2–0.5 yuan/day, 100–200 yuan/year; the money is minor — the main cost is the illusion of "believing prevention is already done"
- In plain terms: in two trials (26,000-odd people for 5 years, and 21,000-odd older adults for 5 years), supplementing people who were not deficient to begin with changed nothing for cancer, cardiovascular disease, or death — mortality 5.3% vs 5.1%. People with diagnosed deficiency, osteoporosis, or little sun exposure are not covered; supplement as medically advised.
- Benefit: VITAL (25,871 people, 2000 IU/day, median follow-up 5.3 years) invasive cancer HR 0.96 (95% CI 0.88–1.06), major cardiovascular events HR 0.97 (0.85–1.12), all-cause mortality HR 0.99 (0.87–1.12); D-Health (21,315 Australians over 60, 60,000 IU/month, 5 years) all-cause mortality HR 1.04 (0.93–1.18), mortality 5.3% vs 5.1%; it looks like a good deal because observational studies show people with low vitamin D levels have higher mortality, which gets misread as "supplementing works"
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Manson JE et al. (2019). Vitamin D Supplements and Prevention of Cancer and Cardiovascular Disease. NEJM. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1809944>；Neale RE et al. (2022). The D-Health Trial: a randomised controlled trial of the effect of vitamin D on mortality. Lancet Diabetes Endocrinol. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S2213-8587(21)00345-4>
- Notes: Controversy: neither trial specifically enrolled people who were truly deficient, so the conclusions apply only to "people who are not deficient"; those with diagnosed deficiency, osteoporosis, little sun exposure, infants, and other specific groups should still supplement as medically advised. This item covers only all-cause mortality and cancer/cardiovascular endpoints.

### 4. Do not take antioxidant supplements (beta-carotene, vitamin E, vitamin A) to prevent cancer
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: about 0.5–2 yuan/day; for smokers the cost is a higher lung cancer risk
- In plain terms: this one is not merely useless: pooled across 78 trials and nearly 300,000 people, antioxidant supplements actually pushed mortality up slightly; in two large trials of smokers, beta-carotene raised lung cancer incidence by 18% to 28% and total deaths by 8% to 17%. Smokers and people exposed to asbestos should especially not take these.
- Benefit: Cochrane review (78 RCTs, 296,707 people) antioxidant supplements all-cause mortality RR 1.02 (95% CI 0.98–1.05), low risk-of-bias trials RR 1.04 (1.01–1.07), beta-carotene RR 1.05 (1.01–1.09), vitamin E RR 1.03 (1.00–1.05); ATBC (29,133 male smokers, 20 mg/day) lung cancer incidence up 18% (3%–36%), total deaths up 8% (1%–16%); CARET (18,314 smokers/asbestos-exposed people) lung cancer RR 1.28 (1.04–1.57), all-cause mortality RR 1.17 (1.03–1.33); it looks like a good deal because every step of the chain "antioxidant = anti-aging = cancer prevention" sounds like common sense
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Bjelakovic G et al. (2012). Antioxidant supplements for prevention of mortality in healthy participants and patients with various diseases. Cochrane Database Syst Rev. <https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD007176.pub2>；The Alpha-Tocopherol, Beta Carotene Cancer Prevention Study Group (1994). The effect of vitamin E and beta carotene on the incidence of lung cancer and other cancers in male smokers. NEJM. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJM199404143301501>；Omenn GS et al. (1996). Effects of a combination of beta carotene and vitamin A on lung cancer and cardiovascular disease. NEJM. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJM199605023341802>
- Notes: USPSTF 2022 issued a D grade (recommend against) for beta-carotene and vitamin E; smokers and asbestos-exposed people are the clearly harmed groups; getting antioxidants from vegetables and fruit is outside the scope of this item.

### 5. Do not expect glucosamine/chondroitin to treat knee osteoarthritis
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: about 2–5 yuan/day, 700–1,800 yuan/year
- In plain terms: 1,583 people took it for 24 weeks: in the placebo group 60.1% of people reported pain relief to begin with, and glucosamine beat that by only 3.9 percentage points, chondroitin by 5.3 — neither reaching statistical significance. Seven hundred to eighteen hundred yuan a year, and what you are mostly buying is that sixty-percent placebo effect.
- Benefit: GAIT (1,583 patients with knee osteoarthritis, 24 weeks) pain-response rate placebo 60.1%; glucosamine 3.9 percentage points above placebo (P=0.30), chondroitin 5.3 percentage points (P=0.17), combination 6.5 percentage points (P=0.09) — none significant; the comparator drug celecoxib 10.0 percentage points above (P=0.008); it looks like a good deal because "supplying the raw material for cartilage" sounds like it targets the problem directly, and the placebo itself already yields a sixty-percent response
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Clegg DO et al. (2006). Glucosamine, chondroitin sulfate, and the two in combination for painful knee osteoarthritis. NEJM. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa052771>
- Notes: Controversy: in the same trial, the subgroup with baseline moderate-to-severe pain had a combination response rate of 79.2% vs 54.3% (P=0.002), but the authors explicitly called it an "exploratory analysis." Weight loss and strength training for knee osteoarthritis are evidence-backed alternatives — see Section 2.

### 6. Do not rely on vitamin C to prevent colds
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=小 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: effervescent tablets about 1–2 yuan/tablet, 300–700 yuan/year
- In plain terms: 29 controlled comparisons, 11,306 people: taking vitamin C every day barely reduces the number of colds you catch; starting it only after a cold begins shows no consistent effect either. It does shorten a cold by about 8% — roughly half a day less misery per cold; do the math yourself on whether that is worth 300–500 yuan a year. Marathon runners and soldiers on winter marches are the exceptions: their odds of catching a cold can drop by half.
- Benefit: Cochrane review (29 controlled comparisons, 11,306 people) cold incidence with regular vitamin C supplementation in the general population RR 0.97 (95% CI 0.94–1.00), i.e. essentially no reduction in the number of colds; starting after a cold begins "no consistent effect seen in therapeutic trials"; it looks like a good deal because it is cheap, sweet-and-sour and pleasant to drink, and "vitamin C prevents colds" has been circulating for half a century
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Hemilä H, Chalker E (2013). Vitamin C for preventing and treating the common cold. Cochrane Database Syst Rev. <https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD000980.pub4>
- Notes: Controversy: the same review shows regular long-term supplementation shortens cold duration by about 8% in adults (3%–12%) and 14% in children (7%–21%) — roughly half a day less per cold; marathon runners, skiers, and soldiers on winter marches (5 trials, 598 people) incidence RR 0.48 (0.35–0.64). The vast majority of people do not fall into either of these groups.

### 7. Do not get a "whole-body PET-CT" or a "tumor marker panel" if you have no symptoms
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: whole-body PET-CT about 7,000–10,000 yuan per scan plus ionizing radiation; tumor marker panels a few hundred yuan; the real cost is the repeat testing, biopsies, and surgery that follow a false positive
- In plain terms: screened in people without symptoms, the main output is false positives: of 1,192 symptom-free people given a whole-body CT, 86% had at least one "abnormality" flagged, 37% were called back for further work-up, and the vast majority were benign. In the big trial of ovarian cancer marker screening, the screened group's death rate was actually slightly higher, 0.2% to 3.25% of people went under the knife because of a false positive, and up to 15% of those had serious surgical complications. Seven to ten thousand yuan a scan, plus radiation. The few screenings with actual evidence are a different matter.
- Benefit: USPSTF gives a D grade (recommend against) for ovarian cancer screening in asymptomatic women using CA-125 and ultrasound: in the PLCO trial ovarian cancer mortality was 0.34% in the screened group vs 0.29% in usual care (RR 1.18, 95% CI 0.82–1.71), no benefit; the share undergoing surgery for false positives ranged from 0.2%–3.25% across trials, with up to 15% of those having serious surgical complications; whole-body CT screening of 1,192 asymptomatic people found 86% with at least one "abnormality" and 37% referred for further testing, while "most findings were benign by description"; it looks like a good deal because "one scan checks everything, find it early treat it early" fits intuition
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: US Preventive Services Task Force (2018). Screening for Ovarian Cancer: US Preventive Services Task Force Recommendation Statement. JAMA（同一份建议另有 USPSTF 官方页）. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2017.21926>、<https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/recommendation/ovarian-cancer-screening>；Furtado CD et al. (2005). Whole-body CT screening: spectrum of findings and recommendations in 1192 patients. Radiology. <https://doi.org/10.1148/radiol.2372041741>
- Notes: the tumor marker part rests on RCT evidence; the whole-body CT/PET-CT part has only descriptive studies (equivalent to grade B). Screenings with evidence (colorectal, cervical, breast, and low-dose chest CT for high-risk people) are a different matter — see Section 1; PET-CT for people with existing symptoms or a confirmed cancer diagnosis is a diagnostic and treatment tool, outside the scope of this item.

### 8. Do not expect a fitness band or smartwatch's activity tracking to lose weight for you
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: 200–2,000 yuan for the device, plus the daily attention spent watching the data
- In plain terms: 471 overweight adults did the same lifestyle intervention; the group wearing devices lost 3.5 kg over two years, while the group without them lost 5.9 kg. The device does not help with weight loss — the exercise itself is what works.
- Benefit: IDEA trial (471 overweight/obese adults, 24 months) on top of the same lifestyle intervention, the wearable-device group lost 3.5 kg (95% CI 2.6–4.5) while the non-wearer group lost 5.9 kg (5.0–6.8), a difference of 2.4 kg (1.0–3.7, P=0.002); it looks like a good deal because of the "you can only manage what you quantify" line and the one-time device payment
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Jakicic JM et al. (2016). Effect of Wearable Technology Combined With a Lifestyle Intervention on Long-term Weight Loss: The IDEA Randomized Clinical Trial. JAMA. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2016.12858>
- Notes: a single trial, using early-2010s armband devices; the conclusion cannot be generalized to "all wearables are useless"; exercise itself works — see Section 2. This item only says the device is not a necessary expense for weight loss.

### 9. Do not pay a premium for organic food "for your health"
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: usually 1–3 times the price of ordinary food
- In plain terms: put together, 17 human studies and 223 food-composition studies offer no strong evidence that organic food is more nutritious; only 3 studies looked at actual health outcomes, and they showed no difference either. Organic produce does test positive for pesticide residues 30 percentage points less often, but a detection is not the same as exceeding the limit. Eating more vegetables and fruit is what helps, and whether they are organic does not change that.
- Benefit: systematic review (17 human studies, 223 food-composition studies) "strong evidence is lacking that organic food is significantly more nutritious than conventional food"; only 3 human studies looked at clinical outcomes (allergies, symptomatic infections), with no significant difference between groups; organic produce carries a 30-percentage-point lower risk of detectable pesticide residues, but detection is not the same as exceeding the limit; it looks like a good deal because every step of "no pesticides = safer = healthier" sounds like common sense
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Smith-Spangler C et al. (2012). Are organic foods safer or healthier than conventional alternatives?: a systematic review. Ann Intern Med. <https://doi.org/10.7326/0003-4819-157-5-201209040-00007>
- Notes: human studies on health outcomes are very few; this is "no evidence of benefit" rather than "proof of no benefit"; environment, animal welfare, and taste are outside this item's scope. Eating more vegetables and fruit works in itself, and organic or not does not affect that point.

### 10. Do not spend big money on health supplements, herbal tonic formulas, or restoratives to "regulate your body"
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=大 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: anywhere from a few hundred yuan to over ten thousand; once it becomes a gift, the price often doubles or more
- In plain terms: regulators require that supplement packaging devote no less than 20% of its area to the line "health food is not medicine and cannot replace drugs in treating disease" — the very agency that oversees it does not accept that it cures anything. The most common ingredients (multivitamins, fish oil, vitamin D, antioxidants, glucosamine, vitamin C) were checked one by one in the first six items of this section, and none showed a difference. But most of this money is not really spent for the effect — it is bought for parents, or carried along on a visit, and the thought itself is fine; the problem is only that the goods are wrong. Spend the same money on these instead, each with a source in the book: take your elders to get the vaccines they are due (annual flu vaccine for older adults, item 20 of section 1; shingles vaccine after 50, item 21; pneumococcal vaccine over 65, item 22); buy a blood-pressure monitor and make sure the blood-pressure pills get taken as prescribed (item 7 of section 1); fix up the bathroom and stairs at home and practice balance and leg strength with them (item 13 of section 1); go along to a cancer screening once they reach the recommended age (items 17 to 19 of section 1); for an elder who is bedridden long term, see pressure sores and long-term care insurance, items 7 and 8 of section 17. If you want to bring something to the door, bring fruit, rice and flour, or food they like — more real than an herbal tonic formula.
- Benefit: the State Administration for Market Regulation requires that the main display panel of health food packaging devote no less than 20% of its area to the statement "health food is not medicine and cannot replace drugs in treating disease", and defines the purpose of health food as "supplementing dietary nutrients, maintaining or improving the body's health state, or reducing disease risk factors" — that is, the regulator itself does not recognize any therapeutic effect; the first 6 items of this section already list RCT results for its most common ingredients (multivitamins, fish oil, vitamin D, antioxidants, glucosamine, vitamin C); it looks like a good deal because the claim "doesn't cure but regulates" cannot be falsified, and in gift-giving the purchase decision is driven by sentiment rather than evidence
- Evidence grade: C
- Sources: 国家市场监督管理总局 (2019). 市场监管总局就《保健食品标注警示用语指南》和《保健食品原料目录与保健功能目录管理办法》有关情况举办专题新闻发布会. <https://www.samr.gov.cn/tssps/sjdt/tpxw/art/2023/art_4b658b824b1b4b0ba57c09a56cc93aad.html>
- Notes: this is "no evidence supports it" rather than "proven useless", hence grade C. For products with a clear ingredient, check the corresponding literature in the earlier items; anyone with diagnosed nutrient deficiency or under treatment should follow medical advice. What this item rejects is the product, not the sentiment — if an elder bought it themselves and is already taking it, as long as it is not replacing prescribed medication and nothing has gone wrong, there is no need to go argue about it; arranging the due vaccines and screenings matters more. What really needs blocking is "pay-first retirement investments" and the kind that replaces medication — see item 5 of section 17

### 11. Do not take probiotics long-term without a gut problem
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=小 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: about 2–5 yuan/day, 700–1,800 yuan/year
- In plain terms: pooled over 45 trials in healthy adults: probiotics do not produce a lasting change in the gut microbiome — once you stop, it reverts — and blood lipids do not improve either. Specific situations such as antibiotic-associated diarrhea have their own separate evidence and are not covered by this item.
- Benefit: a review of 45 trials in healthy adults, "failed to support that probiotics can induce a lasting change in the gut microbiome or improve blood lipids in healthy adults"; the microbiome changes are transient and disappear on discontinuation; it looks like a good deal because "gut health" is a concept broad enough to mean anything, and the unit price of a bottle a day is low
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Khalesi S et al. (2019). A review of probiotic supplementation in healthy adults: helpful or hype? Eur J Clin Nutr. <https://doi.org/10.1038/s41430-018-0135-9>
- Notes: the same review reports small improvements in healthy people on measures such as stool consistency and vaginal lactobacilli in women. Antibiotic-associated diarrhea and specific strains for specific indications have independent evidence and are outside this item's scope; this item targets only "daily use by healthy people."

### 12. Do not force yourself to take cold showers "to boost your immunity"
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=小 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: zero money; 30–90 seconds of discomfort daily, and willpower to keep it up through winter; people with cardiovascular disease face risks from cold exposure
- In plain terms: in a trial of 3,018 people, cold shower takers called in sick 29% less often, but the total number of sick days was no different — it looks more like "going to work anyway while sick" than getting sick less. A 2025 pooled analysis of 11 trials: after a cold rinse, inflammation markers briefly rise instead, and immune markers are unchanged. If you enjoy it, fine — just don't treat it as a health investment.
- Benefit: Dutch RCT (3,018 people, 30/60/90 seconds of cold water at the end of a hot shower) sick-leave episodes down 29% (IRR 0.71), but "no significant between-group difference in sick-leave days," with no clinically relevant differences in quality of life, work productivity, or anxiety; 2025 systematic review (11 RCTs, 3,177 people) shows inflammation markers rise rather than fall immediately and 1 hour after cold-water immersion, immune markers are unchanged, stress is reduced only at the 12-hour time point, and the authors call the evidence "limited by few RCTs and small samples"; it looks like a good deal because it is free, plus the intuition "suffering = effective"
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Buijze GA et al. (2016). The Effect of Cold Showering on Health and Work: A Randomized Controlled Trial. PLOS ONE. <https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0161749>；Cain T et al. (2025). Effects of cold-water immersion on health and wellbeing: A systematic review and meta-analysis. PLOS ONE. <https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0317615>
- Notes: Controversy: the 29% fewer sick-leave episodes is a real, significant result, but since sick days do not decrease it looks more like "going to work as usual while sick" than getting sick less; recovery after athletic training is a separate question. Cold showers are harmless in themselves for healthy people; this item only says not to treat them as a health investment.

### 13. Do not buy detox, colon-cleansing, enzyme, or alkaline-water products
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: several hundred to several thousand yuan; fasting-style "detoxes" can also bring low blood sugar and undernutrition
- In plain terms: the review's own words: the detox industry is this large, yet the clinical evidence supporting it is very scarce, and there are no randomized controlled trials of commercial detox diets at all. As for the "acidic constitution causes cancer" claim, screening 8,278 papers left only 1 that qualified, and it showed no association. Fasting-style detoxes can also bring low blood sugar and undernutrition.
- Benefit: review conclusion: "despite the detox industry's vigorous growth, there is very little clinical evidence to support these diets," and "no randomized controlled trials of commercial detox diets"; the systematic review of alkaline diets/alkaline water and cancer screened 8,278 papers, only 1 met the inclusion criteria and showed no association, and the authors state that "promotion of alkaline diets and alkaline water to the public for cancer prevention or treatment is not justified"; it looks like a good deal because the two premises — "there are toxins in your body that must be flushed out" and "an acidic constitution causes cancer" — sound like physiology
- Evidence grade: C
- Sources: Klein AV, Kiat H (2015). Detox diets for toxin elimination and weight management: a critical review of the evidence. J Hum Nutr Diet. <https://doi.org/10.1111/jhn.12286>；Fenton TR, Huang T (2016). Systematic review of the association between dietary acid load, alkaline water and cancer. BMJ Open. <https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2015-010438>
- Notes: this is "no evidence of support," hence grade C. The liver and kidneys already handle the clearance of metabolic waste; without a corresponding disease there is no need for extra "detoxing."

### 14. Do not force yourself to drink water to hit "8 glasses a day"
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=小 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: zero money; a little time and effort, more nighttime bathroom trips
- In plain terms: trace the origin of "8 glasses a day" and you will not find a single study supporting it; on the contrary, surveys of several thousand people's food and fluid intake suggest that much is not needed, because food and other drinks already bring in a lot of water. Drink when thirsty and check your urine color — that is enough. People working in high heat, exercising hard, with a history of kidney stones, or older adults whose thirst response has dulled need to drink proactively.
- Benefit: a literature review of "8×8" (eight 8-oz glasses of water a day) "found no scientific research supporting the 8×8" and instead found that "surveys of food and fluid intake in thousands of adults strongly suggest that such large amounts are not necessary," because food and other beverages already provide plenty of fluid; it looks like a good deal because water is free, the number is neat, and "drink more water" seems harmless in any setting
- Evidence grade: C
- Sources: Valtin H (2002). "Drink at least eight glasses of water a day." Really? Is there scientific evidence for "8 x 8"? Am J Physiol Regul Integr Comp Physiol. <https://doi.org/10.1152/ajpregu.00365.2002>
- Notes: this is "no evidence for a fixed number" rather than "drinking more water is harmful"; drink when thirsty and watch urine color. Those working in high heat, exercising intensely, with a history of kidney stones, or with reduced thirst sensation in older age need to drink proactively and are outside this item's scope.

### 15. Do not pay for fortune-telling, tarot, or astrology to make decisions
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: zero money if you skip it; you give up a shortcut to making up your mind
- In plain terms: in the classic classroom experiment, every student received the exact same "personality analysis" — one copy shared by all — and most found it strikingly accurate; that is why fortune-telling always seems accurate. The real cost goes beyond a few dozen yuan: the online fortune-telling ring in Xixian, Henan first offered free palm readings to draw people in, then collected "incense money" and "offering packages" of up to 3,888 yuan, with more than 50 victims and over 2 million yuan in illicit gains recovered; the ringleader was sentenced to eleven years. Spending a few dozen yuan as entertainment is one thing; using it to decide on quitting your job, marrying, or investing is where the money gets lost.

- Sources: Forer BR (1949). The fallacy of personal validation: A classroom demonstration of gullibility. Journal of Abnormal and Social Psychology. <https://doi.org/10.1037/h0059240>；国家市场监督管理总局 (2026). 市场监管总局公布一批传统工艺市场「打假清源」典型案例. <https://www.samr.gov.cn/xw/zj/art/2026/art_1529399cfe874b8780c4496eb0158020.html>
- Notes: This item says one thing: the claimed effects cannot be verified and no predictive power can be measured. It evaluates no belief or custom, and does not target gifting, keepsakes, or family heirlooms. Keep two things apart: claims of concrete effects are covered by explicit rules, so you can complain; "turning your luck around" has neither research behind it nor any way to be proven false, so only your own budget line can hold it in check. Before buying, treat the piece as an ordinary ornament and ask yourself: "If it changed nothing at all, would I still pay this price?" How to check materials and certificates, and why it makes a poor investment, see items 33 and 34 of section 5 (count bead bracelets and luxury watches as money spent; for gems and jade insist on a CMA report). Paying for fortune-telling: see item 15 of this section (fortune-telling, tarot, astrology).

### 23. Do not expect purchases to fix your mood or your sense of status
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=是 收益=中 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: Zero. The hard part is holding off on the order when your mood is low.
- In plain terms: The more importance people place on money and material things, the lower the well-being they report on average. This comes from 259 samples pooled together, but the association is weak, and it cannot tell which causes which. Another line of research compared buying experiences with buying things: money spent on an experience satisfies more. So when your mood is bad, moving the budget to a trip out or a meal with other people is more likely to help than moving it into a shopping cart.
- Benefit: A meta-analysis — many studies combined into one calculation — covered 259 independent samples and 753 effect sizes. The result: the more materialistic a person is, the lower their self-reported well-being. On the most commonly used multidimensional scale the correlation was r = -0.19 (corrected ρ = -0.24). On scales that measure only the "importance of money" facet, the association is weaker, r = -0.08 to -0.11. Broken down by the kind of outcome measured, the strength varies: strongest with risky health and consumer behaviors and negative self-evaluation, r = -0.28 to -0.44; weakest with life satisfaction and negative mood, r = -0.13 to -0.15. The authors also ran mediation analyses — tracing what carries the association through — and the result suggests it may pass through unmet psychological needs. Another line of research: in two surveys of different populations, respondents said money spent on acquiring a life experience made them happier than money spent on things. In the follow-up laboratory experiment, recalling a purchase made for an experience also produced a better mood than recalling a material purchase (the national-scope figure does not apply; both studies used foreign samples).
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Dittmar H, Bond R, Hurst M, Kasser T (2014). The relationship between materialism and personal well-being: A meta-analysis. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 107(5), 879-924. <https://doi.org/10.1037/a0037409>；Van Boven L, Gilovich T (2003). To do or to have? That is the question. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 85(6), 1193-1202. <https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.85.6.1193>
- Notes: Controversy: most of the included studies asked a group of people at a single point in time and checked whether two things moved together, so they cannot say which comes first. The authors themselves wrote in the conclusion that establishing direction will take more experiments and long-term follow-up. So this item cannot be read as "buying things makes people unhappy" — only as "relying on shopping to make yourself happy is a thin bet." The association is also weak: r = -0.19 explains only a few percentage points of the difference, and people vary widely. Both studies used foreign samples; there is no comparable data from Chinese populations to cite. This item is not against spending on things you love; it is against using shopping as an emotion-regulation tool, because that trains your first response to a bad mood to be another order. Concrete money-saving moves: see section 5. What actually has evidence for a bad mood: see the second half of section 22 and section 29. The extra budget added "to rank one notch above others": see item 24 of this section (moving up a notch).

### 24. Do not spend more to move up a notch among the people around you — a bigger house, a newer car, a different circle
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: Zero. All it takes is asking one more question before raising the budget: is this premium buying the thing itself, or my position in this group. The hard part is holding back when everyone around you has already upgraded.
- In plain terms: Compare people with the same income: the more your neighbors earn, the lower the well-being you report. This happens only among neighbors with the same education as you whom you see often. So the extra money spent to move into a place where everyone is better off than you buys you a move one notch down. But when incomes rise together, people do feel better off — the absolute level still counts.
- Benefit: The US National Survey of Families and Households (NSFH) re-interviewed the same people over two waves. The authors matched this data against local average income. Local average income (PUMA) was estimated from the 5% sample of the 1990 census. After subtracting the person's own income and a set of personal characteristics, the result: the higher the neighbors' income, the lower the person's self-reported well-being. The effect is large, robust to a different specification, and highly significant. The authors put it this way: a rise in neighbors' income lowers well-being by the same order of magnitude as an equal-sized fall in the person's own income. When the comparison is each person against their own earlier self (this is called adding individual fixed effects), the coefficient keeps its direction and a similar size, only the significance drops to the 10% level. Restricting the comparison to within the same state and the same survey wave (California and survey-wave fixed effects) barely changes the result. This shows it is not an artifact of "people who like moving to poorer places were already happier," nor of unmeasured state-level factors. The effect is stronger among people who interact with their neighbors often, and not significant among those who only see friends outside the community. Renters and owners show no difference — which does not support the explanation that "in expensive places housing costs eat your disposable income." Well-being falls only with the income of neighbors with the same education, and barely reacts to the income of neighbors with different education. And it passes mainly through "satisfaction with one's own material situation," not through satisfaction with family life or other domains. Finally, if the person's income and the neighbors' income rise by the same proportion together, well-being still rises.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Luttmer EFP (2005). Neighbors as Negatives: Relative Earnings and Well-Being. The Quarterly Journal of Economics, 120(3), 963–1002. <https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/120.3.963>；Luttmer EFP (2004). Neighbors as Negatives: Relative Earnings and Well-Being. NBER Working Paper No. 10667（本条引用的表述与数量级按这一版逐字核对）. <https://www.nber.org/papers/w10667>
- Notes: Several reasons for the B grade: the data come from a survey that re-interviewed the same people, it is a single study, and the sample is foreign; the outcome is self-reported well-being, not money. The authors did use statistical tools to rule out several confounders — comparing each person with their own earlier self, comparing within the same state and wave — but it is not a randomized trial in the end. There is no comparable data from Chinese populations to cite either. The benefit magnitude is set at "medium," following the convention of item 23 of this section (do not expect purchases to fix your mood). The money saved can indeed reach the tens-of-thousands level, but "how much well-being that saved money buys back" has no numbers behind it, so it is not recorded as "large" at the tens-of-thousands level. This item is not against paying for a bigger house, a quieter neighborhood, or a shorter commute — those buy the thing itself. What it stops is the single reason "my colleagues have all upgraded; I can't fall behind." How to rank housing priorities: see item 18 of section 4 (put commute time first when choosing a place to live). Shopping to regulate mood: see item 23 of this section (do not expect purchases to fix your mood). Constant upward comparison online: see item 21 of section 3 (don't treat "how everyone else is doing" as daily reading).

### 25. Do not believe "willpower is like a muscle: use it a little and it runs out"
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: Zero. What changes is a single sentence.
- In plain terms: The popular story goes: do one self-control-heavy task and you are more likely to give in afterwards, so you should ration your willpower. 23 laboratories with 2,141 people reran this experiment by one shared protocol, and the result came out near zero. Stop explaining yourself with "my willpower is used up today," and stop paying for courses that teach you to build up willpower like a reserve.
- Benefit: A multi-laboratory preregistered replication, 23 laboratories and 2,141 participants in total, reran the sequential-task paradigm with the standardized protocol from Sripada et al.: first a task that drains self-control, then a second task that needs it. The pooled result showed the ego-depletion effect was very small, and the 95% confidence interval included 0 (d = 0.04, 95% CI −0.07 to 0.15). On that basis the authors discussed whether the "self-control as a limited resource" model still stands.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Hagger MS, Chatzisarantis NLD, Alberts H, et al. (2016). A Multilab Preregistered Replication of the Ego-Depletion Effect. Perspectives on Psychological Science, 11(4), 546–573. <https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691616652873>
- Notes: What this item rejects is the model that "self-control is a resource that gets used up," not the fact that "people get tired." Too little sleep or working too long in one stretch does lower performance — that is a different matter, handled by the sleep and working-hours items of section 3. What actually has randomized-trial support is changing the environment and rewording the plan: see item 10 of section 4 (move what you want to avoid out of reach) and item 1 of section 4 (write it as "at what time, where, doing what when X happens"). The other items in this section count money; this one counts time and energy, and the two are not converted into each other.

### 26. Do not count on breakfast or 16:8 intermittent fasting to control your weight; pick the meal timing you can keep up long term
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=小 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: Zero. What you save is the daily mental effort of wrestling with meal timing.
- In plain terms: The popular claim is that skipping breakfast makes you gorge at lunch and dinner, so the day's total ends up higher. The randomized trials say otherwise: breakfast skippers ate more than two hundred kcal less over the day and were slightly lighter. Compared with simply eating less, 16:8 shows no difference in weight at the one-year mark. People who skip breakfast long term do have about seventeen percent more cardiovascular disease, while all-cause mortality shows no difference.
- Benefit: Start with the claim that "skipping breakfast makes you eat more later." A meta-analysis pooled 13 randomized trials. People assigned to eat breakfast took in 259.79 kcal more per day than the no-breakfast group (95% CI 78.87–440.71, the credible range) and weighed 0.44 kg more (0.07–0.82). In other words, breakfast skippers did not make it back at lunch and dinner. These trials were all short: the weight ones averaged 7 weeks, the intake ones 2 weeks. Now the 16:8 style of eating only within fixed hours. One trial had 116 overweight adults go 12 weeks: the group eating only from noon to 8 pm and the group eating three regular meals a day differed in weight by −0.26 kg (−1.30 to 0.78), not significant. Another was done in Guangzhou: 139 patients with obesity followed for 12 months. Time-restricted eating plus calorie restriction beat calorie restriction alone by 1.8 kg (−4.0 to 0.4), also not significant. Finally, whether long-term breakfast skipping harms you. In a pooled sample of 2.38 million people, breakfast skippers had about 17% higher cardiovascular disease risk (OR 1.17, 1.09–1.26) and about 49% higher cardiovascular death (OR 1.49, 1.20–1.84). But these are records-only, unrandomized studies. In the original study that followed people 17 to 23 years, the risk ratio for all-cause death was 1.19 with a credible range of 0.99 to 1.42, which crosses 1 — not a difference.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Sievert K, Hussain SM, Page MJ, 等 (2019). Effect of breakfast on weight and energy intake: systematic review and meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials. BMJ. <https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.l42>；Lowe DA, Wu N, Rohdin-Bibby L, 等 (2020). Effects of Time-Restricted Eating on Weight Loss and Other Metabolic Parameters in Women and Men With Overweight and Obesity. JAMA Internal Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2020.4153>；Liu D, Huang Y, Huang C, 等 (2022). Calorie Restriction with or without Time-Restricted Eating in Weight Loss. New England Journal of Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa2114833>；Zhang H, Zhang S, Liu Y, 等 (2025). The association between skipping breakfast and cardiovascular disease: a meta analysis. Frontiers in Cardiovascular Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.3389/fcvm.2025.1565806>；Rong S, Snetselaar LG, Xu G, 等 (2019). Association of Skipping Breakfast With Cardiovascular and All-Cause Mortality. Journal of the American College of Cardiology. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jacc.2019.01.065>；Fernandes-Alves D, Teixeira GP, Guimarães KC, Crispim CA (2026). Systematic Review and Meta-analysis of Randomized Clinical Trials Comparing Time-Restricted Eating With and Without Caloric Restriction for Weight Loss. Nutrition Reviews. <https://doi.org/10.1093/nutrit/nuaf053>；Sun T, Zhang L, Lu Y, 等 (2024). Non-linear relationship between the first meal time of the day and gallstone incidence in American adults. Frontiers in Nutrition. <https://doi.org/10.3389/fnut.2024.1521707>；Liu T, Wang Y, Wang X, 等 (2023). Habitually Skipping Breakfast Is Associated with the Risk of Gastrointestinal Cancers: Evidence from the Kailuan Cohort Study. Journal of General Internal Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.1007/s11606-023-08094-7>
- Notes: Controversy. The 17% for cardiovascular disease comes from records-only, unrandomized studies. Breakfast skippers are more likely to smoke, drink, sit all day, and earn less to begin with, and those factors cannot be adjusted away cleanly. So how much of that 17% is caused by breakfast itself cannot be said. The all-cause mortality figure crossed 1 to begin with. The controversy on the other side concerns 16:8: pooling 30 trials and 1,341 people, when both arms ate the same amount, the time-restricted group lost 1.46 kg more (−2.65 to −0.26), at the cost of 0.41 kg more lean mass lost. But most of those trials were short, and the longest and strictest one — twelve months — found no difference. Long fasting stretches also connect to the gallbladder. In a cross-sectional survey of 6,547 people, each hour later in the day's first meal came with about 5% more detected gallstones (OR 1.05, 1.02–1.08); people whose first meal slipped to 9 am to 2 pm had about 49% more than earlier eaters (OR 1.49, 1.24–1.77). The mechanism: the longer bile sits in the gallbladder, the more easily stones precipitate. But this is a same-time-point survey, and it may also be that a gallbladder problem came first and changed the eating habits. The Kailuan cohort in China followed people 5.6 years with 369 digestive-tract cancers: breakfast skippers had 2.32 times the colorectal cancer risk (1.34–4.01) and 5.43 times the gallbladder and extrahepatic bile duct cancer risk (1.34–21.93). The credible range on the latter runs to 21.93 — too few events, so treat it only as a lead. Put the sides together: for weight control, meal timing matters very little; do not spend willpower on it. As for whether never eating breakfast long term is harmful, all the existing evidence is association and cannot prove causation, but it leans consistently unfavorable — the safe choice is to eat normally, just without expecting it to slim you. The main causes of gastritis and stomach ulcers are not an empty stomach but Helicobacter pylori and long-term painkiller use; get the test done — see item 23 of section 1 (test for H. pylori and eradicate it if positive). What to do when a checkup finds gallstones that have never hurt: see item 20 of this section (gallstones found by checkup that never hurt — do not remove the gallbladder preventively). For weight control, what actually has evidence is what you eat and how much: see item 28 of section 2 (five servings of fruit and vegetables a day), item 29 of section 2 (cut back on ultra-processed food), and item 33 of section 2 (keep BMI at 20–25). Not applicable to people with diabetes, people on glucose-lowering medication, or pregnancy — a skipped meal can bring low blood sugar; follow medical advice. Extreme fasting and vomiting are a different matter: see item 1 of section 28 (do not control weight with extreme dieting, fasting, or vomiting).
