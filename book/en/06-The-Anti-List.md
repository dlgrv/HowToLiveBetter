# 6. Things to avoid

This section lists products and practices that *seem* cheap and effective at first glance. Yet once we examine the original research, their benefits are practically zero — or even negative. The items are ordered based on two factors: how much money people typically spend on them and how strong the supporting evidence is. The higher up they appear, the sooner you should stop using them.

The entries are grouped by topic, with the corresponding item numbers shown in parentheses.

**Supplements and dietary aids**: Multivitamins (Item 1), regular fish oil (Item 2), vitamin D supplements (Item 3), antioxidant pills aimed at cancer prevention (Item 4), chondroitin for knee arthritis (Item 5), vitamin C to ward off colds (Item 6), splurging on premium supplements (Item 10), long-term probiotic use (Item 11), detox or colon-cleansing products (Item 13), and excessive calcium intake after a bone fracture (Item 30).

**Medical screenings and minor health issues**: Full-body PET-CT scans plus tumor marker panels (Item 7), routine general health checkups (Item 18), avoiding medication when uric acid levels are high but there’s no pain (Item 19), refraining from gallbladder removal when there’s no pain or symptoms (Item 20), and not cutting calcium intake to prevent kidney stones (Item 21).

**Wellness myths**: Fitness trackers won’t help you lose weight (Item 8), organic food offers no extra health benefits (Item 9), cold showers won’t boost immunity (Item 12), there’s no need to force yourself to drink water (Item 14), blue-light-blocking glasses are useless (Item 16), donating blood isn’t a health strategy (Item 17), breakfast or intermittent fasting won’t magically control weight (Item 26), and proper lifting techniques alone won’t prevent back pain (Item 29).

**Spending for luck or mood**: Paying for fortune-telling or astrology readings (Item 15), buying crystals, Pixiu statues, or similar trinkets (Item 22), purchasing items solely to lift your mood (Item 23), and spending extra cash to move up to a higher social tier (Item 24).

**Willpower and sexuality**: The idea that willpower works like a muscle is a myth (Item 25), masturbation does no harm to the body (Item 27), and attempts to change sexual orientation are both ineffective and harmful (Item 28).

**AI-generated advice**: Relying on AI chatbots to diagnose medical conditions is a bad idea (Item 31).
### 1. Don’t take multivitamins just to live longer or protect your heart and blood vessels  
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: About 0.5–2 yuan per day, or 200–700 yuan per year. Taking them takes almost no time — just one pill each day. Over time, though, people can become dependent, feeling they’ve missed out on something important if they skip a day.  
- In plain terms: 14,400 male doctors took multivitamins for 11 years. The number of serious heart or blood vessel problems and deaths among them was no different from those who didn’t take them. When nine studies involving over 50,000 people were combined, there was still no effect on overall mortality. Skipping multivitamins saves you 200–700 yuan each year. This does not apply to people who truly lack certain nutrients or pregnant women needing folic acid.  
- Benefit: The PHS II trial followed 14,641 male doctors for a median of 11.2 years. Both groups — those taking multivitamins and those who didn’t — showed the same rate of major cardiovascular events. The hazard ratio was 1.01 (meaning no real difference), with a 95% confidence interval of 0.91–1.10. Similarly, overall mortality rates were identical, with a hazard ratio of 0.94 (95% CI 0.88–1.02). In 2022, USPSTF evaluated nine randomized controlled trials involving 51,550 participants and concluded there is no link between multivitamin use and reduced mortality; they assigned it a Grade I rating, meaning evidence is insufficient to draw any firm conclusions. The idea that multivitamins are a good deal stems partly from the convenience of “one pill for all nutrients” and their low daily cost.  
- Evidence grade: A  
- Sources:Sesso HD et al. (2012). Multivitamins in the prevention of cardiovascular disease in men: the Physicians' Health Study II randomized controlled trial. JAMA. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2012.14805>；US Preventive Services Task Force (2022). Vitamin, Mineral, and Multivitamin Supplementation to Prevent Cardiovascular Disease and Cancer. JAMA. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2022.8970>；Gaziano JM 等 (2012). Multivitamins in the Prevention of Cancer in Men. JAMA. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2012.14641>（备注里那项癌症结果）
- Notes: A point of debate: among these same male doctors, cancer rates were slightly lower among multivitamin users — about 8% fewer cases (95% CI 0.86–0.998, P=0.04, just barely significant). Deaths from cancer were also reduced by roughly 12% (95% CI 0.77–1.01), though this range includes 1, so it may simply be a coincidence. Any such benefit is very small and has only been observed in male doctors. It does not apply to people with proven nutrient deficiencies, pregnant women needing folic acid, or those with extremely poor diets. 〔14000〕

### 2. Don’t take regular fish oil just to prevent heart disease  
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: Roughly 1–3 yuan per day, or 400–1,000 yuan per year. It requires no extra time to take.  
- In plain terms: Two large trials enrolled 26,000 and 15,000 people with diabetes respectively. Those who took 1 gram of fish oil daily showed no difference from non-users in terms of major cardiovascular events or death rates. The fact that eating fish is healthy does not mean fish oil capsules are equally beneficial. Skipping them can save you 400–1,000 yuan each year.  
- Benefit: The VITAL trial followed 25,871 participants for 5.3 years; those taking 1 gram of fish oil daily showed no difference in major cardiovascular events or mortality compared to non-users. The hazard ratio for major events was 0.92 (95% CI 0.80–1.06), well below 1, indicating no benefit. All-cause mortality showed a similar pattern (HR 1.02, 95% CI 0.90–1.15). The ASCEND trial, involving 15,480 diabetic patients over 7.4 years, also found no difference in severe vascular events (RR 0.97, 95% CI 0.87–1.08) or mortality (RR 0.95, 95% CI 0.86–1.05). These results appear favorable only because many assume “eating fish is healthy” equates to “fish oil capsules are beneficial.”  
- Evidence grade: A  
- Sources:Manson JE et al. (2019). Marine n-3 Fatty Acids and Prevention of Cardiovascular Disease and Cancer. NEJM. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1811403>；ASCEND Study Collaborative Group (2018). Effects of n-3 Fatty Acid Supplements in Diabetes Mellitus. NEJM. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1804989>；Bhatt DL 等 (2019). Cardiovascular Risk Reduction with Icosapent Ethyl for Hypertriglyceridemia. New England Journal of Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1812792>（备注里那项高纯度处方鱼油试验）
- Notes: Controversy exists: One trial did show benefits. The REDUCE-IT trial included 8,179 high-risk patients with existing cardiovascular disease or diabetes, already on statins and with elevated triglyceride levels (135–499 mg/dL). Those taking 4 grams of prescription-grade pure EPA daily saw a 25% lower risk of events (HR 0.75, 95% CI 0.68–0.83). However, this was a high-dose prescription medication for a specific high-risk group, not ordinary fish oil supplements for the general population. This discussion excludes the health benefits of eating fish itself. 〔17.2〕

### 3. Don’t give vitamin D supplements to people who already have adequate levels
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=金钱 -->

- Cost: It costs roughly 0.2–0.5 yuan per day, or 100–200 yuan per year. That isn’t much money, but the real cost is the false sense of protection it creates.
- In plain terms: For people who already have normal vitamin D levels, taking supplements for five years does not lower their risk of cancer, cardiovascular disease, or death compared to those who don’t supplement. This does not apply to people who are actually deficient, have osteoporosis, or get little sun exposure — they should follow their doctor’s advice.
- Benefit: The VITAL trial included 25,871 participants who took 2,000 IU of vitamin D daily for a median of 5.3 years. No difference was seen in cancer rates, cardiovascular disease, or overall mortality. The hazard ratio for aggressive cancers was 0.96 (95% CI 0.88–1.06); for major cardiovascular events it was 0.97 (95% CI 0.85–1.12); for all-cause death it was 0.99 (95% CI 0.87–1.12). The D-Health trial involved 21,315 Australians over 60 who received 60,000 IU monthly for five years; again, no significant differences appeared, with an all-cause mortality HR of 1.04 (95% CI 0.93–1.18). The death rates were 5.3% versus 5.1%. The impression that supplementation is beneficial arises because some studies only observe people without intervening, finding higher mortality among those with low vitamin D levels — this is wrongly interpreted as proof that supplements help.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources:Manson JE et al. (2019). Vitamin D Supplements and Prevention of Cancer and Cardiovascular Disease. NEJM. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1809944>；Neale RE et al. (2022). The D-Health Trial: a randomised controlled trial of the effect of vitamin D on mortality. Lancet Diabetes Endocrinol. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S2213-8587(21)00345-4>
- Notes: Note: Neither trial specifically enrolled people known to be deficient in vitamin D, so these results apply only to those who already have adequate levels. Individuals with confirmed deficiency, osteoporosis, limited sun exposure, or infants should still follow medical guidance. These trials examined only overall mortality, cancer, and cardiovascular outcomes; no other effects were evaluated.

### 4. Don’t take antioxidant supplements to prevent cancer (beta-carotene, vitamins E and A)
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: Approximately 0.5 to 2 yuan per day. For smokers, the real cost is a higher risk of lung cancer.
- In plain terms: This isn’t just ineffective — it’s actually harmful. A combined analysis of 78 trials involving nearly 300,000 participants showed that people taking antioxidant supplements had a slightly higher overall mortality rate. Two large trials focused specifically on smokers found that beta-carotene increased the likelihood of developing lung cancer by 18% to 28% and raised mortality risk by 8% to 17%. Smokers and individuals exposed to asbestos should absolutely avoid these supplements.
- Benefit: Cochrane reviewed 78 randomized controlled trials with 296,707 participants total. The overall risk ratio for mortality among supplement users was 1.02 (95% CI 0.98–1.05). When only higher-quality trials were considered, the risk ratio rose to 1.04 (1.01–1.07). Specifically, beta-carotene raised mortality risk to 1.05 (1.01–1.09) and vitamin E to 1.03 (1.00–1.05). The ATBC trial tracked 29,133 male smokers who took 20 mg daily; it found a 18% increase in lung cancer incidence (3%–36%) and an 8% rise in overall mortality (1%–16%). The CARET trial involved 18,314 smokers or asbestos-exposed individuals; it showed a 28% higher risk of lung cancer (RR 1.28, 1.04–1.57) and a 17% higher risk of all-cause mortality (RR 1.17, 1.03–1.33). The perception that these supplements are beneficial stems from the flawed logic that antioxidants prevent aging, which in turn prevents cancer — each step of this reasoning seems intuitive but is untrue.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources:Bjelakovic G et al. (2012). Antioxidant supplements for prevention of mortality in healthy participants and patients with various diseases. Cochrane Database Syst Rev. <https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD007176.pub2>；The Alpha-Tocopherol, Beta Carotene Cancer Prevention Study Group (1994). The effect of vitamin E and beta carotene on the incidence of lung cancer and other cancers in male smokers. NEJM. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJM199404143301501>；Omenn GS et al. (1996). Effects of a combination of beta carotene and vitamin A on lung cancer and cardiovascular disease. NEJM. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJM199605023341802>
- Notes: The U.S. Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF) rated both beta-carotene and vitamin E as “D” in 2022, meaning they advise against their use. Smokers and asbestos-exposed individuals have already suffered harm from taking them. Naturally occurring antioxidants found in fruits and vegetables are not included in this recommendation; you may continue eating those as normal.

### 5. Don’t expect glucosamine/chondroitin to treat knee osteoarthritis  
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: Roughly 2–5 RMB per day, or 700–1,800 RMB per year.  
- In plain terms: Even people with knee osteoarthritis who took a placebo for 24 weeks reported less pain in about 60% of cases. Those taking glucosamine or chondroitin saw only a 4–5% improvement over this baseline — a difference so small it could easily be due to chance. Spending 700–1,800 RMB yearly nets you little more than that same 60% placebo effect.  
- Benefit: The GAIT trial enrolled 1,583 patients with knee osteoarthritis who took the supplements for 24 weeks. Among those receiving a placebo, 60.1% also reported pain relief. Glucosamine produced just a 3.9% extra improvement (P=0.30), while chondroitin added 5.3% (P=0.17). Taking both together yielded a 6.5% gain (P=0.09) — all differences far too small to rule out random variation. For comparison, celecoxib, a genuine medication used as a control in the same trial, delivered a 10.0% improvement (P=0.008). The supplements appear “cost-effective” only because “cartilage-building ingredients” sound relevant to the condition, and because a placebo alone already helped six out of ten patients.  
- Evidence grade: A  
- Sources:Clegg DO et al. (2006). Glucosamine, chondroitin sulfate, and the two in combination for painful knee osteoarthritis. NEJM. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa052771>
- Notes: Controversy: In this same trial, among participants with severe baseline pain, those taking both supplements reported a 79.2% improvement rate versus 54.3% in the placebo group (P=0.002). However, researchers note this finding was derived post-hoc from the data and should be viewed as a tentative clue rather than a definitive conclusion. Proven strategies for managing knee osteoarthritis remain weight loss and muscle-strengthening exercises — see Section 2.

### 6. Don’t rely on vitamins C to prevent colds
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=小 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: Each effervescent tablet costs roughly 1–2 RMB; annual expense ranges from 300 to 700 RMB.
- In plain terms: Regularly taking vitamin C won’t significantly lower your chances of catching a cold, and taking it only after you’ve already fallen ill yields inconsistent results. For the general population, daily intake shortens the duration of a cold by about 8%, which equals roughly half a day of reduced discomfort. Whether this is worth the annual cost is up to personal judgment. Marathon runners, skiers, and soldiers stationed in cold climates are exceptions: for them, daily vitamin C intake cuts cold incidence by half.
- Benefit: Cochrane reviewed 29 randomized controlled trials involving 11,306 participants. For ordinary people, regular vitamin C consumption does not meaningfully reduce overall cold incidence; the risk ratio here is 0.97, meaning there is virtually no difference between groups. The 95% confidence interval for this figure is 0.94–1.00. Trials where vitamin C was taken only after cold symptoms appeared showed mixed results: some indicated a benefit, others did not. For the general population, long-term daily intake does shorten cold duration by about 8% in adults (95% CI 3%–12%) and 14% in children (95% CI 7%–21%). Marathon runners, skiers, and soldiers in cold climates again form an exception: 5 trials with 598 participants showed their cold incidence dropped by roughly half, with a risk ratio of 0.48 (95% CI 0.35–0.64). The perceived value of vitamin C for cold prevention is largely driven by its low cost, pleasant sweet-and-sour taste, and the fact that this claim has been widely circulated for half a century.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources:Hemilä H, Chalker E (2013). Vitamin C for preventing and treating the common cold. Cochrane Database Syst Rev. <https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD000980.pub4>
- Notes: Controversy note: The same Cochrane review confirms that regular vitamin C intake does shorten the duration of cold symptoms. For adults, this reduction is about 8% (95% CI 3%–12%); for children, it is 14% (95% CI 7%–21%). This translates to roughly half a day of reduced illness per cold episode. Marathon runners, skiers, and soldiers in cold climates are again the exception: 5 trials with 598 participants showed their cold incidence fell by about half (risk ratio 0.48, 95% CI 0.35–0.64). The vast majority of people do not fall into these special groups. 〔500〕

### 7. Don’t get a whole-body PET-CT or tumor marker panel just because you have no symptoms  
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=死亡率 -->
- Cost: A whole-body PET-CT costs roughly 7,000–10,000 RMB per scan and exposes you to ionizing radiation. Tumor marker panels cost a few hundred RMB. The real cost comes later: if a test flags an “abnormality” that turns out to be a false positive, you’ll need follow-up tests, biopsies, and possibly surgery — all of which add up financially and physically.  
- In plain terms: For people without symptoms, most screening results are false alarms. Over 80% of those who get a whole-body CT find at least one “abnormality,” and nearly 40% are called back for more tests — yet most of these findings are benign. In women tested for ovarian cancer markers, the death rate from ovarian cancer is actually slightly higher; some even undergo unnecessary surgery because of a false positive.  
- Benefit: The USPSTF, which evaluates preventive measures in the U.S., gave a D rating to using CA-125 and ultrasound to screen asymptomatic women for ovarian cancer — meaning it does not recommend this screening. Their basis is the PLCO trial, which showed a 0.34% death rate from ovarian cancer in the screened group versus 0.29% in the control group (RR 1.18, 95% CI 0.82–1.71). Screening offers no real benefit. The rate of unnecessary surgery due to false positives ranges from 0.2% to 3.25% across studies, with up to 15% of those patients experiencing serious complications. Another study screened 1,192 symptom-free individuals with whole-body CT; 86% had at least one “abnormality” noted, and 37% were advised to undergo further tests. Most of these findings were benign. The idea that screening is “worth it” because it enables early detection stems largely from intuition rather than solid evidence.  
- Evidence grade: A  
- Sources:US Preventive Services Task Force (2018). Screening for Ovarian Cancer: US Preventive Services Task Force Recommendation Statement. JAMA（同一份建议另有 USPSTF 官方页）. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2017.21926>、<https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/recommendation/ovarian-cancer-screening>；Furtado CD et al. (2005). Whole-body CT screening: spectrum of findings and recommendations in 1192 patients. Radiology. <https://doi.org/10.1148/radiol.2372041741>
- Notes: The tumor marker portion of this recommendation is supported by randomized controlled trials. In contrast, the evidence for whole-body CT and PET-CT relies mainly on observational follow-ups without proper control groups, so it falls to a B rating. Other proven screening methods — such as tests for colorectal and cervical cancer, breast cancer screening, or low-dose chest CT for high-risk individuals — are discussed in Section 1. For people already showing symptoms or diagnosed with cancer, PET-CT is a diagnostic tool, not a screening measure, and is handled separately.

### 8. Don’t expect fitness trackers to help you lose weight
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: Devices cost between 200 and 2,000 RMB. Plus, you have to constantly check those numbers every day.
- In plain terms: Overweight people following the same diet and exercise plans lost 3.5 kg in two years if they wore fitness trackers, while those who didn’t wear any lost 5.9 kg. To lose weight, buying such devices won’t help — actual exercise does the job.
- Benefit: The IDEA trial involved 471 overweight or obese adults and followed them for 24 months. Everyone followed identical diet and exercise routines; the only difference was whether they wore wearable devices. The group wearing trackers lost 3.5 kg (95% CI 2.6–4.5). The other group lost 5.9 kg (95% CI 5.0–6.8), a difference of 2.4 kg (95% CI 1.0–3.7, P=0.002). It seems cost-effective only because the idea “you must measure to manage” sounds logical, and the devices are a one-time purchase.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources:Jakicic JM et al. (2016). Effect of Wearable Technology Combined With a Lifestyle Intervention on Long-term Weight Loss: The IDEA Randomized Clinical Trial. JAMA. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2016.12858>
- Notes: This is just one trial, using devices from the early 2010s worn on the arm; we can’t conclude that all wearable devices are useless. Exercise itself remains effective, as discussed in Section 2. The bottom line: buying fitness trackers isn’t necessary if you want to lose weight.

### 9. Don’t pay a premium for organic food just to be “healthier”
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: Typically 1 to 3 times the price of regular versions of the same foods.
- In plain terms: There is no solid evidence that organic food is more nutritious. Only three studies have actually examined how eating organic affects human health, and none showed any difference. While organic produce does have pesticide residues at a rate 30 percentage points lower than conventional produce, finding residues does not mean they exceed safety limits. Eating more fruits and vegetables is beneficial regardless of whether they are organic or not.
- Benefit: One systematic review combined findings from 17 human studies and 223 studies analyzing food composition. Its conclusion was that “there is insufficient evidence to show organic food is significantly more nutritious than conventional food.” Of those human studies, just three looked at actual health outcomes such as allergies and symptomatic infections; no differences were observed between organic and conventional diets. The perception that organic food offers better value stems from the common-sense chain of reasoning: “no pesticides means greater safety, and greater safety means better health.”
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources:Smith-Spangler C et al. (2012). Are organic foods safer or healthier than conventional alternatives?: a systematic review. Ann Intern Med. <https://doi.org/10.7326/0003-4819-157-5-201209040-00007>
- Notes: Very few studies have directly examined how organic food impacts human health. Therefore, the conclusion is “there is no evidence that organic food is superior,” rather than “it has been proven to offer no benefits.” Environmental impact, animal welfare, and taste are not addressed here. Eating more fruits and vegetables remains beneficial regardless of whether they are organic or not.

### 10. Don’t spend a lot of money on health supplements, tonics, or “body‑balancing” products
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=大 口径=金钱 -->

- Cost: Prices range from a few hundred to tens of thousands of yuan. Once packaged as gifts, the price often multiplies several times over.
- In plain terms: Multivitamins, fish oil, and vitamin D — common ingredients in these products — have all been examined in the first six sections of this chapter. None of them show any real benefit. The statement “Health supplements are not medicines and cannot replace medical treatment” must appear on every package by regulatory rule. Many people buy these items as gifts for their parents, and that intention is admirable; the problem is simply choosing the wrong products.
- Benefit: The real benefit is simply saving that money. The reasons are laid out in the first six sections: for the most common supplement ingredients — multivitamins, fish oil, vitamin D, antioxidants, glucosamine, and vitamin C — randomized controlled trials found no expected advantages; the corresponding numbers are listed in the “Benefit” columns of those sections. Regulatory rules only define what claims a product may make; they do not prove a product is useless. The State Administration for Market Regulation requires that at least 20 % of a health‑supplement package’s front surface carry the mandatory line: “Health supplements are not medicines and cannot replace medical treatment.” The same rules limit their purpose to “supplementing dietary nutrients, maintaining or improving overall health, or reducing disease risk factors.” These products seem worthwhile because the phrase “they don’t cure disease, they only help balance the body” is technically true; no one can prove it false. When bought as gifts, the value comes from the gesture, not from any scientific evidence.
- Evidence grade: C
- Sources:国家市场监督管理总局 (2019). 市场监管总局就《保健食品标注警示用语指南》和《保健食品原料目录与保健功能目录管理办法》有关情况举办专题新闻发布会. <https://www.samr.gov.cn/tssps/sjdt/tpxw/art/2023/art_4b658b824b1b4b0ba57c09a56cc93aad.html>
- Notes: The conclusion is “no evidence supporting benefit,” not “proven ineffective,” which is why the grade is C. For products whose ingredients are clearly listed, check the relevant studies described in the earlier sections. People who truly lack certain nutrients or who are undergoing medical treatment should follow their doctor’s advice. What should be avoided are the products themselves, not the goodwill behind the gift. If an elderly person is already taking them without substituting them for prescribed medicines and shows no adverse effects, there’s no need to argue; instead, make sure they receive recommended vaccines and screenings. That same amount of money can be spent on far better options: annual flu shots for seniors (see Section 1, Item 20); shingles vaccine after age 50 (Section 1, Item 21); pneumococcal vaccine for those over 65 (Section 1, Item 22). Buying a blood‑pressure monitor and ensuring they take antihypertensive meds as directed (Section 1, Item 7) also yields real benefits. Home modifications such as bathroom and stair upgrades, plus balance and leg‑strength exercises (Section 1, Item 13), are equally valuable. Age‑appropriate cancer screenings should also be arranged (Sections 1, Items 17‑19). For bedridden seniors, pressure‑ulcer prevention and long‑term care insurance are covered in Section 17, Items 7‑8. Instead of tonics, delivering fresh fruit, rice, noodles, or other favorite foods is far more practical. Two types of schemes should be avoided: pre‑payment “retirement‑investment” programs and products marketed as substitutes for prescribed medicines (see Section 17, Item 5).

### 11. Don’t take probiotics long-term if you have no gut problems
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=小 口径=金钱 -->

- Cost: Approximately 2–5 RMB per day, or 700–1800 RMB per year.
- In plain terms: A review of 45 trials conducted on healthy adults found that probiotics do not cause lasting changes to the gut microbiome; once people stop taking them, the microbial composition returns to its original state. There was also no improvement in blood lipid levels. Separate evidence exists for cases where antibiotics cause diarrhea, but that falls outside the scope of this review.
- Benefit: The same review concluded that there is no reliable evidence that probiotics can produce lasting alterations in gut flora or improve blood lipid profiles in healthy adults. While gut bacteria do shift temporarily while taking probiotics, these changes reverse once use stops. The practice may seem cost-effective simply because “gut health” is a broad concept that can be loosely applied, and the daily cost of a single probiotic dose is quite low.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources:Khalesi S et al. (2019). A review of probiotic supplementation in healthy adults: helpful or hype? Eur J Clin Nutr. <https://doi.org/10.1038/s41430-018-0135-9>
- Notes: The review also notes a few minor benefits in healthy individuals, such as firmer, better-textured stools and increased Lactobacillus levels in women’s vaginas. Separate evidence exists for probiotics used to treat antibiotic-induced diarrhea or for specific strains targeting particular medical conditions; those cases are not covered here. This review pertains solely to healthy adults using probiotics as a daily supplement.

### 12. Don’t force yourself to take cold showers just to “boost immunity”
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=小 口径=金钱 -->

- Cost: No cost at all. It requires enduring discomfort for 30 to 90 seconds each day; staying consistent in winter demands real willpower. People with cardiovascular conditions should be cautious, as cold exposure poses certain risks for them.

- In plain terms: In a trial involving 3,018 participants, those who took cold showers had 29% fewer sick days. However, the total number of days taken off for illness remained identical across groups, so it doesn’t actually reduce overall illness. A 2025 systematic review analyzing 11 trials found that cold showers temporarily raised markers of inflammation while leaving immune indicators unchanged. Feel free to take cold showers if you enjoy them, but don’t treat them as a genuine health investment.

- Benefit: A randomized controlled trial conducted in the Netherlands enrolled 3,018 participants who took hot showers followed by 30, 60, or 90 seconds of cold exposure. These individuals showed a 29% reduction in sick days (IRR 0.71). Yet the study explicitly notes “no significant difference in total sick days between groups,” meaning the overall duration of illness remained equal. No meaningful differences emerged regarding quality of life, work productivity, or anxiety levels. The 2025 review synthesized 11 RCTs totaling 3,177 participants; it revealed that cold exposure temporarily increased inflammatory markers both immediately and one hour post-shower, with no change in immune markers. Stress levels decreased only at the 12-hour mark. Authors themselves acknowledge the evidence is “limited by the small number of RCTs and modest sample sizes,” reflecting how few such trials exist and how few participants each includes. Cold showers appear cost-effective solely because they’re free and align with the intuitive belief that enduring hardship yields health benefits.

- Evidence grade: B

### 16. Don’t buy blue-light-blocking glasses to “protect your vision,” and don’t believe claims that staring at screens for months will ruin your eyes — but severe eye pain, redness, and swelling require emergency care

- Cost: These glasses cost nothing; you also avoid paying the extra few hundred to a few thousand yuan for blue-light-blocking lenses.

There is no evidence they protect the retina. While screen use can cause temporary eye strain and dryness, the true threats to eye health are welding arcs, UV radiation, lasers, and acute angle-closure glaucoma.
- Benefit: A 2023 Cochrane systematic review analyzed 17 randomized controlled trials involving 5 to 156 participants each, with follow-up periods ranging from under a day to five weeks. All results below compare participants wearing blue-light-blocking lenses to those wearing regular lenses. - Evidence grade: A

### 17. Don’t treat blood donation as a health habit: claims that it “detoxifies, lowers cholesterol, and prevents heart attacks” lack randomized trial support; frequent donation does, however, cause iron deficiency

- Cost: There is no monetary cost. A single donation — including registration, blood draw, and post-donation observation — takes roughly one hour. Over the following weeks, the body works to replace the lost iron.

- In plain terms: Assertions that donating blood helps detoxify, lowers cholesterol, or prevents heart attacks are not reliable. People who donate regularly tend to be healthier to begin with; after accounting for this factor, the mortality benefit of donating once more per year drops from nearly 20% to just 7.5%. In studies that directly compared donors and non-donors, overall death rates were similar. On the other hand, donating too often does make people feel weak and cold — this is due to iron deficiency.

- Benefit: The Kuopio study in Finland followed 2,862 men aged 42–60 for nearly 9 years. During the 24 months prior to the start of monitoring, 153 men donated blood; only one (0.7%) suffered an acute heart attack. Among the remaining 2,529 non-donors, 316 (12.5%) experienced the same outcome (P<0.0001 for the difference between groups). After adjusting for age and other coronary risk factors, donors showed an 88% lower risk of heart attack; the relative risk was 0.12 (95% CI 0.02–0.86, P=0.035). However, this finding came from observational data, not a controlled trial. The Nordic SCANDAT database includes 1,182,495 blood donors followed for a total of 9,526,627 person-years; 15,401 deaths occurred. When only age and gender were considered, donating once more per year correlated with an 18.6% reduction in mortality (95% CI 16.8%–20.4%). Once the fact that only healthy individuals are allowed to donate is also taken into account, the benefit shrinks to 7.5% (95% CI 5.7%–9.4%). The authors conclude that this cannot be interpreted as conclusive proof of health benefits. The FeAST randomized trial enrolled 1,277 patients with symptomatic peripheral artery disease; one group underwent regular blood removal every 6 months while the other served as a control. Overall mortality rates were 20% in the treatment group versus 23% in controls (HR 0.85, 95% CI 0.67–1.08, P=0.17). When deaths and non-fatal heart attacks or strokes were combined, rates were 28% versus 32% (HR 0.88, 95% CI 0.72–1.07, P=0.20). No significant differences emerged. The INTERVAL trial involved 45,263 blood donors assigned to three different donation intervals: 12, 10, or 8 weeks for men and 16, 14, or 12 weeks for women. Shorter intervals resulted in significantly more blood collected over two years (the 8‑week group donated 1.69 extra units compared to the 12‑week group, 95% CI 1.59–1.80). No differences appeared in quality of life, physical activity, or cognitive function across groups; however, shorter intervals increased side effects such as fatigue, shortness of breath, dizziness, and restless legs. Average hemoglobin and ferritin levels also fell, and more donations were postponed due to low hemoglobin levels (all P<0.0001). Iron deficiency impairs thermoregulation: 10 women with iron‑deficiency anemia, 8 with depleted iron stores but no anemia, and 12 controls were immersed in 28 °C water for 100 minutes. The anemia group’s rectal temperature was 36.0 ± 0.2 °C versus 36.2 ± 0.1 °C in controls (P=0.001); oxygen consumption was 5.28 ± 0.26 versus 5.99 ± 0.29 mL·min⁻¹·kg⁻¹ (P=0.04). Levels of thyroid hormones thyroxine and triiodothyronine were also lower (P<0.002). These findings are largely explained by the fact that donors are already a carefully screened, healthier population; the hypothesis that iron removal itself protects the heart has not been confirmed in randomized trials.

### 19. Don’t start taking uric‑acid‑lowering drugs just because a routine check shows high uric acid levels but you’ve never had any symptoms
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=小 口径=死亡率 -->

- Cost: There’s no cost at all; you also avoid long‑term medication expenses, repeated follow‑up tests, and the unnecessary risk of taking drugs you don’t need.

- In plain terms: For people whose uric acid levels are high but who’ve never experienced gout attacks or developed tophi, the American College of Rheumatology recommends not taking uric‑acid‑lowering drugs at all. In a study of 24 participants, only one gout attack was prevented after three years of treatment. These drugs also fail to protect kidney function — patients taking them still saw a similar decline in kidney performance as those taking a placebo. Treatment should be started only after a genuine gout episode occurs.

- Benefit: The 2020 gout guidelines from the American College of Rheumatology define asymptomatic hyperuricemia as blood uric acid levels above 6.8 mg/dL in individuals with no prior gout attacks or tophi. Under certain conditions, the guidelines advise against initiating any uric‑acid‑lowering therapy; such drugs include allopurinol, febuxostat, and probenecid. The evidence supporting this recommendation is rated as “high.” A footnote clarifies that, based on attributable risk, 24 patients must be treated for three years to prevent a single gout attack. The CKD‑FIX randomized controlled trial involved 363 participants with stage 3 or 4 chronic kidney disease, no history of gout, and an elevated risk of kidney deterioration. One group received 100–300 mg of allopurinol daily while the other received a placebo; after 104 weeks, eGFR — a key measure of kidney function expressed in mL/min/1.73 m² — declined at an annual rate of –3.33 (95% CI –4.11 to –2.55) in the drug group versus –3.23 (–3.98 to –2.47) in the placebo group. The difference between groups was –0.10 (–1.18 to 0.97), with a P‑value of 0.85, indicating no meaningful difference in kidney‑function decline. Rates of serious adverse events were comparable at 46% versus 44%. The PERL trial enrolled 530 patients with type 1 diabetes and early‑to‑moderate diabetic kidney disease; after three years of allopurinol therapy followed by a two‑month washout period, uric acid levels fell from 6.1 to 3.9 mg/dL in the treatment group. Yet the resulting change in GFR between the two groups was merely 0.001 mL/min/1.73 m² (95% CI –1.9 to 1.9, P = 0.99). Moreover, urinary albumin excretion rose by 40% (range 0–80%) in the allopurinol group. The apparent “benefit” of these drugs stems largely from the intuitive belief that any elevated marker must be lowered by medication.

- Sources:FitzGerald JD, Dalbeth N, Mikuls T, et al. (2020). 2020 American College of Rheumatology Guideline for the Management of Gout. ### 20. Don’t have a gallbladder removed prophylactically just because an ultrasound found gallstones but you’ve never felt pain
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=死亡率 -->

- Cost: There’s no cost at all — in fact, you avoid the expense of general anesthesia and a hospital stay. The trade-off is simply remembering a few warning signs that mean you must seek medical care right away.
- In plain terms: People with gallstones but no pain have stayed symptom-free for nearly 80% of them over the past nine years. Without any intervention, only about 0.1% to 0.3% develop acute cholecystitis or pancreatitis each year. Gallbladder removal isn’t risk-free either — roughly 1.5% of patients suffer bile duct injury. That’s why European and UK medical guidelines recommend against surgery for asymptomatic individuals.
- Benefit: In Italy, the MICOL study followed 11,229 people aged 29 to 69 using ultrasound screening. Of the 856 participants with gallstones, 580 had no symptoms and were tracked for an average of 8.7 years. Out of those 580, 453 (78.1%) remained symptom-free, while 61 (10.5%) experienced mild symptoms and 66 (11.4%) had severe symptoms. Only 189 gallbladder removals were performed during the study, and 41.3% of those were on symptomless patients. The authors concluded that watchful waiting remains the best approach for most patients. A similar U.S. study of 691 patients with gallstones showed that only 10% of the 135 symptomless participants developed symptoms over 58 months, and just 7% needed surgery. Of all 50 deaths in the group, only two were linked to bile duct problems, and both occurred in symptomatic patients. The UK’s NICE CG188 guideline explicitly states that people with incidental asymptomatic gallstones should not be treated unless symptoms appear. European hepatology guidelines estimate that 0.7% to 2.5% of asymptomatic individuals develop symptoms yearly, while acute cholecystitis, pancreatitis, obstructive jaundice, or cholangitis affect only 0.1% to 0.3% annually. After the first episode of pain, that risk rises to 1% to 3% per year, meaning most people experience pain before facing serious complications. Nationwide Swedish data on 51,041 gallbladder removals found that 1.5% resulted in bile duct injury, with 0.3% involving partial or total duct rupture. Mortality within a year was 3.9% for those with duct damage versus 1.1% for others. Even after surgery, 10% to 40% of patients continue to feel discomfort or see symptoms return. Overall, guidelines agree that surgery neither extends life nor reduces long-term costs for asymptomatic patients, since its risks outweigh those of watchful waiting. One 1983 calculation estimated that a 30-year-old man who elects prophylactic removal lives 4 days less on average, while a 50-year-old loses 18 days. To date, no randomized trials have compared prophylactic removal to watchful waiting, though intuition suggests early surgery is preferable given the inevitable future pain.

### 21. Don’t cut calcium out to prevent kidney stones
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=大 口径=死亡率 -->

- Cost: No cost at all. In fact, it’s easier — no need to constantly watch out for calcium-containing foods.
- In plain terms: People who’ve had calcium oxalate stones are often advised to limit calcium intake, yet research shows the opposite is true. For men with recurrent stones, eating a normal amount of calcium while cutting back on animal protein and salt cut recurrence rates over five years to half that of those on a low‑calcium diet. UK guidelines likewise advise against restricting calcium.
- Benefit: A five‑year randomized trial enrolled 120 men with recurrent calcium oxalate stones and high urinary calcium levels. One group consumed 30 mmol of calcium daily, plus reduced animal protein (52 g/day) and salt (50 mmol sodium chloride/day). The other group followed a low‑calcium diet (10 mmol/day). Over five years, recurrence occurred in 12/60 men in the normal‑calcium group versus 23/60 in the low‑calcium group. The unadjusted relative risk was 0.49 (95% CI 0.24–0.98; P = 0.04), meaning the normal‑calcium diet cut recurrence risk by half. Both groups saw a similar drop in urinary calcium (~170 mg/day), but oxalate excretion fell by 7.2 mg/day in the normal‑calcium group while rising 5.4 mg/day in the low‑calcium group. NICE guideline NG118 states adults should aim for 700–1,200 mg of calcium daily; children and teens need 350–1,000 mg/day. The “benefit” of not cutting calcium stems from this intuitive link between calcium intake and stone formation.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources:Borghi L, Schianchi T, Meschi T, et al. (2002). Comparison of two diets for the prevention of recurrent stones in idiopathic hypercalciuria. New England Journal of Medicine, 346(2), 77-84. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa010369>；National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (2019). Renal and ureteric stones: assessment and management. NICE guideline NG118，第 1.8.1 条. <https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng118/chapter/Recommendations>
- Notes: Caveats: This trial involved only men with recurrent calcium oxalate stones and high urinary calcium; results may not apply to all stone types. Moreover, the intervention combined normal calcium intake with reduced protein and salt, so some benefits could stem from those changes. The advice is to avoid deliberately cutting calcium from the diet — not to actively increase supplementation. Separate from dietary calcium, calcium supplements should be discussed with a physician. For hydration advice, see Section 16, Item 8: aim for 2.5–3 L of water daily after a stone episode.

### 22. Don’t buy crystals, bracelets, or Pixiu figurines for “good luck,” “wealth attraction,” or “health benefits”
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=金钱 -->

- Cost: There’s no direct cost involved. The real challenge is resisting the social pressure to buy them when everyone else is wearing or talking about them.

- In plain terms: There’s no verifiable evidence that wearing such items brings good luck, attracts wealth, or improves health. Their perceived effectiveness works much like fortune‑telling: vague statements are easy to interpret personally. When sellers claim medical benefits, it constitutes false advertising — in 2026, several livestream vendors were penalized for making such claims. As simple decorative items, they’re perfectly fine.

- Benefit: Classic classroom experiments show that people receive the same generic “personality analysis” yet each believes it fits them perfectly; this is known as the Barnum effect. It explains why many feel their lives improve after wearing these items. The researchers concluded that personal acceptance alone doesn’t prove the description is accurate. Official examples: in June 2026, China’s State Administration for Market Regulation released several cases of “crackdowns on fraud” in the craft‑goods sector. One jewelry shop in Sihui, Guangdong, fabricated material details on livestreams and falsely advertised benefits such as “treating rheumatism, boosting immunity, improving sleep, and clearing blood vessels.” Such deceptive practices violate Article 9, Paragraph 1 of China’s Anti‑Unfair Competition Law.

- Evidence grade: B
- Sources:Forer BR (1949). The fallacy of personal validation: A classroom demonstration of gullibility. - Notes: The bottom line is simple: there’s no way to verify any special effects or predictive power of these items. This assessment makes no judgment about personal beliefs or cultural traditions, nor does it address their use as gifts, mementos, or heirlooms. Two points must be distinguished: sellers’ advertised benefits are subject to legal regulation and can be reported; statements like “bringing good luck” lack any research support and can’t be disproven, so you must decide for yourself whether the expense is justified. Before buying, treat the item purely as an ornament and ask yourself, “If it changed nothing, would I still pay this price?” For guidance on checking materials and certifications, or why they’re poor investments, see Sections 5, Items 33 and 34 (bracelets and watches are evaluated by cost, while gemstones require CMA certification). ### 23. Don’t expect shopping to lift your mood or boost your sense of self-worth
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=是 收益=中 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: No cost at all. The real challenge is resisting the urge to buy things when you’re feeling down.
- In plain terms: People who place a higher value on money and material possessions tend to report lower levels of happiness. However, this link is quite weak, and it’s unclear whether valuing material goods causes lower happiness or vice versa. Research also shows that spending money on experiences brings more joy than buying physical items. So when you’re in a bad mood, spending on a trip or a meal with friends is far more likely to help than filling your shopping cart.
- Benefit: One meta-analysis (combining findings from many studies) examined 259 separate samples and 753 effect sizes. The results showed that individuals who prioritized material possessions reported lower happiness levels. On the most commonly used multi-dimensional scale, the correlation coefficient was r = -0.19 (adjusted ρ = -0.24). On scales that measured only “material valuing,” the link was even weaker, with r values ranging from -0.08 to -0.11. The strength of this association varied depending on the outcome measured: it was strongest with risky health and spending behaviors and negative self-evaluations (r = -0.28 to -0.44), and weakest with life satisfaction and negative emotions (r = -0.13 to -0.15). Mediation analyses suggested this negative link may arise because unmet psychological needs drive it. Another study found that across two separate surveys, respondents consistently reported greater happiness from spending on experiences than on material goods. Subsequent lab experiments also showed that recalling a positive mood after spending on an experience led to higher happiness ratings than recalling a similar positive mood after a shopping spree. Both studies used samples from outside China.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources:Dittmar H, Bond R, Hurst M, Kasser T (2014). The relationship between materialism and personal well-being: A meta-analysis. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 107(5), 879-924. <https://doi.org/10.1037/a0037409>；Van Boven L, Gilovich T (2003). To do or to have? That is the question. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 85(6), 1193-1202. <https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.85.6.1193>
- Notes: Caveats: Most of the included studies asked participants at a single point in time whether they valued material goods and how happy they felt, so they cannot determine which factor comes first. The authors themselves note that further experiments and long-term follow-ups are needed to clarify this directionality. Therefore, this finding should not be interpreted as “shopping makes people unhappy”; rather, it means “relying on shopping to improve your mood is unlikely to work.” Moreover, the association is quite weak: an r value of -0.19 explains only a few percentage points of variation, and individual differences are substantial. Both studies used foreign samples, so there are no comparable data available for Chinese populations. This does not mean you shouldn’t spend on things you enjoy; rather, it warns against using shopping as a way to regulate your emotions, as doing so makes impulse buying your first reaction when you’re feeling down. Specific money-saving tips are covered in Section 5. Evidence-based ways to improve your mood when you’re feeling low are outlined in the latter part of Section 22 and in Section 29. ### 24. Don’t spend extra money to move up a social tier by buying a pricier home, car, or social circle
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=些 收益=中 口径=金钱 -->
- Cost: No cost at all. All you need to do is ask one extra question before increasing your budget: is this extra expense buying a real product or service, or merely securing a higher standing among those around you? The difficulty lies in resisting the urge to upgrade when everyone else does.
- In plain terms: When comparing people with similar incomes, those whose neighbors earn more report lower levels of happiness. This effect appears only among neighbors with comparable education levels and frequent social interaction. Therefore, spending extra money to move into a neighborhood where everyone is wealthier actually pushes you down a social tier. Yet when incomes rise across the board, people still feel better off, so absolute standards remain relevant.
- Benefit: The National Survey of Families and Households (NSFH) in the U.S. surveyed the same individuals twice. The authors matched this data with local average incomes calculated from a 5% sample of the 1990 census. After controlling for personal characteristics and income, they found that higher neighbor incomes correlated strongly with lower reported happiness. This effect held true under various analytical methods and remained highly significant. In fact, the drop in happiness caused by a rise in neighbor incomes matched the drop caused by a similar fall in one’s own income. Even after accounting for individual differences or regional factors, the pattern persisted. It was strongest among people who interacted closely with neighbors and absent among those with mostly external friendships. Renters and homeowners showed no difference, contradicting the idea that high housing costs reduce disposable income. Happiness declined only when comparing neighbors with similar education levels, not when they differed. The effect operated mainly through satisfaction with one’s material circumstances rather than other life domains. Finally, when both personal and neighbor incomes rose proportionally, happiness still increased.

### 25. Don’t believe that “willpower is like a muscle that gets exhausted after use”
<!-- 成本标签: 钱=0 时间=少 毅力=否 收益=中 口径=时间 -->
- Cost: No cost at all. All that needs changing is how we talk about it.
- In plain terms: The popular idea is that after doing something that requires self‑control, it becomes easier to give in to temptations later on, so we should “conserve” our willpower. Yet 23 laboratories and 2,141 participants repeated the same experiment under identical conditions, and found virtually no effect. Stop using the excuse “I’ve run out of willpower today,” and don’t spend money on courses that promise to help you “build up” willpower.
- Benefit: In a multi‑lab preregistered replication study, 23 labs with 2,141 participants followed Sripada et al.’s standardized protocol: they first performed a task demanding self‑control and then a second similar task. The pooled results showed a very small self‑exhaustion effect; its 95% confidence interval included zero (d = 0.04, 95% CI −0.07 to 0.15). Another replication, also preregistered, involving 36 labs and 3,531 participants yielded d = 0.06 — again a negligible effect. These findings call into question the model that self‑control is a finite resource. It’s important to note that this does not mean people never get tired; lack of sleep or prolonged effort can indeed reduce performance, which is addressed elsewhere in this chapter.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources:Hagger MS, Chatzisarantis NLD, Alberts H, et al. (2016). A Multilab Preregistered Replication of the Ego-Depletion Effect. Psychological Science. <https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797621989733>
- Notes: What’s being refuted is the notion that self‑control is a consumable resource, not the fact that people can feel fatigued. Adjusting one’s environment and planning actions in advance — e.g., moving undesirable items out of reach or writing down exact times, places, and steps for tasks — costs little or nothing and saves time and mental effort. This approach is far more effective than trying to “build up” willpower; it won’t improve your judgment in the afternoon, but it does eliminate unnecessary hesitation and wasted minutes.

### 26. Don’t expect skipping breakfast or following a 16:8 eating schedule to help you control your weight; instead, pick a meal timing you can stick to long-term

- Cost: It costs nothing. What you save is the mental energy you’d otherwise spend worrying about when to eat.
- In plain terms: Popular belief holds that skipping breakfast leads to overeating at lunch and dinner, resulting in a higher total daily calorie intake. Research does not support this: people who skip breakfast consume over 200 fewer calories per day on average, and tend to weigh slightly less. Compared to simply reducing overall calorie intake, a 16:8 eating schedule yields no meaningful difference in weight loss over a year. That said, people who consistently skip breakfast do have a roughly 17% higher risk of cardiovascular disease, though no significant difference in overall mortality is observed.
- Benefit: Let’s first address the claim that skipping breakfast causes people to eat more later. A meta-analysis of 13 randomized trials found that participants who ate breakfast consumed 259.79 kcal more per day on average than those who skipped it (95% CI: 78.87–440.71). Their weight was also 0.44 kg higher on average (95% CI: 0.07–0.82). However, people who skipped breakfast did not make up for the missed calories at later meals. These trials had relatively short follow-up periods: weight was tracked for an average of 7 weeks, and calorie intake for an average of 2 weeks. Next, consider 16:8 eating schedules, which restrict food intake to a specific daily window. One trial involving 116 overweight adults over 12 weeks found no significant weight difference between the group eating only between 12 PM and 8 PM and the group eating three regular meals per day: the former group weighed 0.26 kg less on average, but this difference fell well within the margin of error (−1.30 to 0.78 kg). Another trial conducted in Guangzhou with 139 obese participants over 12 months showed that combining time-restricted eating with calorie restriction led to 1.8 kg more weight loss than calorie restriction alone, though this difference was also not statistically significant (−4.0 to 0.4 kg). Finally, we look at the long-term effects of skipping breakfast. A pooled analysis of data from 2.38 million people found that those who skipped breakfast had a 17% higher risk of cardiovascular disease (OR 1.17, 95% CI: 1.09–1.26) and a 49% higher risk of cardiovascular-related death (OR 1.49, 95% CI: 1.20–1.84). However, these studies only recorded meal habits without grouping participants by eating pattern. The original long-term study with 17–23 years of follow-up reported a 19% higher risk of all-cause mortality among breakfast skippers, but this figure fell within the margin of error (95% CI: 0.99–1.42), so no meaningful difference was found.
